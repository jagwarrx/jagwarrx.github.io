The Science of Enlightenment

Shinzen Young. Notes from September 2020. 1,406 words.

Science of Enlightenment

By Shinzen Young

Book Summary

This book offers a secular explanation of enlightenment as a phenomena. It also points out the common threads in the various religious paths to enlightenment.

Shinzen Young is meditation teacher. Originally from an academic background, he went to Japan 50 years ago and became a Buddhist monk, undergoing strict Zen training, including a 100 days of solo meditation in a cave. 

As he became a proficient practitioner, he studied comparative religion, and found lots of overlap in their mystical traditions. His goal is to design a system, aided by science, to fast track the results from meditation. He calls this system Unified Mindfulness, and explains it in this book.

Rating: 9/10. Read the notes; if compelled, read the book!

Notes

Meditation in Daily Life

Being in a meditative state means being very alert, and at the same time, deeply relaxed.

Meditation trains your (1) concentration ability, (2) sensory clarity and (3) equanimity.

Sensory Clarity involves two aspects:

  • Detecting the subtle details of our sensory states (like an X-Ray)
  • Deconstruction of complex sensory event and breaking it down into its natural components (like atoms)

Meditation works your concentration muscle. Added focus is available to you, enhancing all your activities. Since attention lies at the core of doing any activity, every aspect of life is affected.

A lot of energy is spent worrying. Meditation helps us examine those thought loops, and puts us in a better mental place.

What is Enlightenment

Concentration ➞ Shift in identity

All of the world's religions have a mystical, contemplative side — Vajrayana in Buddhism, Sufism in Islam, Christian mysticism. This gives a common ground for all religions to talk with each other.

In the Hindu tradition, Yoga means 'yoking' or 'anchoring' with a mythic archetype. One of the eight limbs of Yoga is Dharana, which refers to holding your concentration on an object of awareness. Part of your attention flows to the object and part of it flows back into subjective thoughts and feelings. When you attention is highly concentrated, it flows completely to the objects, and none of it flows back into mental images, thoughts or feelings. This state is called Samadhi. You briefly adopt the identity of the thing you are concentrating on.

The next stage after this is "Samadhi without a seed". Awareness flows so completely that there's no time to fixate the object in time or space. The object of concentration ceases to be an object — a particle becomes a wave. You become that waveform. The wave links to other waves in the universe, and cancels out into deeply fulfilling nothingness.

Consciously tasting Samadhi without a seed in daily life is the initial state of enlightenment. Enlightenment is a spectrum, and this is the first milestone. Some artists and athletes, after long years of practice, experience this flow state when performing.


Clarifying ➞ Purification ➞ Insight

The other half of the process is clarifying — observing, analyzing and deconstructing the sensory experience. Clarifying leads to insight — this is the great discovery of the Gautama Buddha, that you can develop an internal microscope to observe the nature of self and the human condition. This clarification aspect of meditation is known as vipassana.

I attended a 10 day Vipassana camp, with 10-11 hours of daily meditation. For the first four days, we did nothing but concentrate on the breath. This was to increase concentration to levels required to analyze the sensory experience.

The clarification aspect of meditation involves analyzing the sensory experience into components and tracking how those components react.

Shinzen uses precise terminology for these components — mental imagery, mental talk, and emotional body sensations. He refers to mental images as “See In,” mental talk as “Hear In,” and emotional-type body sensations as “Feel In.” Any perception of self involves one or a combination of those components.

These components can interact in a number of ways: three possible single occurrences (mental image, mental talk, body emotion), three possible paired occurrences (any two at the same time), and two possible extreme situations (all three inner components are active at the same time or all three inner components are inactive at the same time).

Without sensory clarity, the different components of the experience don’t just add together, they crisscross and mutually multiply. For example, if we have ten units of discomfort in the body and ten units of negativity in the mind, what we actually have is twenty (10 + 10 = 20) units of undesirable experience. That is what you will experience if you have sensory clarity—just what it is. However, if you don’t keep track of what is going on, each of the ten units of discomfort in the body will interact with each of the ten units of negativity in the mind, cross-multiplying to produce the illusion of one hundred (10 × 10 = 100) units of undesirable experience.

This microscopic coagulation in the natural flow of the senses creates a kind of internal friction. It causes our visual field to congeal giving us the impression that space is rigid. It causes our internal conversations to congeal, giving us the impression that thoughts are real. It causes our body sensation to congeal, giving us the impression that the body is material.

When you have the perception of seeing the external world, hearing the external world, and being touched by the external world, the sense of an “I” that sees externally, hears externally, and feels externally arises because the inner system reacts to those outer stimuli.

In order for suffering of any type to occur there must be grasping. There is a way to eliminate grasping; therefore, there is a way to eliminate suffering. When suffering is eliminated, a state of non-suffering arises, which the Buddha referred to as nirvana.

Impermanence

Clarity allows you to track your I-am-ness in terms of the same variables that characterize quantified science: how much, of what, when and where, interacting in what ways, and changing at what rate.

Most people are aware of the moment when a sensory event starts, but are seldom aware of the moment when it vanishes. There is only a finite amount of real estate available in consciousness at any given instant. Each arising somewhere causes a passing somewhere else. When any sensory experience—whether it’s pleasant, painful, or neutral—vanishes, it can potentially leave a sense of satisfaction in its wake.

Each time you note Gone, for a brief instant your attention is pointed directly toward the richness of the Source. That is what’s behind the seeming paradox of “satisfying nothingness.” If your ability to continuously focus on Goneness is high, those moments of microrelief might sum to significant relief, macrorelief.

Where sensory events go to is where sensory events arise from. The gaps between the Gones get shorter and shorter until a figure-ground reversal takes place. Self and world become fleeting figures, and Gone becomes the abiding ground. Experiencing that will have a huge impact on how you relate to aging and death. For a period of time you un-become the product of impermanence, and you re-identify with impermanence itself. Impermanence viewed this way could also be called spirit or even soul.


The Realm of Power

When journeying from surface to Source, some people encounter celestial and empowering experiences. It may seem to them as though they are meeting spirit beings or angels, visiting the worlds of the gods, or achieving psychic and healing powers. It may seem to them that they can remember former lives, or that they can travel outside of the body.

These represent samskaras or blockages percolating up from the subconscious and becoming tangible. If you happen to have such experiences, the trick is just to observe them with concentration, clarity, and equanimity so that the purification process continues.

In the world of surface, conventional reality, people are preoccupied with status. The realm of power can feed that preoccupation, just as “mating and rating” do on the surface. That’s why getting caught in the realm of power is referred to as spiritual materialism.

Instead of getting tripped out on the sensation itself, the intrinsic content of the experience, become fascinated with the changing contour of the experience. You track the undulation or vibratory movement moment by moment. You see the experience as a verb, not a noun, a doing, not a thing.

    What judges reward

    Three things decide most results. Claude uses these same tips and questions when it judges your idea.

    1. Demo impact Weighs the most

    Does it work live, and does the judge understand it in the first 30 seconds? A polished, narrow demo beats an ambitious broken one almost every time. This usually weighs the most in practice, even when the rubric doesn't say so.

    Tips

    • Open on the result, not the login screen. The first thing the judge sees is the product doing its job.
    • Write the first 30 seconds word for word: who it's for, their pain in one sentence, then the live moment.
    • Demo one path that works every time. Rehearse it five times, on the venue wifi, on the demo laptop.
    • Seed realistic data. An empty dashboard or lorem ipsum kills the moment.
    • Make the change visible: a before and after, a number that moves, a document that appears.
    • Have a fallback for every flaky call (cached response, recorded video). Judges forgive a backup; they don't forgive a spinner.
    • Freeze features about three quarters of the way through. The rest is polish and rehearsal.

    Think about

    • What does the judge see in the first 10 seconds?
    • Which single moment makes a judge lean forward?
    • If the wifi or the API dies mid-demo, what still works?
    • What can you cut and still have the demo land?

    2. Problem significance Why anyone should care

    Is this a real, specific pain for a clearly named user, and does it fit the hackathon's theme or a sponsor track? Judges reward "I'd use this" or "someone would pay for this" over technical cleverness aimed at nothing.

    Tips

    • Name the user as a role in a situation ("an ICU nurse at shift handover"), never "people" or "businesses".
    • Put a number on the pain: minutes lost, money, errors, how often it happens.
    • Talk to one real user before or during the event, and quote them in the pitch.
    • Tie the idea to the theme and one sponsor track in a sentence. If you can't, pick a different track or idea.
    • Score yourself against the rubric before building. A team that plays the rubric beats a better project that ignores it.
    • Impact = how much it changes one person's life × how many people. Fun apps rarely beat real problems for the top spots.

    Think about

    • Who would use this tomorrow, and what do they use today instead?
    • Who would pay for it, or use it every week?
    • What does the problem cost if nobody solves it?
    • Which prize is this built to win?

    3. Technical novelty or depth What sets you apart

    Did you build something non-trivial in the time, or use the sponsor's tech in a way others didn't? This is what separates you from the ten other teams that picked the same idea.

    Tips

    • Find the one hard part and build it for real. Fake the rest: auth, settings, payments, onboarding.
    • Use the sponsor's tech for its unusual feature, not the hello-world call every team makes.
    • Assume ten teams have the obvious version of your idea. Write down what yours does that theirs won't.
    • Show the work: one architecture slide, and one sentence on the hard thing you solved.
    • Choose depth or breadth on purpose. One API used deeply, or many APIs stitched together, both win; drifting between them doesn't.
    • Don't train a model in 24 hours unless that is the project. Use a strong model and spend the time on the data and the loop around it.

    Think about

    • What's the hardest thing this does, and did you build it or just call it?
    • What would make the engineer on the panel ask "how did you do that"?
    • What's the obvious version another team will build, and how is yours different?
    • Which sponsor feature will most teams overlook?

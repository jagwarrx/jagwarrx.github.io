About this chart

An original chart that keeps the side-by-side idea of printed world history wall charts and changes the parts that tend to mislead.

What is different

  • Uncertainty is drawn. A bar fades out where a date is traditional, disputed, or stands in for a gradual change. Tap a bar to see which.
  • States and cultures look different. Dashed bars are archaeological cultures, migrations or periods, which had no capital, ruler or border.
  • No Europe-based period names. The axis is plain dates. Labels like Classical Antiquity or Middle Ages fit Europe and little else.
  • Regions get equal standing. Sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas, the steppe, Southeast Asia and Oceania each have a full column.
  • Events are marked. Small icons mark about 140 dated events, each in the region where it happened. The icon shows the kind: crossed swords for wars and battles, a ship for voyages, a book for writing and print, a crown for rulers, and so on. Detail in the Show panel sets how many show: Major, Notable or All. Zooming in raises it a step at a time until you pick one yourself. Names appear beside the icons when a row has room, so show one or two regions to read them.
  • Everything links out. Each bar, event and shared event has a Wikipedia link and a picture from Wikipedia, loaded when you open it. Links go through Wikipedia's search, so a few may open a results page or show no picture.
  • One region can be regrouped. Show a single region (Only, in the Show panel) and pick Group by. It re-sorts the same bars into columns by language, religion, way of life, trade network, ecology, polity type or claimed lineage.
  • Shared events. Hatched bands mark plagues, conquests, trade routes and climate shocks that crossed regions.
  • Two layouts. Across runs time left to right with one row per region. Down runs time down the page with regions side by side.
  • Regions unfold into parts. Tap a region's heading on the chart, or the small triangle next to it in the Show panel, to split it into sub-regions, and some of those split again. Each part only has a row for the years it has history; hatching marks the years before or after. Folded, everything sits in the one row.
  • Overlays. Ten layers add people, religion and belief, society, law and rights, arts and culture, technology and ideas, infrastructure and buildings, trade and business, disasters and disease, food, farming and subsistence, and migration and population. They follow the zoom like events: the most important first, more detail as you zoom in, most of it in recent centuries. A line after a person shows their lifetime. Each item was checked against a source, usually its Wikipedia article.
  • Links. Switch on Links to draw arrows for things that crossed from one region to another: faiths, crops, inventions, people, trade, conquests. An arrow shows when both of its regions are selected. A slanted arrow left in one year and arrived in a later one.
  • One year at a time. The red line marks one year. Move it with the slider in the time bar under the chart, the arrow keys, the step buttons or Play, or just scroll the chart: when the line leaves the screen it jumps back into view. The slider moves in big steps where little happened and in single years where events crowd together. Drag the strip under it to zoom the slider into a range of years.
  • This moment. With nothing selected, the right column lists what each region looked like in the red line's year, and any shared event under way. Tap a name to open it.
  • A map of borders. The map shows the territories of the selected regions in the red line's year. Opening an event or bar moves the line to its year. Drag the map to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom, or use Zoom to area. Borders come from historical-basemaps, about 50 snapshots from 3000 BCE to 2010, with the borders of today from Natural Earth; the map shows the nearest snapshot at or before the year.
  • Search and share. Search finds states, events, people and years; press / to start. The address bar keeps the year, regions, layers, detail and what is open, so Share view copies a link that opens the same view. Keys: Left and Right step the year, 1, 2 and 3 set the detail, Escape closes.
  • Two time scales. An even scale is honest about duration. Stretch recent gives the crowded last five centuries three times the room.
  • The Roman Empire does not end in 476. The eastern empire is labelled as the Roman state continuing.

Limits

  • It is a selection of about 190 states and cultures and 140 events. Many states are missing, especially small or short-lived ones.
  • The Group by labels are one-word judgement calls about societies that were mixed and that changed. Each bar gets a single label per grouping, for its most typical or longest state. Treat them as a starting point.
  • Bar width means nothing. It does not show territory, population or importance.
  • Dates follow widely used conventional chronologies. Before about 1400 BCE, Near Eastern dates can shift by decades depending on the scheme.
  • Regions with fewer early bars had people and history too. Bars follow the surviving evidence, which favours societies that built in stone and wrote.

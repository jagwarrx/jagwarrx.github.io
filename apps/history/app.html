About this chart

An original chart that keeps the side-by-side idea of printed world history wall charts and changes the parts that tend to mislead.

What is different

  • Uncertainty is drawn. A bar fades out where a date is traditional, disputed, or stands in for a gradual change. Tap a bar to see which.
  • States and cultures look different. Dashed bars are archaeological cultures, migrations or periods, which had no capital, ruler or border.
  • No Europe-based period names. The axis is plain dates. Labels like Classical Antiquity or Middle Ages fit Europe and little else.
  • Regions get equal standing. Sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas, the steppe, Southeast Asia and Oceania each have a full column. The depth is not even yet: Greece, Rome and Byzantium have a fine level of detail that no other region has, and battles are thickest in Europe.
  • Events are marked. Small icons mark over 300 dated events and battles, each in the region where it happened. The icon shows the kind: a ship for voyages, a book for writing and print, a crown for rulers, and so on. Detail, in the View menu, sets how many show: Major, Notable or All. Zooming in raises it a step at a time until you pick one yourself. Names appear beside the icons when a row has room, so show one or two regions to read them.
  • Everything links out. Each bar, event and shared event has a Wikipedia link and a picture from Wikipedia, loaded when you open it. Links go through Wikipedia's search, so a few may open a results page or show no picture.
  • One region can be regrouped. Show a single region (click its button above the chart while all eight show, or double-click it) and pick Group by in the View menu. It re-sorts the same bars into columns by language, religion, way of life, trade network, ecology, polity type or claimed lineage.
  • Shared events. Hatched bands mark plagues, conquests, trade routes and climate shocks that crossed regions.
  • Two layouts. Across runs time left to right with one row per region. Down runs time down the page with regions side by side.
  • Regions unfold into parts. Tap a region's heading on the chart to split it into sub-regions, and some of those split again. Each part only has a row for the years it has history; hatching marks the years before or after. Folded, everything sits in the one row.
  • Overlays. Twelve layers add people, wars and battles, religion and belief, society, law and rights, arts and culture, technology and ideas, infrastructure and buildings, trade and business, disasters and disease, food, farming and subsistence, migration and population, and voices: the words of people who were there. They follow the zoom like events: the most important first, more detail as you zoom in, most of it in recent centuries. Turn them on with the buttons above the chart; each shows how many are in the years on screen. A line after a person shows their lifetime. Each item was checked against a source, usually its Wikipedia article.
  • Links. Switch on Links to draw arrows for things that crossed from one region to another: faiths, crops, inventions, people, trade, conquests. An arrow shows when both of its regions are selected. A slanted arrow left in one year and arrived in a later one.
  • One year at a time. The red line marks one year. Move it with the slider in the time bar under the chart, the arrow keys, the step buttons or Play, or just scroll the chart: when the line leaves the screen it jumps back into view. The slider moves in big steps where little happened and in single years where events crowd together. Click the year to type one. The Years button limits the line to a period, and so does holding Shift and dragging along the line.
  • The world in that year. With nothing selected, the right column shows the red line's year: how long ago it was, shared events under way, who was alive and how old they were, one thing worth knowing, what was beginning or ending in each region, and what comes next. Tap any of them to open it.
  • A map of borders. The map shows the territories of the selected regions in the red line's year. Map, at the top, makes it fill the chart's space; Minimize turns it into a small window over the chart that you can drag and resize. Events and overlay items sit where Wikipedia or Wikidata places them (people at their birthplace, from age 20 to their death); the rest show when the red line is within some years of them, and the map says which years. Opening something moves the map to it: a state to its territory, outlined in red. Drag the map to pan, scroll or pinch to zoom, or use Zoom to area. Borders come from historical-basemaps, about 50 snapshots from 3000 BCE to 2010, with the borders of today from Natural Earth; the map shows the nearest snapshot at or before the year.
  • Search and share. Search finds states, events, people and years; press / to start. The address bar keeps the year, regions, layers, detail and what is open, so Share view copies a link that opens the same view. Keys: Left and Right step the year, Space plays and pauses, 1, 2 and 3 set the detail, S opens the library, Escape closes.
  • Pages. About 5,900 short pages sit behind the chart, on states, peoples, events, people, works and places. Open one from a card, a link or the search. Links in the text go to other pages; a link with no page yet says so. Hold Shift and click a link to keep that page open in a sidebar while you read on.
  • Library. The Library button lists the stories, the voices and the videos. A voice is a passage in the words of someone who was there, copied from a named source. A story plays like a short film: the chart and the map travel from step to step while the text is read out. Pause at any time, look around, and press play to carry on. The voice is your browser's and can be switched off.
  • Ask the map. Ask a question and Claude builds a story from the chart to answer it; Ask about this, on a card, answers questions about one item. Both need your own Anthropic API key, which stays in your browser, and each question costs you a few cents.
  • Two time scales. An even scale is honest about duration. Stretch recent gives the crowded last five centuries three times the room.
  • The Roman Empire does not end in 476. The eastern empire is labelled as the Roman state continuing.

What this page stores and sends

  • No cookies, no tracking, no account. Your settings, the pages you keep open and the stories you save stay in this browser.
  • Opening an item, or resting the pointer on a link, asks Wikipedia for its picture and summary. Wikipedia sees the item's title.
  • The Claude features send your question and the text of the card to Anthropic, with your own API key. The key is kept in this browser only; Forget key removes it.
  • The map of borders before 2010 comes from historical-basemaps (GPL-3.0). The fonts are Barlow (OFL), the small pictures Noto Emoji (Apache 2.0).

Limits

  • It is a selection of about 190 states and cultures, over 300 events and battles and about 2,300 items in the layers. Many states are missing, especially small or short-lived ones.
  • The pages are short summaries written with Claude from general knowledge. They have not been checked line by line against sources. Treat them as a way in, and follow the Wikipedia link before you rely on one.
  • The Group by labels are one-word judgement calls about societies that were mixed and that changed. Each bar gets a single label per grouping, for its most typical or longest state. Treat them as a starting point.
  • Bar width means nothing. It does not show territory, population or importance.
  • Dates follow widely used conventional chronologies. Before about 1400 BCE, Near Eastern dates can shift by decades depending on the scheme.
  • Regions with fewer early bars had people and history too. Bars follow the surviving evidence, which favours societies that built in stone and wrote.

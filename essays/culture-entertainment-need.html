Culture, Entertainment and the Need

November 2026. 1,550 words.

The argument of The Need Is Built In fits in one line: all humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. When they can’t, they reach for a story, a ritual or someone who claims to know.

But there’s an obvious problem with that. We pay not to know what comes next. We buy thrillers so we won’t know who did it. We watch matches because nobody knows who will win. A film whose ending you can guess is a bad film. If uncertainty hurts, why is so much of what we do for fun built out of it?

The answer explains most of entertainment, and a fair amount of culture.

Two faces of uncertainty

Uncertainty about a threat hurts. That’s the evidence from the last essays: stress tracks how uncertain a shock is, not how likely.

Uncertainty about a reward is different. In 2003 Christopher Fiorillo, Philippe Tobler and Wolfram Schultz recorded dopamine neurons in monkeys that were shown cues predicting a reward with different odds. Besides the familiar burst at the cue, the neurons showed a slower, sustained rise in activity while the monkeys waited, and that rise was largest when the odds were fifty-fifty: the point of maximum uncertainty. Not knowing whether something good is coming is itself attractive.

Entertainment is the trick of turning the first face into the second. It gives you uncertainty, but in a frame where nothing can actually hurt you, and with a promise that it will resolve. Safe uncertainty, with an ending guaranteed. That’s the deal.

How the forms use it

Stories are the oldest version. A story opens a question in the first pages and closes it in the last. In between, it lets you live through uncertainty that isn’t yours. Keith Oatley and Raymond Mar argue that fiction works as a simulator for social life, the way a flight simulator works for pilots: you rehearse fear, loss and betrayal without paying for them. You get the practice and the resolution, and you go home.

Horror and sad films push the dose higher. Paul Rozin called the enjoyment of negative feelings in a safe frame benign masochism: chili, roller coasters, tearjerkers. In a study at a haunted house, Marc Andersen and colleagues found that enjoyment peaked at moderate fear. Too little was boring. Too much stopped being fun. The frame only holds up to a point.

Sport is uncertainty inside fixed rules. Economists have a name for what fans want, the outcome uncertainty hypothesis: the closer the contest, the more people watch. A blowout is dull for the same reason a predictable film is. And it ends. There’s a final whistle.

Music plays with expectation directly. David Huron’s theory is that music works by setting up predictions and then meeting, delaying or breaking them. Valorie Salimpoor and colleagues found that dopamine is released twice at a moment of musical peak pleasure: once in anticipation, and again at the peak itself. A song is a short course in wanting to know what comes next and then finding out.

Games are the purest form. Clear rules, visible progress, instant feedback, a goal just hard enough to be uncertain. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called the state they produce flow. A game is a world in which you always know what kind of thing happens next, even when you don’t know exactly what.

Comedy

Comedy is the same deal at its smallest: a full cycle of uncertainty in a couple of seconds. The theory that has held up best is incongruity: a joke sets up an expectation, breaks it, and lets you see a second reading that makes sense of the break. Kant and Schopenhauer had versions of it, and Arthur Koestler called it bisociation, one situation suddenly seen in two frames at once. Matthew Hurley, Daniel Dennett and Reginald Adams sharpened it into a theory that fits the predictive brain exactly. In Inside Jokes they argue that humor is the brain’s reward for catching an error in its own model: you committed to a wrong prediction, the punch line exposes it, and the mirth is the payment for the correction. Peter McGraw’s benign violation theory adds the frame: something has to seem wrong and safe at the same time, which is why a joke that’s too cruel, or too close, stops being funny. And laughter itself seems to have started as a play signal, the open-mouthed face apes make to say “this is play, not a fight.” Comedy is a prediction error you’re allowed to enjoy.

Culture is the shared answer

Entertainment hands out uncertainty and then resolves it. Culture does something larger. It answers uncertainty for everyone at once, in advance.

Norms are the plainest case. You know how a wedding goes, what to say at a funeral, how to greet an elder, when to stand. That isn’t trivia. It’s a vast amount of uncertainty about what other people will do, settled before anyone walks in.

Shared rites go further. Festivals, concerts and big matches produce what Durkheim called collective effervescence, the charge a crowd feels when it moves and sings together. Myths answer the largest uncertainties, death, suffering and where we came from, with one story everyone can hold.

And culture answers the uncertainty about who you are. Michael Hogg’s uncertainty-identity theory finds that the less sure people feel about themselves, the more strongly they identify with a group, and the more they prefer groups with sharp edges and strong leaders. Fandoms, teams and tribes meet a real need. At the extreme, so do radical movements, which is why they recruit best among the uncertain.

Breaking the deal

All of the above has one thing in common. It ends. The story closes. The whistle blows. The festival is over and you go home.

Some forms keep the uncertainty and drop the ending. Gambling is the clearest. It runs on random rewards, the schedule Skinner found makes behavior hardest to stop, and on near misses, which feel like progress and aren’t. Twenty-four-hour news sells uncertainty as information, and it has every reason to keep the alarm ringing.

The feed is the newest and the most effective. It’s endless novelty with a random reward on every pull, and it’s designed never to close. There is no last page. The old deal was uncertainty with an ending guaranteed. The new deal is uncertainty with the ending removed, because a need that’s never met keeps you coming back.

That gives a simple test for any entertainment. Does it close the loop, or keep it open? And did I leave calmer or hungrier?

Improv

There’s one form that does something different from all the others, and it’s the one I know best from the inside: improv.

Every other form manages uncertainty by controlling it. Somebody wrote the story, set the rules of the game, composed the song. In improv nobody did. There’s no script, no plan and no ending decided in advance. You walk on stage knowing nothing about what comes next. It is, on paper, exactly what the need hates.

What makes it bearable, and then fun, is that improv replaces knowing what will happen with knowing how you’ll respond. The first rule is “yes, and”: accept whatever your partner offers, and add to it. Whatever comes, you already know your next move: you take it and build. Keith Johnstone, whose Impro shaped the form, argued that what blocks spontaneity is fear of being judged, and that the cure is to stop trying to be clever and say the obvious thing. There are no mistakes, only offers. An error isn’t something to hide. It’s material.

The brain seems to agree. When Charles Limb and Allen Braun scanned jazz musicians improvising, they saw activity drop in a region associated with self-monitoring and rise in one associated with self-expression. Improvising means turning down the part of you that checks. And there’s early evidence that it carries over. Peter Felsman and colleagues found that improv training increased people’s tolerance of uncertainty, and in a later study it reduced social anxiety in teenagers.

So improv is a different answer to the need. Every other form says: here is what comes next, or here is a safe dose of not knowing, and here is the ending. Improv says: you can’t know what comes next, but you can know how you’ll meet it. That’s the one answer that also works off stage.

It also suits an ADHD brain unusually well, which may be why so many of us end up there. It pays for being in the moment and punishes planning ahead. It runs on novelty. And the thing an ADHD brain is usually short of, a script to follow, is the thing improv doesn’t have.

What follows

Entertainment isn’t a distraction from the need. It’s one of the main ways we meet it. The good forms give the need what it actually wants: a question, a safe stretch of not knowing, and an answer. The bad ones give it the first two and keep the third, on purpose.

And the best of them train us. A story rehearses uncertainty. A joke makes a game of being wrong. Improv practices the only lasting answer there is, which is not knowing what comes next and meeting it anyway.

Levels of Leverage: Where to Intervene

November 2026. 595 words.

Donella Meadows ranked the places to intervene in a system, from weakest to strongest. My stack of effectiveness says what sits on what. Her list says where a push changes the most. Put together, they answer a practical question: when something isn’t working, where do I push?

Her twelve points

From least leverage to most:

  • 12. Parameters: the numbers.
  • 11. Buffers: the size of the reserves.
  • 10. Stocks and flows: the physical setup.
  • 9. Delays: how long feedback takes.
  • 8. Balancing loops: what notices drift and corrects it.
  • 7. Reinforcing loops: what grows on itself.
  • 6. Information flows: who sees what, and when.
  • 5. Rules: the incentives, limits and defaults.
  • 4. Self-organization: the power to change the system’s own structure.
  • 3. Goals: what the system is for.
  • 2. Paradigm: the beliefs the whole system grows out of.
  • 1. Transcending paradigms: holding any model lightly.

Her point: almost all attention goes to the numbers, and the numbers rarely change much.

  • Direction holds the most leverage. Change an end and everything below it moves.
  • Time holds the least. Tuning hours and block counts is parameter work. A better plan rarely fixes a wrong goal.
  • Energy is buffers and stocks. Each push moves it a little, and it’s slow to rebuild. Sleep, money and slack are what absorb the shocks.
  • The loops aren’t layers. Information and feedback are the arrows between layers: logging, reviews, seeing it at all. That’s where a lot of cheap fixes live.
  • Rules are cheap leverage. A WIP limit, no by default, when this, do that. They sit between strategy and plans.
  • Habits are loops. Every rep makes the next one easier, good or bad. Nip a bad loop early, before it gains strength.

Diagnose bottom-up, intervene top-down

The stack says debug from the bottom. Meadows says leverage is at the top. Both hold.

  • The bottom is the likeliest cause. Check sleep before strategy. It’s cheap, and it shows up fast.
  • The top is the biggest change. But it’s slow, and it’s resisted.

So: look from the bottom up to find what’s broken. Then fix it at the highest level that’s actually broken, not at the numbers.

One more warning from Meadows: people often find the right lever and push it the wrong way. More hours when the problem is the goal. More rules when the problem is that nobody sees the information.

Weekly review: find it

Bottom-up, fast. Stop at the first no.

  • Buffers: did the base habits hold? Is there any slack left in the week?
  • Loops: what grew this week, good or bad? Any new habit to nip?
  • Feedback: what slipped without being noticed? How late did I notice?
  • Information: what didn’t I see? Is everything in one place?
  • Rules: which rule failed, or is missing? Add or remove one.
  • Numbers, last: only now adjust the hours, blocks and targets.

Quarterly review: change it

Top-down, slow. One change at the highest level that needs it.

  • Goals: are the ends still the ends? Did the people I love feel it this quarter?
  • Paradigm: what am I assuming? (“More hours means more progress.”)
  • Self-organization: what in the system should change its own shape?
  • Stocks: one structural change to health, money or environment.
  • Transcending: where is this model itself wrong?

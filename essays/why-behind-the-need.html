The Why Behind the Need

November 2026. 1,390 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. In The Need Is Built In I argued that this need is built in, and that priests, astrologers and apps don’t create it. They sell into it.

That essay was mostly about what the need does. This one is about why it’s there. And the answer turns out to have layers. The need shows up in the body, in the brain, in the mind and in the shape of a whole life, and four books, each from a different field, explain one layer each.

In one line: the body pays for not knowing, the brain is built to remove it, the mind will take any answer, and culture answers the biggest one.

The body pays for not knowing

Start at the bottom. Lisa Feldman Barrett, in How Emotions Are Made, argues that the brain’s most important job isn’t thinking. It’s running the body. Specifically, it’s managing what she calls the body budget: the water, salt, glucose and energy the body needs, predicted ahead of time so they’re there before they’re needed. Physiologists call this allostasis, regulation by prediction rather than by reaction.

Prediction is what makes the budget cheap. If the brain knows what’s coming, it spends exactly what’s needed. If it doesn’t, it has to prepare for several futures at once. Heart rate goes up, stress hormones rise, the body is kept ready. That readiness costs energy, and keeping it up for long costs health. Achim Peters, Bruce McEwen and Karl Friston made this argument directly in 2017: uncertainty is stressful because it forces the brain to spend more to stay ready, and chronic uncertainty is chronic overspending.

Barrett adds the piece that connects this to feeling. The brain is always sensing the state of the budget, and we feel that sense as mood: pleasant or unpleasant, calm or agitated. An overdrawn budget feels bad. So the first reason uncertainty hurts is that it’s expensive, and the expense is felt.

The brain is built to close the gap

One layer up, Andy Clark’s The Experience Machine is the clearest account of the theory behind all of this, predictive processing. Its central claim is that we don’t perceive the world directly. The brain constantly predicts what its senses are about to report, and what we experience is that prediction, corrected by the senses where they disagree. The disagreement is called prediction error, and the brain’s whole operation can be described as keeping it low.

Clark stresses that there are two ways to do that. You can change the prediction to fit the world, which is learning. Or you can change the world to fit the prediction, which is action. Reaching for a cup is, in this picture, predicting your hand at the cup and then moving until the prediction comes true.

Uncertainty, in these terms, is error the brain can’t get rid of. It can’t settle on a model, and it can’t act its way to one. So it keeps working, and anything that closes the gap is a relief. A story closes it. So does a ritual, so does a forecast, so does a decision. The brain isn’t picky about how the gap closes. It’s built to close it.

This is also where the other answer to the need comes from, the one in Know How You’ll Meet It. If you can’t change the model, you can still act. Knowing how you’ll respond is the brain’s second route, used on purpose.

The mind will take any answer

The third layer is psychology. Arie Kruglanski spent decades studying what he calls the need for cognitive closure: the desire for a firm answer on some question, any firm answer, rather than ambiguity. The key word is any. The need for closure isn’t a need for the truth. It’s a need for the question to stop being open.

Kruglanski and Donna Webster described two tendencies that come with it. Seizing: grabbing the first answer that comes along, fast. Freezing: holding on to it once you have it, and no longer looking at evidence against it. They built a scale to measure it, and people differ. But it also rises and falls in everyone, with conditions. It goes up under time pressure, under fatigue, even in a noisy room. It goes up, in other words, exactly when the body budget from the first layer is under strain.

That’s why the sellers do well. A horoscope, a guru or a new productivity system doesn’t have to be right. It only has to arrive while the question is open and the person is tired, and be firm.

Culture answers the biggest one

The top layer is the largest uncertainty there is. Ernest Becker’s The Denial of Death, which won the Pulitzer in 1974, starts from a simple observation: we are the animal that knows it will die, without knowing when or what follows. Becker argued that this knowledge produces a terror that sits under everything, and that much of human culture is built to manage it. He called the results immortality projects: religion, nations, legacies, children, works that outlast us. Each one gives a person a place in something larger that doesn’t end.

In the 1980s three psychologists, Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon and Tom Pyszczynski, turned Becker’s argument into an experimental program called terror management theory. In the classic studies, reminding people of their own death made them cling harder to their worldview and judge outsiders more harshly. In one, judges reminded of death set much higher bail for a defendant than judges who weren’t.

This fits the other essays. Michael Hogg’s work found that the more uncertain people are about themselves, the more strongly they hold on to a group. Becker would say that every uncertainty is a small version of the big one, and every group is a small immortality project.

How the layers stack

Put together, the four layers make one argument.

Not knowing what comes next costs the body energy, and the cost is felt as unease. The brain is built to close the gap, by changing its model or by acting. Under pressure, the mind closes it with whatever answer arrives first and then stops looking. And the largest gap of all, death, never closes, so cultures build shared answers to close it for everyone at once.

Each layer makes the one above it stronger. A strained body raises the need for closure. The need for closure makes stories and groups more attractive. And stories and groups are how culture answers the biggest question.

How solid is this

Not all four layers stand on the same ground, and it’s worth saying which is which.

The idea that uncertainty is stressful is well supported. The brain-as-predictor view is widely held and very productive, but it’s a framework, not a settled fact, and critics argue it explains too much. Barrett’s theory of how emotions are made is contested by researchers who think some emotions are more built in than she allows, though her body-budget point is less controversial. Kruglanski’s need for closure has a long and consistent research record.

Terror management theory is the shakiest. Hundreds of studies supported it, but in 2022 a large attempt to replicate the basic death-reminder effect across many labs didn’t find it. Becker’s idea may still be right about life. The lab version of it is in doubt.

What follows

The main thing that follows is that the need isn’t a flaw. Every layer of it is a system working as designed. A body that budgets by prediction, a brain that closes gaps, a mind that settles fast under pressure, a culture that answers what can’t be answered: each one made sense for animals like us, for a very long time.

The second thing is that the layers tell you where to push. Lower the cost of not knowing, with sleep, slack and fewer inputs, and the need for closure drops with it. Close the gap by acting rather than by believing, and you need fewer stories. Notice when you’re tired and the question is open, because that’s when you’ll seize. And choose your immortality projects on purpose, because you’ll have some either way.

The need is built in, from the body up. Nobody created it, and nobody can remove it. You can only choose what meets it.

Dynamic Equilibrium

November 2026. 452 words.

In chemistry, a reaction at equilibrium looks still. Nothing seems to change. But underneath, the forward reaction and the reverse reaction are both still running, at the same rate. The stillness is two flows cancelling out. That’s a dynamic equilibrium, and it’s a better picture of balance than a scale at rest.

Disturb it and it moves. Le Chatelier’s principle says a system at equilibrium shifts to absorb a change: add more of one side and the reaction runs the other way until it settles at a new point. The body does the same. Walter Cannon called it homeostasis: your temperature holds near 37 degrees not because nothing happens, but because sweating and shivering keep pushing it back.

Control and letting go

Control and letting go are two flows like that. One pushes on the world, the other lets the world push back. In Control, and Letting the World Play Out, the failures were each flow running alone: all control makes you rigid and fragile; all letting go is drift. A good life is both running at once, steering your part and letting the rest play out.

From outside, that can look calm. Inside, it’s constant small corrections. And when life pushes, a new job, a child, an illness, the point shifts and settles again. The right mix at twenty isn’t the right mix at fifty.

Sellers and buyers

A market price is the clearest dynamic equilibrium in everyday life. Nobody sets it. Buyers and sellers keep trading, and the price settles where what’s offered meets what’s wanted. When something shifts, a bad harvest or a new fashion, the price moves and the trading brings it to a new point. Alfred Marshall said asking whether supply or demand sets a price is like asking which blade of the scissors cuts the paper.

The market for meaning works the same way (The Market for the Need). Buyers come with a need that never goes away; sellers offer answers. When uncertainty rises, demand rises and sellers rush in. When existential security rises, religiosity falls. What looks like a stable culture is buyers and sellers still trading.

What it teaches

  • Stillness on the surface can be motion underneath. Balance isn’t the absence of forces; it’s forces matched.
  • You don’t hold an equilibrium. You keep both flows running. Stop one and the balance is gone.
  • Expect the shift. A push gets answered by a move to a new point, not a return to the old one.
  • Like the two blades, neither side does the work alone.

That’s the same balance as in Balance Is the Ground, Not the Goal: not a place you stay, but one you keep coming back to.

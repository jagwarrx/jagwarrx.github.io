Active Learning Is the Only Learning

November 2026. 412 words.

Reading is not learning. Reading is familiarizing.

Thinking, reasoning and recalling are learning.

Familiar is not known

After reading something, it feels known. I recognize the words, the argument makes sense, I nod along. But recognizing is not the same as being able to produce it. Close the book and try to explain it, and most of it is gone.

That feeling of knowing is the trap. It’s strongest right after reading, which is exactly when I’ve done the least work.

What counts

  • Thinking: working something out instead of being told it.
  • Reasoning: connecting one idea to the next, and seeing why it follows.
  • Recalling: pulling it back out of my head with nothing in front of me.

Each one makes me produce something. That’s the test: if nothing came out of my head, nothing went in.

The ranking

Solving real problems > Socratic learning > flashcards and mind maps.

  • Solving real problems. All three at once: I think, I reason, I recall, and the problem tells me if I got it right. Nothing is set up for me.
  • Socratic learning. Thinking and reasoning, but the questions are chosen for me.
  • Flashcards and mind maps. Recall and connection. They keep what I learned, but they don’t test whether I can use it.

Reading isn’t on the list.

What to do with reading

Reading still has a job. It’s the first contact, the raw material. It just isn’t the learning. So every reading session needs a step after it:

  • Close it and explain it from memory.
  • Answer questions about it, one at a time: the Socratic method.
  • Draw it as a mind map without looking.
  • Turn the parts worth keeping into flashcards, and recall them over the weeks.

Deliberate practice

Solving real problems sits at the top, but not every problem teaches. Deliberate practice is the version that does, from Anders Ericsson’s research on how experts get good:

  • At the edge. Work just past what I can already do. Too easy is repetition, too hard is flailing.
  • A specific goal. Not “practice system design” but “get the data model right for this one problem.”
  • Fast feedback. Know quickly whether I got it right, and why not.
  • On the weak spots. Go back to what I get wrong, not what I already enjoy doing well.
  • Full attention. It’s tiring, so it comes in short, focused blocks.

Doing the same thing for years is experience. Doing it at the edge, with feedback, is practice.

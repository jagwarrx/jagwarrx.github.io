Is the Need the Core Need?

November 2026. 1,200 words.

Most of these essays start from one sentence. All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. I’ve used it to read religion, nations, entertainment, ADHD, meditation, meaning and how we present ourselves to other people. A frame that does that much work deserves a hard look. Is it actually the core human need? Or have I just found a lens that fits everything because I keep pointing it at things?

This essay is that look. The short answer is: partly.

Two versions of the claim

The sentence can mean two different things. The strong version says the need to know what comes next is the only core need, and every other need is a form of it. The weak version says it comes first: before belonging, esteem or meaning, there is the need to have some grip on what happens next.

The evidence treats these two very differently.

The case for

The strongest support comes from neuroscience. Karl Friston’s free energy principle says that every living thing, to stay alive, has to keep itself within the states it expects to be in. A fish has to stay in water. A body has to stay near 37 degrees. Anything that drifts too far from its expected states dies. On this view, hunger is a prediction error about blood sugar, and fear is a prediction error about safety. Every need becomes a version of one need: keep surprise low. That’s the strong version, stated as a theory of life.

Psychology gives more modest support, but it’s broad. When researchers list basic human needs, something like the need almost always appears. Seymour Epstein listed four, and one was keeping a coherent picture of the world. Klaus Grawe listed four, and one was orientation and control. Carol Dweck, in 2017, proposed three, and one was what she called optimal predictability. Maslow’s second level, safety, is close to it too.

And there’s clinical support. Nicholas Carleton reviewed the research on anxiety and asked whether fear of the unknown might be the fear underneath the others. Intolerance of uncertainty shows up across anxiety disorders, depression and more. His title asked whether it is one fear to rule them all.

Finally, the need seems to come first in a practical sense. You can’t pursue belonging, competence or meaning in a world you can’t predict at all. Some grip on what comes next is the ground the other needs stand on.

The case against: belonging

The first objection is that another need looks just as basic. In the 1950s Harry Harlow raised infant monkeys with two artificial mothers: one made of wire that gave milk, and one covered in soft cloth that gave nothing. The infants spent almost all their time clinging to the cloth mother. Comfort and contact mattered more than food.

Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary later argued that the need to belong is a fundamental human motive in its own right, and most models agree. Self-determination theory, one of the best-supported theories of motivation, names three basic needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. Predictability isn’t on the list.

You can try to fold belonging into the need. John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth described a secure attachment as a secure base, and a secure base is, among other things, a carer whose responses you can predict. But that feels like stretching. The infant on the cloth mother isn’t forecasting anything. It wants to be held.

The case against: we don’t want full predictability

The second objection is sharper. If the need were simply to know what comes next, we’d seek out the most predictable life possible. We don’t. We get bored. We seek novelty, surprise, play and stories whose endings we don’t know.

Friston’s theory ran into this directly. Critics called it the dark room problem: if organisms minimize surprise, why don’t they just sit in a dark, quiet room forever? The answer Friston and colleagues gave is that organisms also seek information, because exploring now makes the future less surprising later. Curiosity is built into the theory.

Celeste Kidd and colleagues found a lovely piece of evidence for this in babies. Infants shown sequences of events looked away from the ones that were too predictable and also from the ones that were too random. They paid most attention to the ones in between. Kidd called it the Goldilocks effect. That’s why Dweck’s word is optimal predictability, not maximal. In Culture, Entertainment and the Need I wrote about comedy and improv as safe surprise. The need doesn’t want the gap closed forever. It wants it closable.

The case against: it explains too much

The third objection is about me. Karl Popper argued that a theory that can explain everything explains nothing, because no observation could ever count against it. A frame that fits religion, nationhood, comedy, ADHD, meditation and meaning should make you suspicious, not confident.

It’s a fair charge. Almost any behavior can be described as reducing uncertainty about something. If I can always find the need in anything, finding it doesn’t prove much. In Why Belief Systems Survive I wrote about how belief systems protect themselves by absorbing every observation. A frame for reading belief systems can do the same thing.

The honest test is whether the frame predicts something the alternatives don’t. Sometimes it does. William Swann’s finding that people prefer accurate feedback to flattering feedback is surprising under an esteem frame and expected under this one. Beecher’s soldiers needing less morphine than civilians is easier to explain as pain with a future than as pain with less damage. Where the frame earns its place is in predictions like these. Where it merely redescribes, it’s decoration.

What survives

Taking the evidence together, here’s what I think holds.

The weak version survives. As the brain’s first job, some grip on what comes next is the most general need there is, and it appears in nearly every serious list of basic needs. Fear of the unknown runs underneath a great deal of human distress.

The strong version doesn’t. Belonging stands on its own in most models, and the infant on the cloth mother isn’t forecasting. Self-worth probably stands on its own too.

And the need is better stated with one more phrase. Not to know what comes next, full stop, but to know what comes next well enough to act. That version leaves room for curiosity, which is the need asking for information rather than certainty, and for the Goldilocks babies, who want a world that’s learnable, not one that’s finished.

The short version

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. As the first need, it holds up: neuroscience puts something like it at the root of life, psychology puts it in nearly every list of basic needs, and anxiety research suggests fear of the unknown sits underneath many others. As the only need, it doesn’t: belonging looks just as basic, and we seek surprise as well as certainty.

So the need is a core need, probably the most general one, and the frame is worth using wherever it predicts something new. Where it only redescribes, it’s a lens, and I should say so.

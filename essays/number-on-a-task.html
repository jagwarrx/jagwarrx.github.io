The Unreasonable Effectiveness of a Number

November 2026. 537 words.

“Review flashcards” sits on my list for days. “Review 10 cards” gets done before lunch. “Work on the essay” drifts. “Five blocks on the essay this week” happens.

The only change is a number. It works far better than something that small should, so it’s worth asking why. And the number can be anything except time.

What a number does

It makes an end. “Review flashcards” never finishes, because there are always more cards. “Ten cards” finishes at ten. A task that can be finished is easier to start, and finishing is the payoff an endless list never gives.

It sizes the task before I start. “How much is enough?” is one of the questions that stands in front of any task. The number answers it ahead of time, so it’s one less decision in the moment.

It shows progress while I work. Four of ten is a score, and it moves. An ADHD brain runs on rewards that come now, not at the end, and a count gives one at every step. People speed up as they get near a goal: in one study, coffee-shop customers with a stamp card bought coffee more often the closer they were to a free one. That’s the goal gradient, and a count puts me on one.

It turns “do your best” into a target. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham spent decades showing that specific goals beat “do your best.” “Do your best” has no line, so there’s no way to know whether I’ve met it. Ten has a line.

Why not time

“Study for an hour” has a number in it, and it works much less well.

Time counts what goes in, not what comes out. An hour can pass with nothing done; I can sit through it. Ten cards can’t pass without ten cards being reviewed. And time is the one thing I have no sense of, so a target in minutes is a target I can’t feel moving.

A count of blocks looks like time, but it isn’t. It counts starts. Five blocks means starting five times, and starting is the hardest part. So for open-ended work, where there’s no natural unit of output, I count the starts.

Picking the number

Pick a unit that is the work. Cards, pages, problems, words, calls, sections, reps. If the unit can be hit without doing the real thing, it will be. That’s Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes the target, it stops being a good measure.

Pick it small. Small enough to hit on a bad day. Going over is a bonus. Falling short is a reason to skip tomorrow.

Pick it ahead. The night before, with the rest of the plan. A number chosen in the moment gets chosen by the mood of the moment.

Count where I can see it. Tally marks, a counter, a line ticked off. A count I have to remember isn’t a count.

The short version

Put a number on the task, and make it anything other than time. It gives the task an end, answers “how much?” before I start, and gives me a score that goes up as I work. That’s a lot to get from one number.

Notes

  1. The title borrows from Eugene Wigner, “The Unreasonable Effectiveness of Mathematics in the Natural Sciences,” 1960.
  2. Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, “The goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected: purchase acceleration, illusionary goal progress, and customer retention,” Journal of Marketing Research, 2006. The idea goes back to Clark Hull, 1932.
  3. Locke and Latham, “Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation: a 35-year odyssey,” American Psychologist, 2002.

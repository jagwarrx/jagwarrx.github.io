Log the Block, Don’t Start It

November 2026. 360 words.

Two things are worth tracking: blocks and habits. So the log is one button. Tap it, pick the type, done.

  • Block: which project. The count goes up by one.
  • Habit: which one. Meditation, reading, exercise.

That’s the whole app. Log often, in seconds, right after the thing happens.

Start on paper, log in the app

The block starts on the sticky note: write the task, start the timer, go. The app comes in only at the end, to log the finished block.

I had it the other way round. An app where I picked a card, pressed start, breathed, watched a timer, parked it, resumed it. Each of those is a screen between me and the work, and a phone in my hand at the moment I’m most likely to drift. The start is the fragile part. It should cost nothing: a pen and a note.

Why logging, not planning, belongs in the app

  • A plan in the app goes stale. By noon the cards don’t match the day, and then I stop opening it. A log is always true. It only records what happened.
  • One button beats many screens. If logging takes a decision about where, it gets skipped. One button, two types, no choice about where it goes.
  • Logged blocks are the count. Blocks per project against the plan, and blocks today. No hours, no timer data.
  • Logging is a small win. Tapping the button right after a block is the moment I notice I did it.

What the counts show

  • Today: blocks done against the day’s target.
  • The week: blocks per project per day, against each project’s week target.
  • Each project: blocks done against its total, the pace I’m going, the pace the due date needs, and on track or behind.
  • Habits: a tick per day, times this week against a goal, and a streak.

All of it comes from one kind of entry: a finished block, logged.

What it leaves out

No schedule, no running timer, no task list. Tasks live in the task app, the current one lives on the sticky note, the time lives on a timer. The log app does one job.

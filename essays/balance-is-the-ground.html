Balance Is the Ground, Not the Goal

November 2026. 509 words.

A question I asked myself: in truly spiritual societies, is there less war and strife? And a second one behind it. If people are lazy for too long, that leads to disease too. So is being in balance the highest goal?

Devout is not the same as peaceful

Not reliably. Being devout has never kept a society out of war. Medieval Christendom, the Islamic caliphates and the Buddhist kingdoms all fought plenty of them, sometimes in religion’s name.

But there’s a real distinction inside the word. Religion as group identity can sharpen us-versus-them thinking. That’s the side I looked at in The Nation as Religion, where the border becomes sacred and the people across it stop being quite human. Spirituality as inner practice, meaning humility, compassion and self-restraint, tends to make people gentler.

The communities with the clearest records of peace are the Quakers, the Jains, the Amish and some Buddhist monastic orders. What they share isn’t a stronger belief. They made nonviolence a daily discipline: something you do, in small ways, every day, not something you hold. So what lowers strife seems to be the practiced values, not spirituality as such. In the terms of What Each Religion Sells, it’s the technique that does the work, not the label on the product.

The body wants rhythm

The point about laziness is right. Long inactivity is a major risk factor for heart disease, diabetes and depression. The reverse holds too: chronic overwork and stress also make people sick. Neither end is safe. The body seems built for rhythm, effort and then rest, not for a constant setting in the middle.

That’s worth noticing, because it already changes what balance means. It isn’t a flat line. It’s a swing that keeps coming back.

Is balance the top?

Many traditions put it close to the center. The Buddha’s Middle Way, between indulgence and self-punishment. Aristotle’s golden mean, where courage sits between cowardice and recklessness. Confucius’s Doctrine of the Mean. Taoism’s yin and yang.

Look closely, though, and most of them treat balance as a path, not the destination. For Aristotle, the mean serves flourishing. For the Buddha, the Middle Way is the road to liberation, the waking up I wrote about in What Is Enlightenment? Christianity puts love highest, not moderation.

And some of the best things come from being out of balance on purpose. A parent with a newborn. An artist consumed by the work. Someone fighting an injustice. None of them is in balance, and none of them should be, for a while. What makes it bearable is the same thing as in Meaning Makes Pain Bearable: the cost is paid for something.

The short version

Balance is best understood as the ground, not the top. It’s what lets the highest things grow, love, wisdom and compassion, and it’s what you come back to after you’ve spent yourself on one of them. Spiritual societies aren’t reliably peaceful; practiced ones are more often. And the body, like the traditions, asks for rhythm rather than a fixed point.

Notes

  1. On peace churches and nonviolent communities: the Quaker Peace Testimony (from 1660); ahimsa in Jainism; the Amish practice of nonresistance.
  2. Physical inactivity as a risk factor: World Health Organization, Global recommendations on physical activity for health, 2010, and later WHO guidelines.
  3. The Middle Way: the Buddha’s first sermon, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta. The mean: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, book II. The Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong), one of the Four Books of Confucianism. “The greatest of these is love”: 1 Corinthians 13:13.

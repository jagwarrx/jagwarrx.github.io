Principles Are Guiding Policy for a Life

November 2026. 1,009 words.

Principles are guiding policy. The term guiding policy comes from Richard Rumelt’s Good Strategy Bad Strategy. His kernel of a strategy has three parts: a diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and a set of coherent actions that carry it out. A strategy is built for one challenge. Principles are guiding policy at the level of a life.

The stake and the rope

The picture I keep coming back to is a stake and a rope. Values are the bedrock: what you care about, like honesty, family, freedom or health. Principles are the stake driven into it: deep and slow to change. Policies are the rope: specific ways of acting that you adjust as conditions shift. Day-to-day decisions are the movement at the end of the rope.

“Take care of my health” is the principle. “Walk every morning” is the policy, and you change it when you get injured or your life changes, without giving up the principle. Governments work this way at their best. A constitution says “equal protection under the law,” and the laws built on it get rewritten over generations as a society learns what that principle actually requires.

Things go wrong when the layers get mixed up, in two ways. You treat a policy as sacred and defend it after it has stopped serving its purpose. Or you drop a principle because one policy built on it failed. Keeping them apart is how you stay firm and flexible at the same time.

Values and principles keep each other honest

A principle is a rule for acting on a value. “I value honesty” becomes “tell the truth even when it costs me.” The exception is a principle that describes how reality works, like “trust is slow to build and quick to lose.” That one holds whether you care about it or not, which is the sense Ray Dalio uses in Principles.

Each has a weakness the other covers. Values motivate but stay vague: “I value kindness” doesn’t say what to do when kindness and honesty clash. Values alone invite rationalizing, bending case by case while telling yourself you’re still true to them. Principles guide action but can harden and outlive the value they served. And principles with no felt value behind them are hard to keep when no one is watching.

Tradeoffs decided ahead of time

Many principles are recurring tradeoffs, settled in advance. “Long term over short term.” “Honesty over comfort.” The Agile Manifesto is written this way: working software over comprehensive documentation, while the second still has value. There are roughly three kinds:

  • Priorities, “A over B”: they rank goods that will collide.
  • Bright lines, “never do X”: they don’t weigh anything. They take options off the table so they never enter the tradeoff.
  • Descriptions, “trust builds slowly”: they inform tradeoffs without being choices.

The reason to decide in advance is that you choose worse in the moment. Under pressure the short-term option always looks bigger. A principle is Ulysses tied to the mast: your clearer self binds your future self before the sirens start. It also makes you predictable, which is most of why other people can trust you.

Strategy works the same way. Michael Porter said the essence of strategy is choosing what not to do. And both fail the same way, by turning into fluff. “Be a good person” isn’t a principle, and “be the best in the market” isn’t a strategy. Each has to rule something out. The difference is scope and time: principles last for years across a whole life and set the boundaries, meaning what you won’t do to win. A strategy is the route across one stretch of mud, and you redraw it when the terrain changes. If a strategy needs the stake pulled up, either you’ve lost your way or the principle needs rethinking.

The diagnosis underneath

In Rumelt’s kernel, a guiding policy only makes sense as an answer to a diagnosis. So principles for a life rest on a diagnosis of what the central challenge of a life is, stated or not.

The traditions are built this way. The Buddha’s Four Noble Truths read like a medical consultation: the symptom is suffering, the cause is craving, the prognosis is that it can end, and the treatment is the Eightfold Path (more in Buddhism Through the Lens of the Need). The Stoics diagnosed suffering as trying to control what we can’t, and their main principle follows: focus only on what’s in your power.

Mine: we live in dynamic systems that keep pulling us off balance, so the policy is to anchor to principles and keep correcting. That’s the same point as Balance Is the Ground, Not the Goal: balance isn’t a place you stay, it’s where you come back to.

The whole kernel, mapped onto a life:

  • Diagnosis: your worldview, what you think the central challenge is.
  • Guiding policy: your principles.
  • Coherent actions: your habits and daily practices, the part that, as in Free Up the Higher Mind, you can hand to automation.

It also tells you what to check when your principles stop working. Sometimes the principle is fine and the habits don’t match it. Sometimes the diagnosis underneath has changed: principles built for a struggling twenty-year-old may not fit the challenges of fifty.

Write them down

You don’t have to. Most people live by principles they absorbed growing up and never wrote. But for the ones you choose, writing helps: it forces precision, it shows where two of them collide, and it lets you revise what you can see. Marcus Aurelius’s Meditations was a private notebook of reminders to himself. Benjamin Franklin listed 13 virtues and worked on one each week.

Keep the list short, five to ten. Use your own words. Phrase them as tradeoffs, “when X, I choose Y,” not abstractions. Reread them, especially after a hard decision or after breaking one. The document isn’t the point, though. A principle is yours once it shapes your choices without you having to look it up.

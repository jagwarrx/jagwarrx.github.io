Meeting the Need with ADHD

November 2026. 1,360 words.

The last two essays were about a need. In The Need Is Built In: uncertainty hurts, the brain fills the gap with a story, and anxiety turns into ritual. In The ADHD Version of the Need: in an ADHD brain the same need is tuned to the next ten minutes rather than next year. And sellers sell into it. For ADHD, the new productivity system is the horoscope of the near term.

But the need doesn’t go away when you stop buying. If you don’t meet it, it gets met anyway, by whatever answers “what now?” fastest. Usually that’s the phone.

So this one is practical. What meets the need honestly?

By honestly I mean three things. It ends the uncertainty without pretending to change the future. It costs almost nothing. And it doesn’t depend on my keeping it going. That last one rules out most systems.

The useful thing about knowing the mechanism is that each part of it has its own fix. There are four parts, and four fixes.

Not knowing what to do: decide the night before

The uncertainty that hurts most in ADHD is “what do I do right now?” It feels like waiting, and waiting is aversive. And under that pressure an ADHD brain seizes the first answer, which is rarely the right one.

The fix is to move the decision to a time when the need isn’t firing. The morning, when everything is open at once, is the worst time to choose. The evening before, with the day’s work done, is a good one. Philosophers call this precommitment: Ulysses having himself tied to the mast before the ship reaches the Sirens. You make the choice while you’re able to, so you don’t have to make it while you’re not.

The list should be short: three things at most, on a sticky note, not in an app, because opening an app means looking at everything again. And the first item should start with a step concrete enough to begin without thinking. Peter Gollwitzer’s work on implementation intentions, plans of the form “when X happens, I’ll do Y,” finds that saying exactly when and how you’ll start makes starting much more likely.

Done this way, there’s no uncertainty left in the morning. The answer to “what now?” is already in view.

The failure mode is the list growing. A list of twelve things is the same open question as no list, just written down.

Faint time: make it visible

The second part is time blindness. If there are only two times, now and not now, then an hour has no shape. Ten minutes and two hours feel the same from the inside, and a deadline next week doesn’t exist until it’s tomorrow.

Russell Barkley’s advice for ADHD comes down to one idea: if the brain can’t hold it internally, put it outside, at the point where you do the work. Time is the clearest case. A timer turns “not now” into a countdown you can see.

It also helps with the need directly. A task with no end is open-ended uncertainty. A task with a timer on it has a known end, and the last essay’s evidence is that it’s the uncertainty that hurts, not the size of the thing. Twenty-five minutes of something hard, with the end in sight, is easier than an unbounded afternoon of something easy.

Use a timer you can see, not one hidden in a phone. And use it to start, not just to stop: “at 9:00 the timer starts” is a cue the day can hang on. Constraints Drive Actions is about exactly this kind of design: a block has a length, and a timer runs.

Waiting hurts: bring the reward close

The third part is the reward. In most brains the anticipation of a reward carries some of its pull, so a payoff at the end of a long task still reaches back to the start. In ADHD that seems to be weaker. The pull has to come from the reward itself, and it has to come soon. A task that only pays at the end loses to anything that pays now.

So the fix is to put rewards inside the task, not just at the end of it.

The cheapest reward is visible progress. Crossing something off is a small payoff that arrives the moment the work does. That’s one more reason to keep tasks small: a task you can finish in one block pays out today.

The next is pairing. Katherine Milkman calls it temptation bundling: allow yourself something you want only while doing something you should. In her study, people who could listen to audiobooks they loved only at the gym went to the gym more, though the effect faded over the weeks. It doesn’t have to last forever. It just has to get the block started.

The failure mode is the reward becoming the task. A reward that can swallow an hour, like a phone, isn’t a reward. It’s the thing you were trying to avoid.

Can’t keep a ritual going: automate it

The fourth part is the hardest. Ritual works: even rituals people know are meaningless lower anxiety. But ADHD can’t keep a ritual going, because keeping it going takes the things ADHD is short of: remembering, sustaining, doing the same thing tomorrow when it’s no longer new. That’s why the eighteen systems died. Each one was a ritual that needed me to keep it alive.

The fix is a ritual that doesn’t need me. That’s the argument of Not Tools, but Automation. A tool waits for me to remember it. Automation runs anyway. The test is the one from that essay: does it still work on a day I forget it exists?

The clearest example is the morning brief: one short message that arrives without my asking, with today’s three things, the first step and the fixed times. It answers “what now?” before the need has a chance to ask.

The failure mode here is a trap, because building automation is itself novel, absorbing and feels like control. It can become the nineteenth system. The guard is the rule from The goal is not a well-organized life: measure the system by one number, minutes per day you spend on it. If that number rises, the system is failing, however elegant it looks.

The far future: bring it near

All four fixes are about the next ten minutes, because that’s where the need is loudest. The far future has the opposite problem. It pulls too weakly, so it gets no plan at all.

It can’t be met as a goal for next year, because next year is “not now.” It has to be turned into something that fits the near-term machinery: one step small enough to go on tonight’s list. Not “get healthier” but “book the appointment.” Not “write more” but “finish this paragraph.” Once it’s on the list, the list does the rest.

What not to do

One moment deserves its own rule: the moment the old system collapses.

That’s when the need is strongest. Everything is open again, nothing answers “what now?”, and a new system promises to end it all at once. It’s also when judgment is worst. It’s the open sea, and it’s exactly when a seller does best.

So don’t switch then. Go back to the smallest version: a sticky note with three things on it. And before changing anything, write the fault first: what went wrong, and when. If I can’t name one, the system isn’t what failed.

The short version

Four parts of the need, four fixes. Not knowing what to do: decide the night before. Faint time: make it visible. Waiting hurts: bring the reward close. Can’t keep a ritual going: automate it.

None of these changes the future, and none of them claims to. That’s what makes them honest. They do the one thing the need actually asks for, which is to end the uncertainty about what comes next, and they do it cheaply, early, and without my having to remember.

The need is built in. The answer is to meet it before it has to ask.

The Language of an ADHD System

November 2026. 739 words.

An ADHD brain needs an other: a system outside the head. But the system only helps if it speaks the language the brain can hear. So what should it say, and how, so the brain can take it in and act?

What it should say

Seven messages. Most systems send one or two of them, and the wrong way.

MessageWhat it saysExample
What nowOne next action, not the listOpen the doc, write three lines
How muchA number, and how much is left2 blocks; 1 left
By when, for whomThe stakesBefore lunch; Sam is waiting
Done so farProof of progress, right awayA tally on the sticky note
Not nowIt’s parked, safe to forgetOn the list for Sunday’s look
StopThe block or the day is overTimer rings; 6 of 6 blocks
You driftedA check, not a scoldingBell: did this block go to the essay?

“Not now” matters as much as “what now.” The brain lets go of a thing only once it trusts that something else is holding it.

When each message comes

WhenMessages
The night beforeWhat now, how much, by when, not now
During a blockNothing. Only the timer
After each blockDone so far, you drifted
End of the dayStop, at a count of blocks, not a clock time

Every message is written the night before, by a calmer me. The system only delivers it (when this, do that). During a block it says nothing. The drift check comes at the bell, so the most I lose is one block.

How it should say it

  • The same every day. Same place, same marks. A language has to be stable to become automatic, and that takes weeks. A new tool is a new language, and I start learning it again.
  • Already where I look. A note on the desk, a count in the top bar. Not in an app three taps away: a system I have to remember to open says nothing. But in view, not pushed. An alert is a push onto the stack, so the only thing that rings is the timer.
  • One message at a time. Only the top of the stack is heard. Show the next action, and hide the rest.
  • Verb first, one line. “Email Sam about the draft.” If it needs a sentence to explain, it isn’t ready.
  • Seen more than read. Position, color, a checkbox, a column. Marks do the work words do badly.
  • At the right moment. When this, do that: the message arrives with its trigger. Too early, it’s noise. Too late, it’s a scolding.
  • Answers right away. A tick, a tally, a sound. The loop closes now, not at the end of the week. Log the block the moment it’s done.

What it should never say

  • Everything at once. A list of forty is a wall, not a message.
  • A wall of red. Overdue counts and streaks broken add bricks to the Wall of Awful. Show today, not the record of every miss.
  • Constant alerts. When every alert fires, none of them mean anything. Hospitals call it alarm fatigue. One of my old systems sent 311 reminders after I had stopped using it, and none of them changed anything. Each message has to be worth hearing, or they all get ignored.
  • Two voices for one message. Two timers, two lists of today. Each message comes from one place, or I have to decide which one to believe.
  • “Not now” with no later. Parking only works if the later comes. If the weekly look never happens, nothing is safe to forget, and the parked idea gets built today.

Who says it in my system

ToolMessages
AntinoteWhat now: three lines, the first step on top
PlankaHow much, done so far, not now, stop, and the drift check when a block ends
Objectives plannerBy when, in the language of importance
ClaudeNothing during the day. It translates at set times: at close, the objectives and the board become tomorrow’s three lines; at the review, the blocks are read back against the objectives.

The five-second test

Look at the system. Can I act on what it says within five seconds, without thinking? If not, it’s speaking the wrong language. Shorten it, move it where I look, or cut it down to the next step.

Negotiation Needs Structure and Empathy

November 2026. 627 words.

For negotiation itself, the two books worth reading are Getting to Yes by Roger Fisher and William Ury, on principled negotiation, and Never Split the Difference by Chris Voss. They argue with each other, and you need both: Fisher and Ury give the structure, Voss the skills for the emotional side of the conversation.

The structure: Getting to Yes

Look at interests, not positions. Know your alternative if the deal falls through, the BATNA in Secure Your Position First. Settle things by fair standards, not a contest of wills. And when the other side plays dirty, name the tactic (The Art of Defense).

The skills: Never Split the Difference

Voss became the FBI’s lead international kidnapping negotiator, and wrote the book with Tahl Raz, who also co-wrote Never Eat Alone. His main argument is that the biggest error of conventional negotiating theory is assuming people are logical and act in their own best interest. That makes the book a direct challenge to Getting to Yes. The title is his point about compromise: splitting the difference often leaves both sides with something neither actually wanted.

The core skill is what he calls tactical empathy:

  • Mirroring. Repeat the last few words someone said, with a curious tone. They’ll usually explain more.
  • Labeling. Name what they seem to feel: “It sounds like you’re worried about…” There’s research behind this: putting feelings into words calms the amygdala.
  • Accusation audit. Say the worst things they might be thinking about you before they do. Said out loud, it loses its sting.
  • “That’s right,” not “you’re right.” Summarize their view so well that they say “that’s right,” which means they feel understood. “You’re right” is usually a polite way to end the conversation.
  • Questions that invite no. “Is now a bad time?” rather than “Do you have a minute?” Saying no makes people feel safe and in control.
  • Calibrated questions. Open “how” and “what” questions, like “How am I supposed to do that?” They get the other side working on your problem. Avoid “why,” which sounds like an accusation.
  • Voice. Calm, slow and low: his “late-night FM DJ voice.”

Bargaining: the Ackerman method

Pick your target price. Open at 65% of it, then go to 85%, 95% and finally 100%, each step smaller than the last. Make the last offer an exact, non-round number and add a small non-money extra. The precise number has research behind it: people who got precise offers countered closer to them, because a precise number signals that you know what you’re talking about.

How it fits

  • As defense. “How am I supposed to do that?” says no without a confrontation and hands the problem back. Knowing these moves also protects you when someone uses them on you, which is the inoculation idea again.
  • With the network. The network is what you bring to the table: your alternatives and your reputation (Build the Network Before You Need It). Most negotiations are with people you’ll see again, so how you negotiate feeds back into the network.

The caveats

The tactics come from one-off situations. Hostage negotiations rarely involve a long relationship, and the hardball parts, like Ackerman steps and pressure, can feel manipulative with colleagues or family. Most of the book is anecdote. Labeling and precise offers have research support, but much of the rest hasn’t been tested, and he leans on the popular 7-38-55 rule about tone and body language, which is widely stretched beyond the narrow lab studies it came from.

So use the empathy tools all the time, and save the bargaining tactics for one-off deals. In a repeated game you want both animals: the serpent reads the room, the dove keeps the relationship (The Serpent and the Dove).

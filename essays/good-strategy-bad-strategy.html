Good Strategy, Bad Strategy

November 2026. 850 words.

Richard Rumelt’s definition: a strategy is a way through a hard problem. It says what the problem is, picks an approach, and lines up actions behind that approach. A document that doesn’t do those three things isn’t a strategy, whatever it’s called. Strategy isn’t ambition, vision or a target. It’s finding the place where a push will do the most, and pushing there with everything you can spare.

At Trafalgar in 1805, Nelson had 27 ships against 33. The usual battle was two parallel lines firing at each other, which favors the bigger fleet. He sent two columns straight at the enemy line to cut it into pieces, accepting that his lead ships would take fire they couldn’t return. The enemy lost 22 ships; the British lost none. One problem, one approach, actions that fit, a cost accepted with open eyes. No slogan, no list of priorities.

The kernel

  • Diagnosis. What’s going on here? A good one simplifies, explains and points: it sets most facts aside, says why things are as they are, and shows where action could work. It’s a judgment, not a fact, and the same facts can support more than one reading. Ask whether a sensible person could read it differently, and what would prove it wrong. For problems that keep repeating, The Structural Lens is the tool for this stage.
  • Guiding policy. The approach you choose. It works like a guardrail: it doesn’t give every step, it says which steps are out. Ask what it forbids. If the answer is nothing, it isn’t a policy.
  • Coherent actions. Concrete steps that carry out the policy and support each other. Three projects pushing the same way beat ten pulling ten ways. Fit doesn’t happen by itself: someone has to impose it, which means telling capable people no.

Remove any one part and it fails. A diagnosis with no policy is commentary. A policy with no actions is a wish. Actions with no diagnosis are a to-do list.

Bad strategy

Bad strategy isn’t good strategy that failed. It’s writing that looks like strategy but skips the problem and skips the choice. Four signs:

  • Fluff. Inflated words that restate the obvious. Translate it into plain words and see if anything is left.
  • No problem named. Goals, budgets and forecasts, with nothing about what stands in the way.
  • Goals called strategy. A target plus a call for effort. Ask “how?” If the answer is motivation, there’s no strategy.
  • Bad objectives. A long list of everything anyone wanted, or the wish written again as an objective nobody knows how to reach.

It isn’t caused by stupidity. It’s the easy way out. Real strategy needs a hard choice and honest talk about problems, and choosing means someone loses. Templates and positive thinking help people avoid both.

The crux

In The Crux, Rumelt borrows a climbing word: the crux is the hardest move on the route. Strategy picks the most important challenge you have a real chance of solving. List every problem and opening, group them, then judge each group twice: how critical is it, and can we solve it with what we have? What passes both is where to act. Don’t start with goals. Set before you understand the challenge, a goal is a guess with a number on it. It should come after, as a record of the choice.

Where the strength comes from

  • Leverage. See a little ahead, find the spot where effort multiplies, and put it there.
  • Proximate objectives. Aim at what you can hit. The more uncertain things are, the nearer the objective should be.
  • Chain links. Where the weakest part sets the result, making the strong parts stronger gains nothing. Fix the weak link first.
  • Design. Parts shaped to fit each other, like Hannibal at Cannae, where no part of the plan made sense without the others.
  • Focus. That fit, aimed at a target where it wins.

When to use it

You need a strategy when something important is in your way, the way through is unclear, and you can’t do everything. Take away any one of the three and a simpler tool will do: management when the way is clear, an experiment when the choice is small or reversible, a habit when the task repeats, plain action in an emergency. And not for rest, play and friendship: they’re the point, not a means. In a life the kernel is the same, and so is the price: something has to be given up (Principles Are Guiding Policy for a Life).

Where it shows up in these notes

  • A position is strength you build first. Sun Tzu’s first move, putting yourself beyond defeat, is the half in your own hands (Secure Your Position First).
  • Concentration. Skills have thresholds: one taken past the point where others notice beats five left half-learned. That’s the lean towards mastery (Understand Power as Defense, Lean Towards Mastery).
  • Anticipation. Leverage starts with seeing how others will act. In a negotiation that means their interests and their alternatives, not their stated position (Negotiation Needs Structure and Empathy).

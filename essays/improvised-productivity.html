Improvised Productivity

November 2026. 375 words.

Start a timer, then figure it out.

Novelty plays a key role. A timer adds a sense of stakes. Money adds a sense of stakes. People add a sense of stakes.

Once a timer is running you’ve made a small commitment, and you’ve added a bit of urgency. ADHD brains tend to run on urgency, novelty, and interest more than on importance, so a ticking clock can give you the push that “I should really do this” doesn’t. It also gets around the paralysis of needing the perfect plan before you can begin.

Three sources of stakes

  • A timer: the cheapest. The clock is running, so this block is now. The Block Method is a timer with a tally.
  • Money: a bet, a deposit I lose if I don’t follow through, a paid class. Losing money stings more than not gaining it.
  • People: tell someone, work beside someone, book a time with someone else in it. Letting a person down costs more than letting myself down.

All three are ways to borrow the stakes early, instead of waiting until the task turns dangerous.

How it fails

  • The choosing eats the timer. A 25-minute timer, and 15 of it goes to deciding, or browsing “for ideas.”
  • Drift toward the easy or shiny task. Without some structure I pick whatever feels good right now. Busy, but the dreaded important thing never gets touched.
  • Nothing connects across sessions. Each block is productive, but they don’t add up, because each one starts from zero.

Small fixes

  • A short running menu of 3 to 5 things. When the timer starts, I pick from a list instead of inventing one. It still feels spontaneous, and it takes seconds. (Choosing a Task)
  • A 2-minute decision window. Not picked by then? Do the first thing on the menu, or the one I’m avoiding most.
  • A breadcrumb when the timer ends. One line: “next: email Sam about X.” The next session’s “figure it out” is then almost instant. Recall first, then read it: The Novelty of Active Recall.
  • A scrap pad nearby. Random thoughts mid-session go on the pad, so I can drop them without chasing them.

The honest test

If you look back at a week and the things that actually mattered got done, your system works, however improvised it looks.

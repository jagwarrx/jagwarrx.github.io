The Nation as Religion

November 2026. 1,540 words.

At the end of The Market for the Need I said that the largest rebundle of the modern age is the nation-state. Religion was once a bundle: community, ritual, meaning, identity, a calendar, rites of passage, and the promise of being remembered after death. Over the last three centuries, the nation took over most of it.

That raises a question worth taking seriously. Does the nation-state have the same qualities as a religion? Not as an insult, and not as a metaphor, but as a description. If you ran a nation through the same five questions I used in What Each Religion Sells, what would you find?

The short answer is that it passes almost every test, fails two, and the two it fails are what make its fine print heavier than any church’s.

The idea is old

I’m not the first to see this. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in The Social Contract in 1762, argued that every state needs a civil religion: a few simple dogmas that make citizens love their duties. He meant it as a proposal.

Durkheim, a century and a half later, meant it as a description. His theory was that every religion is, at bottom, a society worshipping itself, and he used the flag as his example. A soldier who dies for a flag, he wrote, dies for a piece of cloth, and the cloth is sacred because it stands for the group.

In 1967 the sociologist Robert Bellah gave the idea its modern name in an essay called “Civil Religion in America.” He pointed out that American presidents invoke God in nearly every inaugural address, and that the country has its own prophets, martyrs, scriptures and holy days, alongside and apart from any church. Others have gone further. The historian Carlton Hayes called nationalism a religion outright, and Eric Voegelin and Raymond Aron used the term political religions for the movements of the twentieth century, fascism and communism, that demanded total faith.

The Bundle, applied

Run the nation through the five questions.

The problem it names is insecurity: invasion, disorder, humiliation, being ruled by others. The solution it promises is a people secure, sovereign and great. Many national stories have the shape of exile and return, the same shape as Judaism’s: a golden age, a fall, a struggle, a restoration.

The technique is a full set of rituals. An anthem. A flag, raised and lowered with ceremony and never allowed to touch the ground. A pledge, recited by children. A calendar of holy days: independence, the founding, the remembrance of the dead. Pilgrimage sites: the capital, the battlefields, the memorials. Scriptures: the constitution, the declaration, read with the care once given to sacred texts. And rites of passage, which the state now administers: the birth certificate, the school, the marriage license, the vote, the funeral with a flag on the coffin.

The exemplars are the founders, the heroes and above all the soldiers who died. And the nation has its own forms of sin and conversion. Treason is its heresy. Naturalization, with its oath, is its conversion.

In India the resemblance is literal. Bharat Mata, Mother India, is depicted as a goddess, and in Varanasi there’s a temple to her, opened in 1936, whose object of worship is a marble relief map of the country.

The answer to death

The deepest overlap is the one Ernest Becker would have predicted. Benedict Anderson, in Imagined Communities, opens with a striking observation. There are tombs of the unknown soldier all over the world, and they move people deeply, precisely because nobody knows who lies in them. Anderson asked whether you could imagine a tomb of the unknown liberal, or of the unknown Marxist, and concluded that you couldn’t. Only the nation, and before it religion, could turn death into continuity.

His argument is that nationalism rose just as religious certainty declined in Europe, and that it took over religion’s oldest job: making death meaningful. You will die, but the nation will go on, and you’ll be part of it. It’s an immortality project in Becker’s sense, and it’s why people have been willing to die for countries in numbers no other modern institution can match.

Anderson also explains how it happened, in terms that fit the market essay. Nations were made possible by print. Newspapers and novels in a shared language let millions of strangers imagine themselves as one community, reading the same news on the same morning. A nation is a mass audience that came to believe it was a people.

The traditions are newer than they look

Religions claim ancient origins, and so do nations. Often the claim is stretched. In The Invention of Tradition, the historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger showed how many national customs that feel timeless were in fact created in the nineteenth century, many of them deliberately, to give new states a past. Clan tartans in Scotland, which feel ancient, were largely a nineteenth-century creation. Much British royal ceremony was elaborated in the late Victorian era.

This is the same pattern as in Why Belief Systems Survive: the logic comes later. The practice is built, and then a history is written that makes it look as though it was always there.

Where it differs

So far the nation passes every test. But it fails two, and they matter.

The first is power. A church can excommunicate you. A state can imprison you, tax you, conscript you, and in the last resort kill you. Max Weber defined the state as the institution that holds a monopoly on the legitimate use of force. No religion in the modern world has that, and the ones that did, historically, are the ones whose fine print we read with most alarm.

The second is exit. You can leave a church, at least in most places. Leaving a nation is far harder. Most people are members by birth, the passport decides where they can live and work, and changing it takes years, money and permission. A religion you can’t leave is a different kind of thing.

There’s a third difference, and this one is in the nation’s favor. It delivers real goods. Roads, schools, courts, pensions, hospitals, and above all protection. Remember the first dial from the market essay: religiosity falls as existential security rises. The modern state is the biggest supplier of existential security there has ever been. Anthony Gill and Erik Lundsgaarde found that across countries, higher state welfare spending goes with lower religious participation. The state doesn’t just resemble religion. It competes with it, and where it delivers, it wins.

The fine print

By the rule from the religion essay, each answer suppresses whatever threatens it. The nation’s answer is a single people with a single story, so what it suppresses is anything that complicates the story.

Minorities who don’t fit the picture of the people. Dissenters, who can be recast as traitors. The parts of the national history that don’t match the golden age. And the humanity of the people on the other side of the border, which is the precondition for every war.

The blind spot is the border itself. A nation can make an arbitrary line on a map feel sacred, and the line decides, by the accident of birth, who gets what.

And because the seller has an army, the fine print is heavier than any church’s. That’s the real lesson of the twentieth century’s political religions. When a state demands the total faith a religion asks for, and has the power no religion has, the result can be catastrophic.

The inside critics

Like religions, nations last when they keep their critics inside. Their protective belt is a free press, independent courts, the right to dissent and the vote.

The best example is Bellah’s. Martin Luther King Jr. didn’t reject the American civil religion. He invoked it. In his most famous speech he called the Declaration of Independence a promissory note the country had failed to honor, and asked it to live up to its own creed. That’s the prophet’s move, the same one as “I desire mercy, not sacrifice”: holding a tradition to its own scripture.

When it surges

If the nation sells into the need, nationalism should rise and fall with uncertainty, and it does. Michael Hogg’s uncertainty-identity theory predicts that when people feel uncertain about themselves and their future, they identify more strongly with their group and prefer groups with sharp boundaries and strong leaders. That’s a fair description of what happens to nationalism in economic crises, after attacks and during rapid change. A frightened mass, as the market essay put it, is a strongly identified one.

The short version

The nation-state has almost every quality of a religion: a problem and a promise, rituals and scriptures, holy days and pilgrimage sites, martyrs and heretics, and an answer to death. It took them over as religious certainty declined, and it delivers more real security than any church ever did.

It differs in two ways: it holds the power to coerce, and it’s hard to leave. That makes its fine print the heaviest of all. The question from The Bundle applies with extra force here. What does it make hard to say?

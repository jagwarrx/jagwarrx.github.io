Traditional To-Do Lists Don’t Work for ADHD Brains

November 2026. 735 words.

A traditional to-do list is one long column where everything goes. You write tasks down, you tick some off, and you add more. It never ends. Finish one and three more arrive.

For an ADHD brain, that isn’t a neutral tool that needs a bit more discipline. It’s built on assumptions we don’t meet. We need focus and structure, not endless tasks. What we need is not a list of everything. It’s a short, focused list of things that are important and that we can complete today.

What the list assumes

Each assumption runs into one of the constraints I have to design around.

That you’ll look at it. Out of sight, out of mind. A list inside a closed app doesn’t exist.

That you can pick. Thirty lines all pull about equally. Attention goes to what’s new, not what’s important, so the newest line wins, or the easiest, or none.

That you feel time. A list has no clock. Nothing on it says this one takes twenty minutes and that one takes the afternoon, or that it’s already four.

That you can tell now from later. There’s no feeling for the future. Everything is in the present. A task for next month lands with the same weight as one for this afternoon, so thirty tasks feel like thirty things due now. That’s why looking at the whole list is overwhelming.

That finishing is the reward. An endless list is never finished. Ticking a line only shows the next one, so the payoff never comes.

Put together, the list stops being a plan. It turns into a record of everything I haven’t done, and I stop opening it.

Focus

Focus means less in front of me, not more effort. One thing at a time, picked from a short menu, and kept until it’s done or at a stopping point.

The rest still exists. I just don’t look at it while I work. I look at items one at a time, and the only question on the screen is “this, or next?”

Structure

Structure doesn’t mean more fields, tags or priority scales. Those are upkeep, and upkeep is what we have least of. Structure means limits.

A few lists, not one pile. A handful of broad areas with a flat list under each, so every item has one place.

An order that means something. The top of a list is what matters most. Then nothing has to be decided again each time I look.

A cap on what’s started. No more than three things in progress. To open a fourth, I finish one or drop one.

A day that can be finished. At most three important things I can complete today, each with a length of time on it. A short list ends, and ending it is the payoff the long list never gave.

Two lists

So we need two lists, not one.

The first is for today. It’s short, three items at most, it holds only things that are important, and all of it can be completed today. It’s best picked the night before. Picking means looking at everything, and looking at everything is the overwhelming part. Done the night before, that’s over before the day begins, and the morning starts with the short list, not the long one.

This list is physical. I use sticky notes, never an app. A sticky note is small, so the list stays short. It sits where I can see it without opening anything. I can recreate it whenever: if the day changes, I write a new one. And when the day is done, it goes in the bin.

The second holds everything. It’s storage: the place things stay so they aren’t lost. I don’t work from it, and I touch it only on weekends. During the week I pick from it and leave it alone.

The inbox is part of either list. New things need somewhere to land, and a third list is one more place to forget. So a new item goes on today’s note or at the end of the big list, whichever is closer.

A traditional to-do list is one list doing both jobs, so the working list is as endless as the storage. We don’t need a longer list or more willpower. We need less in front of us, and limits that hold. Two lists is the minimum system.

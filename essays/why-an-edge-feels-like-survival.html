Why an Edge Feels Like Survival

November 2026. 290 words.

Why do I care about having an edge over others? Not out of vanity. For most of human history, rank in the group decided survival: who ate first, who was protected, who found a mate. The brain still treats relative position as a survival issue, the same old machinery as in The Need Is Built In.

The evidence

People give up money for rank. In a well-known survey, people chose between two worlds: in one they earn $50,000 and others earn $25,000; in the other they earn $100,000 and others earn $200,000. About half chose the first, half the money but ahead.

Rank reaches the body. The Whitehall studies of British civil servants found that men at the bottom of the office hierarchy had about four times the risk of death of the administrators at the top, though none of them were poor. Michael Marmot, who led the studies, named a sense of control over your life as one of the biggest factors in that gap. Often what an edge really buys is control.

No enough

The problem is that an edge is relative. Someone is always ahead, and every gain is someone else’s loss. So it can’t reach enough, which is why securing never feels finished (Power Is a Means).

What’s underneath

It helps to ask what the urge is for. Usually one of three things:

  • Security: fear of falling behind.
  • Significance: wanting to be seen and respected.
  • Mastery: wanting to be excellent at something.

The first two depend on other people’s position. Mastery can be measured against your past self. And if control is what you need, you can often build it directly, without beating anyone. That’s where Happiness Can Be Designed, Partly picks up.

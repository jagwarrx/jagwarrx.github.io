The Art of Defense

November 2026. 777 words.

This is the defensive reading of Greene from Understand Power as Defense, Lean Towards Mastery. Defending against manipulation and power plays comes down to five things: secure your position, learn the tactics in advance, slow decisions down, call out the tactic when it’s used, and stay trusting. The surprising part is that suspicion and trying to read people don’t protect you much. Most of the defense is done beforehand, not in the moment.

The first, and the one that does the most work, has its own note: Secure Your Position First. This one is the other four.

1. Learn the tactics in advance

Inoculation theory holds that meeting weak versions of an argument builds resistance to stronger ones later. Prebunking does this for manipulation: it warns people it’s coming and shows how it works on harmless examples. One finding stands out: resistance training worked best when it convinced people that the source meant to manipulate them and that they personally were vulnerable. Believing you can’t be fooled is the biggest weak spot.

The tactics most worth learning:

  • Cialdini’s six levers: consistency, reciprocation, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity. When a gift turns out to be a tactic, relabel it as one and the pull to repay goes away. When your gut says you’d be a fool to stay consistent with an earlier yes, say so out loud.
  • DARVO: deny, attack, reverse victim and offender. Bystanders who don’t know the pattern get swayed by it; teaching it reduces the harm.
  • Gaslighting: abuse aimed at making you seem or feel crazy. People often describe an uneasy feeling in the stomach that something isn’t right. Trust that signal.
  • Hot and cold: it works like a slot machine. When rewards are rare and unpredictable, the brain doesn’t let go; it tries harder.

2. Rely on process, not on reading people

Across 206 studies, people told lies from truths 54% of the time, barely better than a coin flip. Your own state is a bigger risk than your skill at reading faces: in a Stanford lab study, excitement and anger made people more likely to buy falsely advertised items. The FTC calls urgency the giveaway; honest businesses give you time. Scammers also try to cut you off from anyone who might slow you down. So, four rules:

  • Don’t decide while upset or excited.
  • Don’t decide alone.
  • Don’t decide on their deadline.
  • Verify through a channel you choose, like calling your bank’s number yourself.

3. When it happens, name it instead of copying it

Getting to Yes gives three steps: recognize the tactic, raise it explicitly, and question whether it’s legitimate. Simply asking about a tactic is often enough to stop it. Question the tactic, not the person. Aikido does the same physically: Ueshiba wanted an art that defends you while keeping the attacker from harm. You blend with the force and redirect it instead of meeting it head-on. Blending isn’t giving way.

4. Stay trusting, because trust protects you

Toshio Yamagishi found that people who trust more readily were more sensitive to signs that someone was untrustworthy, and better at predicting how others would act. In another study, high trusters were better at detecting lies. Distrust is a loop: you avoid risky interactions, so you never build the social intelligence to handle them, so you stay vulnerable. The cynic is less safe, not more.

Beforehand, not in the moment

Four of the five are done before the moment: the position, the tactics, the rules, the habit of trust. Only naming the tactic happens during it. That’s the order in The Levers on How You Respond: the earlier the lever, the better it works and the less it costs. In the moment you’re at your worst, aroused, rushed and alone, and that’s exactly the state a manipulator tries to put you in. So the response has to be fixed in advance, as in Know How You’ll Meet It.

The same defense as against bullshit

A manipulator and a bullshitter care about the same thing: the effect on you, not the truth. So the defense is the same one as in Avoid, Detect, Remove Bullshit with ADHD. Vigilance doesn’t hold up, so the defense has to be fast, written down and run at a calm time. Don’t decide when the need is at its peak there; don’t decide while upset or on their deadline here. Avoid is the secure position, detect is knowing the tactics, remove is naming it and walking away.

That’s how to read the 48 Laws defensively, and it’s “wise as serpents, harmless as doves” put into practice (The Serpent and the Dove).

Suffering = Pain × Resistance

November 2026. 1,500 words.

The most useful single line I’ve found in meditation writing is a formula. Shinzen Young, in The Science of Enlightenment, puts it this way: suffering equals pain multiplied by resistance.

It looks too simple to be interesting. But it has a consequence that is genuinely strange. If it’s a product, then as resistance goes to zero, suffering goes to zero, whatever the pain. This essay is about what that would actually mean, and what it wouldn’t.

The formula

Pain here means any unpleasant experience: a sore back, grief, fear, boredom, the itch of not knowing what comes next. Resistance means the fight with it: the bracing, the pushing away, the insistence that it stop. Suffering is what you actually live through.

The idea is old. In a short discourse called the Sallatha Sutta, the Buddha compares pain to being struck by an arrow. An untrained person, he says, is then struck by a second arrow: their own distress about the first. The first arrow is pain. The second is resistance. Shinzen’s contribution is to make the relationship precise, and to say it multiplies rather than adds. A little pain with a lot of resistance can be worse than a lot of pain met cleanly.

Two dials

The formula has two dials, and that turns out to be a good way to sort every answer to the need.

Most of the market turns the first dial. Painkillers, fixes, forecasts and reassurance all try to make the pain smaller. In the language of these essays, uncertainty is the pain, and a forecast tries to remove it by telling you what comes next.

Buddhism turns the second. It doesn’t promise less pain. It trains less resistance. And seen this way, the need to know what comes next is itself a kind of resistance: the insistence that the gap close. Uncertainty is the first arrow. The need is the second.

The answer from Know How You’ll Meet It sits between them. It doesn’t remove the uncertainty, and it doesn’t ask you to stop minding it. It gives you a move to make while it’s there.

Resistance is a separate dial

If the formula is right, it should be possible to turn resistance down without changing the pain. Pain research suggests it is.

Pain has two measurable parts: how intense it is, and how unpleasant it is. In a study by David Perlman, Richard Davidson and colleagues, experienced meditators given painful heat rated it as just as intense as beginners did, but much less unpleasant. The signal was the same. The fight with it was smaller.

Fadel Zeidan and colleagues found something similar after only four days of training in beginners: during meditation, ratings of pain unpleasantness fell by more than half, and intensity by around 40 percent. And Antoine Lutz and colleagues, scanning expert meditators, found that the difference showed up before the pain arrived. Experts showed less activity in anticipation of a painful stimulus, and their responses faded faster once it came. That is resistance to what comes next, measured.

Why resistance multiplies

Several lines of research show how resistance makes things worse rather than better.

Daniel Wegner asked people not to think of a white bear. They thought of it more, and when they were later allowed to think of it, it came back more often than for people who had never tried to suppress it. Pushing a thought away feeds it. James Gross, in the levers essay, found the same for feelings: hiding an emotion leaves it just as strong inside and adds physical stress on top.

In chronic pain, the fear-avoidance model describes the cycle. Pain leads to fear of pain, fear leads to avoiding movement, avoiding movement leads to weakness and more pain. The resistance becomes a second injury. Acceptance and commitment therapy, developed by Steven Hayes, was built around this: the struggle to control unwanted experience is often what keeps it going.

What resistance at zero is not

This is where the formula is most often misread, so it’s worth being exact.

It isn’t zero pain. The arrow still hits. The meditators in Perlman’s study still felt the heat.

It isn’t numbness. Numbness is suppression, and suppression is a form of resistance: you’re fighting the feeling by shutting it off.

It isn’t liking it. You don’t have to enjoy a sore back to stop fighting it.

It isn’t resignation. Resignation says nothing can be done. Zero resistance says nothing about what can be done. It’s about how you meet the sensation, not whether you act.

And it isn’t zero preference. You can prefer that the pain stop, and act to make it stop, without fighting the sensation while it’s here.

What it is

What’s left, once those are cleared away, is something quite specific. Full contact with the experience, with no fight against it. The sensation is allowed to be as big as it is, to move and change and pass in its own time. Shinzen’s word for this is equanimity, and he’s careful to say it isn’t indifference. It’s closer to not interfering.

The surprising claim, and the one the pain studies support, is that this changes the experience itself. Pain met without resistance tends to feel different: less solid, more like a flow of changing sensations, and easier to bear. Shinzen pairs his first formula with a second, about pain met with equanimity doing something useful rather than only hurting. Whether or not that second claim holds in full, the first is clear: the same arrow, met differently, is a different experience.

And it changes action. Resistance distorts response. A person fighting their own panic makes worse decisions than one who can feel the panic and still look at the situation. Gary Klein’s fire commanders could recognize the right move because they weren’t spending their attention on the fight. Low resistance doesn’t mean no response. It means a cleaner one.

The paradox

There’s a trap in the formula, and it’s the same trap as in Buddhism Through the Lens of the Need. If you try hard to get resistance to zero, the trying is resistance. You end up fighting your own fighting.

The way out is to apply the formula to itself. Meet the resistance, too, without resistance. Notice that you’re bracing, and let the bracing be there. It sounds like a riddle, but it’s practical. You don’t have to have reached equanimity to begin. You only have to stop requiring it of yourself.

Does it kill the drive?

A natural worry, especially for anyone who has used stress to get things done, is that resistance is what makes you move. Take it away, and why would you do anything?

In Dealing with Anxiety When You Have ADHD I described anxiety as an engine: for many ADHD brains, urgency is the only thing that starts the work, so they keep manufacturing urgency. That engine runs on resistance. It works, and it costs a great deal.

The formula suggests the drive doesn’t need to come from there. Preference survives at zero resistance, and so does purpose. What goes is the thrashing. The answer from the anxiety essay applies: replace urgency with external structure, a fixed start, a timer, a person waiting. Then act from purpose, and let the discomfort of starting be there without fighting it. The work still gets done. It costs less.

Can it reach zero?

Probably not, and it doesn’t need to. The meditators in these studies reduced resistance a lot, not completely. Shinzen describes enlightenment as a stable, deep shift in this direction, but that claim rests on reports, not measurements.

It also comes with a warning. Willoughby Britton and Jared Lindahl, studying meditators who ran into trouble, documented that intensive practice can sometimes bring up fear, disorientation or old trauma, and that these effects aren’t always brief. Turning toward pain is powerful, which is also why it should be done gradually, and with a teacher or clinician if the pain is large.

And some pain is a signal. Resistance at zero means not fighting the sensation. It doesn’t mean ignoring what the sensation is telling you. A broken bone needs a doctor. An injustice needs a response. You can meet the anger fully and still act on it.

The short version

Suffering is pain multiplied by resistance. Most of the market tries to shrink the pain. Buddhism trains down the resistance. And the need to know what comes next is resistance to uncertainty: the second arrow.

Resistance at zero isn’t zero pain, numbness, liking it, resignation or giving up preferences. It’s full contact with no fight, which changes how the pain feels and makes your response cleaner. You won’t reach zero, and trying hard to will only add resistance. But every bit you take off the second arrow comes off the suffering, whatever the first arrow was.

Not knowing what comes next is the pain. You can’t always change it. You can stop fighting it.

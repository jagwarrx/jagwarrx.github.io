Every Rep Makes the Next One Easier

November 2026. 626 words.

Superstitions are self-perpetuating, so it’s better to nip them in the bud. Same with bad habits. Every rep makes the next one easier.

This holds for both kinds of habit: mental (a thought, a belief, a superstition) and behavioral (an action). And the two feed each other.

Why they keep themselves going

A superstition protects itself. I knock on wood, nothing bad happens, and the knock gets the credit. Because I always do it, I never find out what happens when I don’t. The behavior stops the test that would disprove the belief.

A habit lowers its own cost. Each rep strengthens the link between the cue and the action. The first time takes a decision. The hundredth runs on its own.

A mental habit is a habit too. Catastrophizing, “I always mess this up,” checking whether something is wrong: each time the thought runs, it gets a little more automatic and feels a little more true.

Each feeds the other. The belief makes the action feel needed. The action makes the belief feel confirmed. Behavior can even drag the belief back: when what I do and what I believe disagree, the mind often fixes it by changing the belief, not the behavior.

So early is cheap. A new superstition or habit has had few reps. Stop it then, and there’s little to undo.

Knowing isn’t enough

The hard case: I know the belief is wrong, and I still act on it. I know the plane is safe and my hands still sweat. I know skipping the ritual changes nothing and I still do it.

The philosopher Tamar Gendler has a word for this: an alief. A belief is what I’d say is true. An alief is what my body and habits act as if is true. They can disagree, and when they do, the alief runs the behavior.

This is why arguing with myself doesn’t work. A belief changes with reasons. An alief changes with experience. It learned from reps, and it unlearns from reps.

Ways to change it

  • Run the test the habit prevents. Skip the ritual once, on purpose. Write down beforehand what I expect, and check afterwards. In therapy this is a behavioral experiment.
  • Do the trigger, don’t do the response. Exposure with response prevention: feel the pull, don’t act on it, and let it rise and fall. The alief learns that nothing happened.
  • Make the mismatch vivid. Change sticks best when the old belief is awake and the experience clearly contradicts it. Notice the prediction (“something bad will happen”) in the moment, then see it fail.
  • Replace, don’t just remove. The cue will still come. Give it a new response that fills the same slot: when this, do that.
  • Find what it does for me. Most superstitions buy a sense of control. Most bad habits buy relief. If the need goes unmet, the habit comes back. Meet it with something real.
  • Name it when it fires. “That’s the alief.” Noticing it puts a gap between the cue and the action.
  • Add friction. Change the environment so the old rep is harder and the new one is easier.
  • Count the reps the other way. Every skip is a rep too. Counting them makes the slow progress visible.
  • Act first, believe later. Behavior shapes belief. Doing the new thing a few times often changes the alief faster than thinking about it.

What to expect

The pull doesn’t vanish after one rep. It gets weaker with each one, the same way it got stronger. A slip isn’t a reset: it’s one rep in the old direction against many in the new one.

And under stress, the old one comes back first. That isn’t failure. It just means the next rep matters.

Notes

  1. On superstition: Skinner, “‘Superstition’ in the Pigeon,” Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1948. Pigeons fed at random times repeated whatever they happened to be doing when the food came.
  2. On belief changing to match behavior: Festinger, A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance, 1957.
  3. On alief: Gendler, “Alief and Belief,” Journal of Philosophy, 2008.
  4. On unlearning fear: Craske et al., “Maximizing exposure therapy: an inhibitory learning approach,” Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2014.

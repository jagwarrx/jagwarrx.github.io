The Serpent and the Dove

November 2026. 312 words.

“Be ye therefore wise as serpents, and harmless as doves.” I used the line in Religion as a Collective of the Higher Self for goodwill with discernment. It deserves its own note, because it packs the whole idea into two animals: understanding the game fully while not playing dirty.

The context

In Matthew 10, Jesus is sending his twelve disciples out to teach, and warning them they’ll meet hostility and persecution. The verse opens: “I send you forth as sheep in the midst of wolves.” That admits they’re vulnerable. The instruction is about how to survive among wolves without becoming one.

The serpent

In the ancient world the serpent stood for shrewdness. Genesis calls it the most cunning of the animals. The Greek word in Matthew, phronimoi, means prudent, perceptive, practically wise. This is the part that reads people, sees danger coming, picks its timing and doesn’t walk into traps. It understands the game, including the power games in Power Is a Means and Why an Edge Feels Like Survival.

The dove

The Greek word for the dove’s quality, akeraioi, literally means unmixed. It was used for undiluted wine and pure metal. It points to someone with no hidden agenda and clean motives, who means no harm.

Why both

Each one fails without the other.

  • A serpent without the dove becomes a manipulator.
  • A dove without the serpent gets eaten.

With both, you can see clearly how the world works, power games included, without letting that knowledge corrupt your intentions. It’s a barbell, as in Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know: open goodwill on one side, sharp sight on the other, and nothing murky in between. And it’s another way to be in the world but not of it (How to Let the Rest Play Out): know the game fully, don’t play it dirty.

Change the Environment to Change the Actions

November 2026. 631 words.

If constraints drive actions, then trying to change an action directly is working at the wrong level. You can force it once. You can’t force it every day. The thing that lasts is changing what creates the constraint: the environment around you, and the situation you’re in.

So the chain runs one step further back. Change the environment and the situation, which changes the constraints, which changes the actions.

What I missed for a long time is that these levers work on very different time scales, and using the wrong one is as bad as using none.

Minutes: what’s in front of you

The fastest lever is what you can see and reach right now. The phone in another room. One tab open. Today’s note on the screen when the laptop opens. The first step of the task already written at the top of the page.

These are cheap, they’re reversible, and you know within minutes whether they worked. For a brain where out of sight is out of existence, this is the lever that matters most, because whatever is in view becomes the obvious next thing.

Days: where and with whom

A little slower: where you work, when, and who’s around. A café instead of the desk. A block on the calendar that says what it’s for, so the empty time isn’t free for meetings. In his talk How to Win, Daniel Gross describes a team that sat with their monitors facing each other. Nobody was really watching, but “there’s a weird accountability that gets enforced when you do that.”

These cost a little more to change and take a few days to judge. They’re also the cheapest source of novelty I know. A new place changes the day without touching anything that has to last.

Weeks and months: the system

Slower again: the system itself. What’s in the list, what the defaults are, what the week looks like, which projects are active this quarter. Changing a default here changes thousands of small actions after it, because most of the day runs on defaults.

It also takes weeks before you can tell whether a change worked. That’s why the store should rarely move, and why a week is the shortest fair test for anything at this level.

Years: the situation

The slowest levers are the situation: the job, the city, the people you see every week, what you’ve committed to. They set the constraints that everything else works inside. They’re also expensive, hard to reverse, and slow to show results. A move takes a year to judge.

These are the levers to pull rarely and deliberately, after the faster ones have had a real chance.

Matching the lever to the problem

The mistake is using a lever from the wrong time scale.

Use a slow lever for a fast problem, and you pay a large cost for something a cheap change would have fixed. That was my years of switching to-do apps. I needed novelty, a minutes-or-days problem, and I kept answering it with a months-scale lever: moving the whole system. A new café would have done it.

Use fast levers for a slow problem, and you get the opposite: an endless supply of tricks, each of which works for a week, on top of a situation that’s simply wrong. No arrangement of tabs fixes a job that doesn’t fit.

So when an action I want keeps not happening, I go through the scales in order. What’s in front of me? Where am I, and with whom? What does the system make the default? And only then: is the situation itself the constraint?

Start fast and cheap. Move slower only when the faster levers have stopped working. Most of the time, the fix is closer than it feels.

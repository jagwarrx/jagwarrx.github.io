What Each Religion Sells

November 2026. 1,990 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. In The Need Is Built In I ended on a line I keep returning to: priests, astrologers and apps don’t create the need. They sell into it.

If that’s right, then every answer to the need can be read like a product. It names a problem, promises a solution, tells you how to get there, points to someone it worked for, and has fine print. This essay reads the world’s major religions that way.

A word on “product” first, because it can sound like an accusation. It isn’t one. In Why Belief Systems Survive I concluded that the priests weren’t running a racket. A product can be good. Bread is a product. The question isn’t whether something is sold. It’s whether it meets the need honestly, and what it costs.

The Product

The starting point is Stephen Prothero’s God Is Not One. Against the popular idea that all religions are different paths up the same mountain, Prothero argues they’re climbing different mountains. Each one asks a different question. Christianity starts from sin, Buddhism from suffering, Confucianism from social chaos. To compare them, he gives each four parts: a problem, a solution, a technique for getting from one to the other, and exemplars who did it.

I’ve added a fifth, and I call the whole frame The Product. Five questions you can ask of any seller.

The problem: what uncertainty does it name? The solution: what does it promise? The technique: how do you get there? The exemplar: who did it? And the fine print: what can’t it see, and what does it make hard to say?

The fifth is the one sellers leave out, and it’s the one that tells you the most.

Three ways to answer

Run the major religions through the first four questions and they sort into three families, depending on how they meet the need.

Some forecast the outcome: they tell you what comes next. Some fix the response: they tell you how to meet whatever comes, the answer from Know How You’ll Meet It. And two do something stranger. They loosen the need itself.

Every religion does all three in practice. The families show where each puts its weight. Prothero supplies the problems and solutions; the mapping onto the need is mine.

Religions that forecast

Yoruba religion, from West Africa and now practiced across the Americas, names disconnection as the problem: being out of step with your destiny, which in Yoruba thought your orí, your inner head, chose before birth. The solution is connection. The technique is divination, above all the Ifá system, in which a diviner reads which of hundreds of verses applies to you, and sacrifice to adjust what’s coming. The exemplar is the diviner. It is the most direct product for the need there is: it tells you what comes next, and what to do about it. The fine print is that misfortune always has a spiritual cause, which can delay the search for a practical one. What gets suppressed is chance. Nothing is simply random. The inside correction is ìwà pẹ̀lẹ́, gentle character, which the tradition treats as mattering as much as any ritual.

Christianity names sin as the problem and salvation as the solution. The technique is faith, grace and the sacraments; the exemplars are the saints. What it sells is certainty about the largest unknown, what happens after death, guaranteed by grace rather than earned. That’s an enormous comfort, and it comes with a strong claim about the worth of every person. The fine print is that the present can become a waiting room, and guilt can become the price of grace. What gets suppressed is doubt: when faith is the virtue, doubt looks like failure. The inside corrections are old and strong. Job argues with God. The Psalms of lament complain to him. Thomas doubts and isn’t thrown out.

A religion that does both

Hinduism names saṃsāra, the cycle of rebirth, as the problem and mokṣa, release from it, as the solution. It offers three techniques, the paths of action, knowledge and devotion, and its exemplars are sages, gurus and devotees. It forecasts and fixes the response at once. Karma explains what comes next, which, as I found in the belief-systems essay, is why the astrological chart is supposed to work. And the Bhagavad Gītā turns the same idea into a response: you have a right to your action, never to its fruits.

The fine print is the gravest in this essay. If suffering follows from past action, then those who suffer can be read as deserving it, and that reading helped hold caste in place for centuries. The blind spot is injustice built into a society, explained as one person’s past life. What gets suppressed is the voice from the bottom. The inside corrections came from there: the devotional poet-saints, many from the lowest castes, like Ravidas; the reformer Basava; and B. R. Ambedkar, who in 1956 left Hinduism for Buddhism with hundreds of thousands of followers.

Religions that fix the response

Islam names pride as the problem: the self-sufficiency that forgets God. The solution is submission, which is what the word islām means. The technique is the Five Pillars, including prayer five times a day, and the exemplar is the Prophet. What it sells is clarity about whose future it is. The outcome belongs to God, which is what inshallah says. Your part is the response, and the response comes with a daily structure. Everyone in the prayer line stands shoulder to shoulder. The fine print is that surrender can slide into fatalism, and interpretation can harden around authority. What gets suppressed is fresh reinterpretation, in the old claim that “the gate of ijtihād,” independent legal reasoning, closed centuries ago, though the historian Wael Hallaq has argued it never did. The inside correction is a single saying: trust in God, but tie your camel.

Judaism names exile as the problem and return as the solution. The technique is the law and the story: the commandments, and the memory told and retold at every Passover. The exemplars are the sages who studied and argued. What it sells is a sense of where history is going, and a response for almost every situation, 613 commandments by the traditional count. That’s the opposite of the small card in How Many Responses You Need, and it works because a community and daily study keep it alive. The fine print is that the form of the law can run ahead of its purpose. What gets suppressed is, unusually, not doubt. Argument is sacred: the Talmud records the opinions that lost alongside the ones that won. What was suppressed for most of its history was women’s voices in study and law, which modern movements have been changing. The inside correction is the prophets: “I desire mercy, not sacrifice.”

Confucianism names chaos as the problem: a society where no one knows what anyone else will do. The solution is harmony. The technique is ritual propriety, lǐ, and learning; the exemplar is the jūnzǐ, the person of noble character. What it sells is the answer to social uncertainty. If everyone knows their role, every interaction is predictable. Its gifts are real: education, an exam system that rewarded merit, a deep sense of duty to family. The fine print is hierarchy. In each of the five relationships one side defers. What gets suppressed is dissent, the young, women, under the later doctrine of the three obediences, and private feeling. The inside correction is Mencius, who taught that the people matter most and that a tyrant has lost the right to rule.

Religions that loosen the need

Daoism names lifelessness as the problem, a life that’s brittle and forced, and flourishing as the solution. The technique is wú wéi, acting without forcing, like water that wins by yielding; the exemplar is the sage. It doesn’t sell certainty at all. It sells ease with not knowing, which makes it the closest of all of these to improv. The fine print is passivity: flowing with what is can mean flowing with whoever holds power. What gets suppressed is striving and structure. Religious Daoism, oddly, also had the opposite excess. Its search for an elixir of immortality produced compounds of mercury and lead, and several Tang emperors are thought to have died of them.

Buddhism names suffering as the problem and awakening as the solution. The technique is the Eightfold Path; the exemplars are the awakened, and in the Mahāyāna the bodhisattvas, who delay their own release to help others. It’s the only product here that names the need itself as the problem. Suffering, the Buddha taught, comes from clinging, and clinging to certainty is a kind of clinging. In Kruglanski’s terms from The Why Behind the Need, it goes straight at seizing and freezing. The fine print is that detachment can become disengagement, what the psychologist John Welwood called spiritual bypassing. What gets suppressed is desire, grief and anger, when they’re filed away as defilements instead of read as signals. The inside corrections are the bodhisattva ideal itself, and Engaged Buddhism, which took the practice into the world.

A coda on reason

Prothero ends with atheism, and it fits the frame too. The problem it names is religion, or more charitably, error. The solution is reason; the technique is evidence; the exemplar is the scientist. What it sells is the forecast answer done honestly: predictions that can fail, and are corrected when they do.

Its fine print is the one most relevant to these essays. It assumes the need leaves when the belief does. It doesn’t. The need is built in, so when the old answers go, it gets met by politics, wellness and productivity systems instead. What gets suppressed is the non-rational part of the need: ritual, awe, grief, belonging. Some secular humanists have seen this and started building rituals of their own.

Reading the fine print

Put the fine print side by side and a pattern appears. Each religion suppresses whatever threatens its own answer.

The forecasting religions suppress chance and doubt, because the forecast has to hold. The response religions suppress dissent and the individual, because a shared response only works if everyone keeps it. The loosening religions suppress desire and engagement, because wanting is the problem. And reason suppresses the need itself, because it assumes the need is gone.

The other pattern is that every one of them carries its own critics. Job, Mencius, the camel saying, the prophets, the poet-saints, Zhuangzi mocking the other Daoists. In the belief-systems essay I borrowed Imre Lakatos’s term for this: a protective belt, the outer layer that bends so the core doesn’t break. The traditions that lasted are the ones that kept their critics inside.

The limits

The frame has limits, and they’re worth naming. The problem-and-solution shape comes from the idea of salvation, which fits Christianity best and bends the others to fit. It flattens each tradition: “Hinduism is the way of devotion” leaves out the Mīmāṃsā school I wrote about, which treated ritual as duty, not as a solution to anything. And one paragraph can’t hold a religion. These are tendencies of emphasis, not verdicts on anyone who believes.

Any seller

The reason to name the frame is that it works on more than religion. Try it on a productivity app. The problem: chaos. The solution: a clear mind and an empty inbox. The technique: the method, with its weekly review. The exemplar: the founder’s own life. And the fine print: it can’t see the day you’re too tired to keep it up, and it makes it hard to say “I don’t want to do this at all.”

Five questions. What’s the problem, what’s the promise, how does it work, who did it, and what’s in the fine print? The first four are on the label. The fifth is the one to read.

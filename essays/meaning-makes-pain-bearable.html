Meaning Makes Pain Bearable

November 2026. 1,260 words.

Meaning turns pain into a cost paid for something. The pain is the same. What changes is what it’s for.

That’s one of the oldest observations about human beings, and Viktor Frankl is the person most associated with it. This essay is about why it’s true, what the evidence says, and where it stops being true.

Frankl

Viktor Frankl was a Viennese psychiatrist who was deported in 1942 and spent nearly three years in Nazi camps, including Theresienstadt, Auschwitz and subcamps of Dachau. His parents, his brother and his wife died. In 1946 he wrote Man’s Search for Meaning, in nine days.

His central observation was that the prisoners who held on longest often had something waiting for them: a child, a person they loved, a piece of work unfinished. Frankl’s own was a manuscript. He’d sewn it into his coat, and it was taken from him on arrival. He spent his time in the camps rebuilding it in his head and on scraps of paper.

He borrowed a line from Nietzsche to sum it up: he who has a why to live can bear almost any how. And he wrote the sentence the book is famous for. Everything can be taken from a person but one thing: the last of the human freedoms, to choose one’s attitude in any given set of circumstances.

He was careful about one thing, and it’s worth being careful too. Meaning didn’t decide who survived. Luck, health and the guards did. Frankl wrote that the best of them did not return. His claim was narrower: that meaning changed how the suffering could be borne, while it lasted.

The grieving doctor

After the war Frankl built a therapy around this, called logotherapy. One case from it shows the idea better than any theory.

An elderly doctor came to him, unable to recover from his wife’s death two years before. Frankl didn’t try to comfort him. He asked a question: what would have happened if you had died first, and she had to survive you? The doctor said it would have been terrible for her. Then, Frankl said, you have spared her that suffering. The price is that you now have to survive and mourn her.

The doctor shook his hand and left. Nothing about his loss had changed. What had changed was what his grief was for. Frankl’s summary: suffering ceases to be suffering, in some way, at the moment it finds a meaning, such as the meaning of a sacrifice.

Three sources

Frankl said meaning comes from three places. From creating a work or doing a deed. From experiencing something or loving someone. And, when neither is possible, from the attitude you take toward suffering you can’t avoid.

The third is the one this essay is about. It’s the last resort, and Frankl insisted it was always available.

Why it works: the formula

In Suffering = Pain × Resistance I wrote about Shinzen Young’s formula. Meaning works on the second dial.

Resistance is the insistence that the pain stop, now, because it shouldn’t be happening. Meaning answers that insistence. If the pain is a cost you’re paying for something you care about, it should be happening. It’s part of the deal. You don’t fight a price you chose. The runner in the last mile, the parent awake at three in the morning, the student before an exam: the pain is real, and it doesn’t multiply, because it isn’t being fought.

Why it works: the need

There’s a second reason, and it goes back to the need. All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next.

Pain without meaning is a dead end. It points nowhere, and you don’t know when or how it ends. That’s uncertainty on top of pain. Meaning gives the pain a place in a story: this is the cost, and this is what it buys. The pain now has a future. The doctor’s grief went from something happening to him to something he was doing for her.

The evidence

The most famous evidence comes from a war. Henry Beecher, an anesthetist with the US Army, noticed at the Anzio beachhead in Italy that badly wounded soldiers often asked for little or no morphine. Later, back home, he compared them with civilian surgical patients with similar wounds. About a third of the soldiers wanted a painkiller. More than four in five of the civilians did. Beecher’s explanation was meaning. For the soldier, the wound meant leaving the battlefield alive. For the civilian, surgery meant disaster.

Modern pain science explains why. Lorimer Moseley and David Butler describe pain as the brain’s output, not the body’s input: its best estimate of how much danger you’re in. Change what the pain means, and you change the estimate. Their trials found that simply explaining how pain works can reduce it in people with chronic pain. That’s the predictive brain again. Pain is a forecast, and meaning revises it.

There’s also evidence at the scale of a whole life. Patrick Hill and Nicholas Turiano followed thousands of American adults for fourteen years, and found that those with a stronger sense of purpose were less likely to die in that time, at every age.

What it isn’t

This is where the idea is most often misused, and Frankl himself drew the line.

It isn’t a reason to stay in pain you could end. Frankl wrote that if suffering is avoidable, the meaningful thing to do is remove its cause, and that unnecessary suffering is masochism, not heroism. Meaning is for the pain you can’t change, not a way to make peace with the pain you can.

It isn’t something you can hand someone else. “Everything happens for a reason,” said to a grieving person, usually hurts. Frankl didn’t tell the doctor what his grief meant. He asked a question and let the doctor find it.

And it can be sold. In The Nation as Religion I wrote about how nations give soldiers’ deaths the meaning of sacrifice. Sometimes that meaning is true. Sometimes it’s used to make people accept pain that serves someone else. When someone offers you a meaning for your suffering, it’s worth asking who benefits.

There’s a related idea, post-traumatic growth, the finding that some people report becoming stronger after trauma. It’s real for some, but the research is mostly based on people’s own reports, and it’s been overstated. Not finding meaning in a loss isn’t a failure.

Small pains

Most pain isn’t a camp or a death. It’s boredom, effort, the discomfort of starting. The same idea applies at that size. A boring task with no why is just pain. The same task connected to something you care about is a cost paid for it. For ADHD brains, where boredom can feel close to physical, this may be one of the most practical uses of the idea: before starting, say in one line what the task is for.

The short version

Meaning turns pain into a cost paid for something. Frankl saw it in the camps and in his patients: a why makes almost any how more bearable. It works on resistance, because you don’t fight a price you chose. And it works on the need, because it gives the pain a place in what comes next.

The soldiers at Anzio and the pain trials say the effect is real. But it’s for pain you can’t avoid, it can’t be handed to someone else, and it can be sold. Find the meaning in what you can’t change. Change what you can.

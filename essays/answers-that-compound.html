Answers That Compound

November 2026. 1,240 words.

In Meeting the Need Yourself I argued that you can make most of the answer to the need at home. Draw the line between what’s up to you and what isn’t. Put the meaning in the act. Build your own library of situations. Join the rest, community and the answer to death, with your eyes open.

It’s good advice, and almost nobody follows it. Most people, most of the time, buy the answer instead. I do too. So the useful question isn’t what the right path is. It’s why the other one is so tempting.

The answer, I think, is that the two paths compound in opposite directions, and the direction only shows up later.

Why buying wins now

In the moment, buying has every advantage.

It’s fast. A bought answer closes the gap today. A self-made one pays off in weeks. Every brain discounts the future, and an ADHD brain discounts it steeply, as I wrote in The ADHD Version of the Need.

It’s certain. A seller offers a firm answer. The answer you make yourself is honest, which means it’s less satisfying: “I don’t know what will happen, but I know how I’ll respond.” Arie Kruglanski’s need for closure doesn’t ask for honesty. It asks for firmness.

It’s new. A new product gives a small hit of novelty. Your own core is boring on purpose. It’s the same card every day.

It comes with people. The bundle comes with a crowd. Making your own can feel lonely.

It moves the blame. If the guru is wrong, it’s the guru’s fault. If your own system fails, it’s yours. Erich Fromm argued in Escape from Freedom, in 1941, that freedom is a burden many people would rather hand to an authority. A bought answer is a small escape from freedom.

And it’s made by professionals. As I wrote in The Market for the Need, a seller facing millions of buyers can test what works on the average. You’re designing for one person, with no test group, while you’re the person who needs it.

Two cost curves

The two paths have different shapes. Buying is renting: a small cost up front and payments forever. Making is owning: a large cost up front, then almost nothing.

Seen at any single moment, renting looks cheaper. It only loses over time. And the need is felt at single moments.

Compounding up

What makes the gap grow is that both paths compound.

A self-made answer compounds up. Every time you meet a situation with your own response, you add to the library Gary Klein described, the stock of situations you recognize. The next time is cheaper. The response gets faster, and you trust it more. And each answer you can make yourself raises your bargaining power with every seller, because you can wait, compare and walk away.

The sociologist Robert Merton called this pattern in science the Matthew effect, after the verse about those who have being given more. Advantage builds on advantage. It holds for answers too. The more of the need you can meet yourself, the easier the rest becomes.

Compounding down

A bought answer compounds the other way, and there’s clinical evidence for exactly how.

In anxiety disorders, and especially in health anxiety and OCD, one of the best-studied behaviors is reassurance seeking: asking a doctor, a partner or a search engine whether everything is all right. Paul Salkovskis and others showed that reassurance lowers anxiety in the short term and keeps it going in the long term. Each reassurance works for a shorter time than the last, so you need more of it. And each time, you miss the chance to learn that you could have tolerated the uncertainty without it. That’s why the treatment for these conditions involves cutting reassurance down, not giving more.

Reassurance seeking is what buying the answer looks like when it’s measured. The relief is real, and it shrinks. The dependence grows. And you don’t build the skill, because someone else did the work. That’s compounding in reverse: tolerance instead of returns.

Dependencies compound too

This doesn’t mean every dependency compounds down. The ones you choose can compound up.

The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed the same people since 1938. Its current director, Robert Waldinger, sums up its main finding simply: the quality of people’s relationships predicted their health and happiness in later life better than their wealth or their fame. A friendship kept for decades, or a group stayed with for years, gets more valuable with time. Naval Ravikant put it as advice: play long games with long-term people.

So the line isn’t between depending and not depending. It’s between dependencies that build something and dependencies that only relieve something. The first kind compounds up. The second compounds down.

The real challenge

If the math is that clear, why is it so hard? Because of a gap in how we see ourselves.

The economist George Loewenstein calls it the hot-cold empathy gap. When we’re calm, we can’t feel how strong an urge will be. When we’re in the grip of one, we can’t think the way our calm self would. Each state misjudges the other.

That’s the whole problem. The need fires hot: in the moment of not knowing, when the day has fallen apart or the fear is up. But the self-made answer has to be built cold, at a calm time, when there’s no urgency and no reason to bother. You build when you don’t need it, so that it’s there when you can’t build.

And compounding hides its returns. The first gains are small, too small to feel. Saving money is hard for the same reason. The returns that make it worth doing only show up years in, and nothing in the moment says so.

Getting past the gap

A few rules follow.

Decide cold. Set things up the night before, or at the weekend, never in the middle of the need. That’s precommitment, Ulysses tied to the mast before the Sirens, from Meeting the Need with ADHD.

Make the first version tiny. One card, one default response. A small core you’ll actually build beats a complete one you won’t.

Buy to learn, not to lean. Sellers aren’t the enemy. A course, a book or a coach can teach you a technique. The question is whether you keep paying for it or learn it and leave. A good purchase makes the next purchase less necessary.

Notice when you’re asking for reassurance. Is this a question I need answered, or relief I want? If it’s relief, wait a little before asking. The waiting is where the skill is built.

And keep one or two chosen dependencies for the parts that can’t be made alone. Those are the ones worth investing in for decades.

The short version

Buying the answer is renting. Making it is owning. In any single moment, renting wins, and the need is felt in single moments. But the two paths compound in opposite directions. Made answers build a library, get cheaper, and raise your power with every seller. Bought answers wear off faster each time, and leave you needing more. Chosen relationships compound up. Relief-only dependencies compound down.

The hard part is that you have to build cold for a need that fires hot, and the returns are invisible at first. So build small, build when calm, and choose the answers that compound.

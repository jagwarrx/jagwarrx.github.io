Dealing with Anxiety When You Have ADHD

November 2026. 1,380 words.

Anxiety and ADHD travel together. In a large American survey, about half of adults with ADHD had had an anxiety disorder at some point in their lives. That’s far above the rate for everyone else.

The useful part is why. A lot of the anxiety in ADHD isn’t a second, separate condition that happens to come along. It’s secondary: it grows out of what the ADHD traits keep doing to your days. And if you know where it comes from, you know where to push.

This fits the essays before it. In The Need Is Built In, uncertainty hurts because the brain is a prediction machine and uncertainty means its predictions are failing. In The ADHD Version of the Need, the uncertainty that hurts most in ADHD is about the next ten minutes. Anxiety is what that hurt feels like when it doesn’t stop.

This is what I’ve found in the research, not medical advice.

Where it comes from

There are seven pathways, and most people with ADHD will recognize several.

Open loops. Unfinished tasks stay active in the mind. Psychologists call it the Zeigarnik effect, after the researcher who noticed that waiters remembered unpaid orders better than paid ones. An ADHD brain starts a lot of things and finishes fewer, so it carries a lot of open loops. The result is a constant hum of “I’m forgetting something,” which is often true.

A history of misses. Years of missed deadlines and forgotten appointments teach you to expect failure. After enough of them, the dread arrives before the task does, and it comes with shame.

Time blindness. If there are only two times, now and not now, then a deadline doesn’t exist until it suddenly does. The day before, “not now” flips to “now” all at once, and that flip is a panic spike.

Anxiety as the engine. This one is sneaky. Because urgency is often the only thing that gets an ADHD brain started, you learn to use stress as fuel. Leave it late, panic, do it. It works well enough that you keep doing it, which means you keep manufacturing the anxiety you’d like to get rid of.

Emotional intensity. Many people with ADHD find that emotions rise faster, go higher and take longer to settle. A review by Philip Shaw and colleagues treats this as a core feature for many, not a side effect. Small things hit hard. The popular term “rejection sensitivity” points at part of this, though it isn’t a formal diagnosis.

The idle mind. An ADHD mind wanders more, and the brain’s idle network doesn’t switch off cleanly. Give it unstructured time and it drifts, often toward worry.

Sleep. A delayed body clock is very common in adults with ADHD, which means late nights, early alarms and chronic short sleep. And sleep loss feeds anxiety directly. Eti Ben Simon and Matthew Walker found that one night without sleep raised anxiety by up to 30 percent.

Two more things are worth separating out. First, ADHD restlessness can feel like anxiety in the body: the same buzz, the same can’t-sit-still. It’s worth asking which it is. Is this worry, or unspent energy? The answer changes what helps. Second, medication. Some people do feel jittery on stimulants, and that’s a conversation for whoever prescribes them. But on average, studies don’t find that stimulants worsen anxiety, and treating the ADHD often reduces it.

In the moment

When anxiety is already up, the tools have to be fast and physical, because a spiking brain doesn’t follow complicated instructions. The order that works is body, then words, then paper, then action.

Body first: the physiological sigh. Two inhales through the nose, the second one topping up the first, then one long exhale through the mouth. One to three of them. The long exhale slows the heart. In a 2023 study by Melis Yilmaz Balban and colleagues, five minutes a day of this kind of breathing improved mood and lowered anxiety more than the same time spent in mindfulness meditation.

Humming works for the same reason. So does chanting. In Why Belief Systems Survive I found that the best-supported effect of a mantra is what it does to breathing: it slows it to about six breaths a minute. You don’t need the metaphysics to use the mechanism.

Then words: name it. “This is anxiety.” Matthew Lieberman and colleagues found that putting a feeling into words dampens the brain’s alarm response. It helps to name the mechanism too. Anxiety is a smoke detector, set to go off too often because missing a real fire used to be fatal. Most of the time it’s toast.

Then paper: write the open loop. Dump everything that’s spinning onto paper, then write one next step for the worst of it. This isn’t just venting. E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister found that making a specific plan for an unfinished goal reduced the intrusive thoughts it caused, even though the goal was still unfinished. The plan closes the loop well enough for the mind to let go.

Then action: shrink the horizon. Set a timer for ten minutes and start the thing you’re dreading, or the smallest piece of it. Avoidance feeds anxiety. Starting, even badly, usually lowers it. If you can’t start, walk for ten minutes instead. Exercise lowers anxiety within minutes.

For the long term

The in-the-moment tools turn the alarm down. The long-term work is making it go off less.

Treat the ADHD. If much of the anxiety grows out of the traits, then managing the traits shrinks it. That’s a conversation with a prescriber, and often the single biggest lever.

Get therapy that’s built for ADHD. Standard advice for anxiety assumes you’ll keep a worksheet going for weeks. Cognitive behavioral therapy adapted for adult ADHD, which focuses on organizing, planning and the thoughts that come with repeated misses, has trial support. Steven Safren and colleagues found it helped adults who were already on medication.

Close loops by design. This is where the systems essays come back in. One store for everything, and a short list picked the night before, as in The Minimum System for ADHD, mean fewer open loops and no “what now?” in the morning. Structure isn’t just for productivity. It calms anxiety.

Replace urgency as the engine. If panic is the only thing that starts you, you’ll keep making panic. The replacements are external: a fixed start time, a timer, an artificial deadline with a real person attached to it. Meeting the Need with ADHD goes through these in more detail.

Anchor sleep. The same wake time every day, morning light, and a dim evening do more for a delayed body clock than a fixed bedtime you won’t keep.

Exercise regularly. It’s one of the few things that helps both ADHD and anxiety.

Drop the shame. A missed deadline isn’t proof of a flaw in your character. It’s a fault in the design. As I put it in Constraints Drive Actions, treat your traits as constraints to design around, not failings to fix with effort. A fault can be fixed. A flaw can only be dreaded.

Approach in small doses. Avoidance makes the world smaller. The thing you’ve been avoiding gets bigger in your head the longer you avoid it, and it shrinks when you go near it, a little at a time.

And check the inputs: caffeine late in the day, alcohol, and the endless scroll, which is the idle mind with a feed attached.

When to get help

If there are panic attacks, if anxiety is there most days for weeks, if sleep is falling apart, or if the list of things you avoid keeps growing, that’s the point to see a doctor or a therapist. None of the above replaces that.

The short version

Anxiety in ADHD is a smoke detector built for a world of real, brief dangers, wired into a brain that keeps leaving the stove on. In the moment: breathe out long, name it, write the loop and one next step, then start for ten minutes. For the long term: fewer stoves left on. Treat the ADHD, close loops by design, stop using panic as fuel, sleep, move, and treat every miss as a fault to fix rather than a flaw to fear.

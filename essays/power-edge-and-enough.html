Power, Edge and Enough

November 2026. 934 words.

A practical question: is gaining power, and then wielding it properly, the real goal for most people? And if not, why do I care so much about having an edge over others?

Time, fit and position

Three ideas run through Robert Greene’s The 48 Laws of Power: time, fit and position. Timing even gets its own law, the 35th. They line up with what I’ve been writing about.

  • Time: in a dynamic system, the same action succeeds or fails depending on when you take it.
  • Fit: the move has to fit the actual situation, which is Rumelt’s diagnosis from Principles Are Guiding Policy for a Life.
  • Position: placing yourself so that volatility helps you instead of hurting you, which is Taleb’s barbell from Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know.

The trio is old. Mencius ranked them more than two thousand years ago: good timing counts for less than a strong position, and a strong position counts for less than harmony among people. That ranking is already half the answer.

Power is the currency, not the game

If power means the ability to shape your own life and protect what you value, everyone needs some, and almost any goal requires it. The psychiatrist William Glasser listed it as a basic need, next to survival, love, freedom and fun. Greene goes further: you can’t opt out of the game of power, so you may as well play it well.

Bertrand Russell put it best. Power is to social life what energy is to physics: it takes many forms, and each converts into the others. Money is power over resources. Status is power over attention and respect. Fame is reach. That’s why power looks like the goal from a practical angle. You can trade it into almost anything.

As the goal itself, it works badly. Dacher Keltner calls it the power paradox: people gain power through empathy and cooperation, then lose those same qualities once they have it. In one study, people with a higher sense of power felt less distress and less compassion when someone else was suffering. And the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed its subjects since 1938, found that close relationships, more than money or fame, keep people happy through life.

In the stake-and-rope picture, power is the strength of the rope, not the stake. It’s a means, and values decide what it’s for. “Wielding it properly” is the job principles do.

Seven games, and securing

Richard Smoley, in Seven Games of Life and How to Play, names seven games everyone plays at once: survival, love, power, pleasure, courage, creativity, and an optional last one, the Master Game. The term comes from Robert de Ropp’s 1968 book of that name, where it means waking up from ordinary life. Power is a middle game, never finished and never left behind, but meant to serve the higher ones.

The way I split the list: survival, love, power and pleasure are about securing and stabilizing. Having enough, belonging, being able to protect what you care about. Courage, creativity and the Master Game are about going beyond what you’ve secured. Maslow drew a similar line between deficiency needs, which go quiet once they’re met, and growth needs, which get stronger the more you pursue them. Securing is driving the stake. Courage is walking out to the end of the rope. In Taleb’s terms, it’s building the safe side of the barbell so you can afford the bold side.

The trap is that securing never feels finished. There’s always more money to protect you and more power to hold on to, so people spend their whole lives on the first four games.

Why an edge feels so important

For most of human history, rank in the group decided survival: who ate first, who was protected, who found a mate. The brain still treats relative position as a survival issue, the same old machinery as in The Need Is Built In.

The evidence is strong. In a well-known survey, people chose between two worlds: in one they earn $50,000 and others earn $25,000; in the other they earn $100,000 and others earn $200,000. About half chose the first, half the money but ahead. The Whitehall studies of British civil servants found that men at the bottom of the office hierarchy had about four times the risk of death of the administrators at the top, though none of them were poor. Michael Marmot, who led the studies, named a sense of control over your life as one of the biggest factors in that gap. Often what an edge really buys is control.

The problem is that an edge is relative, so it has no “enough.” Someone is always ahead. It helps to ask what’s underneath the urge. Usually it’s one of three things:

  • Security: fear of falling behind.
  • Significance: wanting to be seen and respected.
  • Mastery: wanting to be excellent at something.

Security and significance depend on other people’s position. Mastery can be measured against your past self. And if control is what you need, you can often build it directly, without beating anyone.

The short version

Power is the currency underneath most of life’s games, so some of it is necessary. It can’t buy the games that may matter most: love and friendship, which it erodes; truth, since it surrounds you with people who agree; and health and meaning, which have to be lived. So the principle worth writing down is a definition of enough: the point where securing has done its job and the energy can go to growth.

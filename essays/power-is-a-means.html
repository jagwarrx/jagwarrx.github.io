Power Is a Means

November 2026. 443 words.

A practical question: is gaining power, and then wielding it properly, the real goal for most people? Partly. Power is what you spend to get almost anything else, which is exactly why it makes a bad goal.

Why it looks like the goal

If power means the ability to shape your own life and protect what you value, everyone needs some. The psychiatrist William Glasser listed it as a basic need, next to survival, love, freedom and fun.

Bertrand Russell put it best. Power is to social life what energy is to physics: it takes many forms, and each converts into the others. Money is power over resources. Status is power over attention and respect. Fame is reach. You can trade power into almost anything, so from a practical angle it looks like the thing to collect.

Why it fails as the goal

Dacher Keltner calls it the power paradox. People gain power through empathy and cooperation, then lose those same qualities once they have it. In one study, people with a higher sense of power felt less distress and less compassion when someone else was suffering.

And it can’t buy the things that may matter most. Not love and friendship, which it erodes. Not truth, since it surrounds you with people who agree. Not health or meaning, which have to be lived. The Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed its subjects since 1938, found that close relationships, more than money or fame, keep people happy through life.

Where it sits

Richard Smoley, in Seven Games of Life and How to Play, names seven games everyone plays at once: survival, love, power, pleasure, courage, creativity, and the Master Game, which means waking up. Power is never finished and never left behind, but it’s meant to serve the higher games.

The way I split them: survival, love, power and pleasure are about securing and stabilizing. Courage, creativity and the Master Game are about going beyond what you’ve secured. It’s the safe side of the barbell from Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know, built so you can afford the bold side.

The trap is that securing never feels finished. There’s always more to protect you, so people spend their lives on the first four games.

Enough

In the stake-and-rope picture from Principles Are Guiding Policy for a Life, power is the strength of the rope, not the stake. Values decide what it’s for, and “wielding it properly” is the job of principles. So the principle worth writing down is a definition of enough: the point where securing has done its job and the energy can go to growth.

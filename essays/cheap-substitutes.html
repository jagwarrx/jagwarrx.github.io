Cheap Substitutes for Real Needs

November 2026. 688 words.

In Western Australia, biologists found male jewel beetles trying to mate with discarded beer bottles. The bottles were golden brown and had a row of small bumps near the base that caught the light much like the dimples on a female’s wing covers, only bigger. Biologists call this a supernormal stimulus: an exaggerated version of a cue that draws a stronger response than the real thing it evolved for. A cheap substitute is a beer bottle for a human need.

Three needs, and what imitates them

Self-Determination Theory, which Cal Newport cites, names three needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. The research on daily life points to three related conditions for a good day: connection, presence and progress (Happiness Can Be Designed, Partly). Each era has created a force that competes with these needs, and can imitate them:

  • Consumerism and status pose as competence. Buying and comparing feel like getting ahead, but it’s the relative game with no “enough”, not real mastery (Why an Edge Feels Like Survival).
  • The internet’s attention economy works against presence. Its business depends on pulling your mind away from where you are.
  • AI could pose as relatedness and autonomy. Flattering companionship can stand in for real connection, and relying on it can wear away your own judgment.

Each one offers a cheaper substitute for a real need, so the same framework works for judging new technology. Ask whether a tool builds your autonomy, competence and relatedness, or only imitates them.

Why the substitute wins

Time is where the difference shows up, but it isn’t the root cause. There are four parts to it.

1. Reward now, cost later

Substitutes give you the payoff up front and send the bill later. Real needs work the other way: the cost comes first and the reward comes later. Real goods also compound. Skills, trust and relationships grow over time; substitutes lose value and need bigger doses to give the same effect. Because people naturally overweight the present, the substitute wins in the moment, again and again. It’s the same split as bought and made answers in Answers That Compound.

2. Wanting, not liking

The neuroscientist Kent Berridge found that the brain’s dopamine system drives wanting a reward but isn’t needed for liking it. Substitutes trigger the wanting without delivering much liking, so you crave more without feeling satisfied. A real need, once met, feels like enough. This is the slot-machine pattern of rare, unpredictable rewards, the one behind hot and cold in The Art of Defense.

3. Signal, not substance

Substitutes give you the feeling of a need without the thing itself. Likes feel like connection. Buying feels like getting ahead. An agreeable AI feels like being understood. It’s Goodhart’s law applied to a life: when a measure of something becomes the target, it stops measuring the real thing.

4. Effort is part of what feeds you

The research on the IKEA effect found that people value what they build themselves more, but only when the work ends in success. When participants built and then destroyed their creations, or failed to finish them, the effect went away. The struggle and the finish together produce the value.

The same holds for real needs. Competence needs a skill that can still beat you. Relatedness needs a person who can say no. Presence needs discipline. Substitutes remove the resistance, and without stress you get weaker, Taleb’s point about antifragility (Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know).

A quick test for any habit or tool

  • Does it compound over time, or wear off?
  • Does it leave you satisfied, or wanting more?
  • Is there real resistance in it, like a skill that can fail or a person who can disagree?
  • Would you still choose it if you could see the full long-term cost?
  • Who profits when you keep coming back?

Rest, not the main meal

Substitutes aren’t poison. A snack is fine. Think of the barbell: use substitutes for rest, not as the main meal. And my principle of “enough” is what keeps them in their place (Power Is a Means).

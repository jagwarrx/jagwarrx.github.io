Know How You’ll Meet It

November 2026. 1,120 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. That’s the line I keep coming back to in The Need Is Built In. When we can’t know, we reach for a story, a ritual or someone who claims to know.

While writing about improv in Culture, Entertainment and the Need, I noticed something I hadn’t seen before. You can’t know what comes next, but you can know how you’ll meet it. That’s a different answer to the need.

It’s worth taking seriously, because it may be the only answer that holds up.

Two answers

There are two ways to make the future feel known.

The first is to predict the outcome. That’s the forecast, the horoscope, the detailed plan, the expert on television. It works when the world cooperates. When the world surprises you, it fails all at once, and you’re back in the fog with nothing.

The second is to fix the response. You don’t know what will happen, but you know what you’ll do when it does. That doesn’t depend on the world cooperating. It only depends on you.

Most of what people sell in response to the need is the first kind. Most of what actually works is the second.

Why it works

The brain, in the predictive processing view, is a machine for keeping surprise low. Karl Friston’s theory of active inference adds something important: there are two ways to do that. You can change your model to fit the world, which is what we usually mean by learning. Or you can act on the world to make it fit your model.

Knowing how you’ll meet what comes next is the second route. A large part of any future is your own next action, and that part, at least, you can know. Fix your response and you’ve removed one big source of uncertainty, the one you control, even though the rest stays open.

Psychology points the same way. Albert Bandura found that what predicts fear is less how dangerous a situation is than whether people believe they can cope with it. He called it self-efficacy. And John Bowlby and Mary Ainsworth found that the children who explore most boldly are the ones with a secure base: a parent they know will respond if something goes wrong. The world is no more predictable for them. The response is.

It’s an old idea

Once you see this, you find it everywhere people have had to deal with uncertainty for real.

The Stoics built a philosophy on it. Epictetus opens his handbook by dividing things into what’s up to us and what isn’t. Outcomes aren’t up to us. Our judgments and our responses are.

The Bhagavad Gītā says it in one line that Indian grandmothers still quote: you have a right to your action, never to its fruits. That’s the same move. Stop trying to own the outcome. Own the response.

Armies learned it the hard way. The elder Moltke wrote that no plan of operations survives with certainty past the first encounter with the enemy’s main force. Eisenhower put it as “plans are worthless, but planning is everything.” The planning that survives isn’t the forecast. It’s the preparation of responses. Modern militaries give orders as commander’s intent, the purpose rather than the steps, so that people on the ground can respond to what actually happens.

Pilots and surgeons do it with checklists. A pilot doesn’t predict whether an engine will fail on a given flight. A pilot knows, down to the memorized first steps, what to do if it does.

Engineers have a name for the same distinction. An open-loop system follows a fixed plan and hopes nothing changes. A closed-loop system measures what’s happening and responds. A thermostat doesn’t forecast the weather. It doesn’t need to.

And improv makes a game of it. “Yes, and” is a response fixed in advance. Whatever your partner says, you already know your next move: accept it and build.

Why it suits ADHD

This answer happens to fit an ADHD brain unusually well.

The first answer, predicting the outcome, leans on exactly what ADHD is worst at: holding the future in view, estimating time, planning far ahead. As I wrote in The ADHD Version of the Need, the future arrives faint.

The second answer leans on what ADHD is often good at: being in the moment, reacting fast, improvising. It asks less of the future and more of the present. You don’t have to see far. You only have to know your move when the moment comes.

How to build it

Name the situations that keep recurring. Not every possible future, just the few that come back: the system collapses, the day goes off the rails, anxiety spikes, someone asks for something unexpected.

Write one response for each, in the form “when this happens, I’ll do that.” Peter Gollwitzer calls these implementation intentions, and his work finds that deciding the response in advance makes it far more likely to happen. When the system collapses, I go back to a sticky note with three things on it. When anxiety spikes, I breathe out long, name it, and write one next step, as in Dealing with Anxiety When You Have ADHD.

Practice when calm. Drills exist because a response you’ve only read about isn’t there when you need it. Saying it once out loud, or doing it once on an ordinary day, puts it where a stressed brain can find it.

Keep the intent, not the steps. A response written as a long procedure breaks the first time the situation doesn’t match. A response written as a purpose, “get back to one next step,” bends.

And treat surprise as material. The improv rule is that there are no mistakes, only offers. The unexpected thing is the next thing to respond to, not proof the plan failed.

The limits

This doesn’t replace prediction where prediction works. Check the weather before a hike. Budget before you spend. The point isn’t to stop forecasting. It’s to stop depending on forecasts you can’t make.

And a fixed response can harden into ritual. If the response becomes a rigid script that has to be performed exactly, it’s just the hazard-precaution system again, the one that turns anxiety into compulsion. The answer is the one above: fix the intent, and let the steps move.

The short version

The first answer to the need is a forecast. The second is a practice. The forecast needs the world to cooperate. The practice needs only you.

You can’t know what comes next. You can know how you’ll meet it. That’s a different answer to the need, and it’s the one that still works when the world doesn’t.

The Market for the Need

November 2026. 1,520 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. In What Each Religion Sells I read the major religions as products: each names a problem, promises a solution, and has fine print. That essay looked at the sellers. This one looks at the buyers.

The buyers are the strange part. The need never changes. It’s built in, the same in a hunter-gatherer band as in a city of ten million. But the market for it changes completely, and it has changed more in the last twenty years than in the five hundred before.

Four dials

If the need is constant, what changes is the conditions around it. There are four that matter most.

The first is security: how much uncertainty people actually live with. Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart, comparing surveys across dozens of countries, found that religiosity falls as existential security rises. Where incomes are stable, health care is reliable and old age is provided for, fewer people pray. The reverse holds too. After the 2011 earthquakes in Christchurch, Chris Sibley and Joseph Bulbulia found that religious faith rose among people in the affected areas, against a national trend of decline. More uncertainty, more demand.

The second is the medium: how answers reach people. Speech, then writing, then print, then broadcast, then the feed. Each one changed who could sell and what sold.

The third is scale. Ara Norenzayan argues in Big Gods that gods who watch and judge human behavior appear as societies grow large. In a band of a hundred and fifty, everyone can watch everyone. In a city of strangers, a god who sees everything does the watching instead.

The fourth is competition. Adam Smith noticed it first: in The Wealth of Nations he argued that an established church with no rivals grows lazy, while many small competing sects keep each other vigorous. The sociologists Rodney Stark and Roger Finke built a theory on it, the religious economies model, to explain why religion stayed strong in the United States, with its open market of churches, while it declined in parts of Europe with official ones.

How the market changed

Turn those dials through history and you get a sequence.

In a band, there’s no audience. Everyone dances, everyone sings, and the shaman is a neighbor. The answer to the need is local and shared.

In the first states, the audience becomes subjects. Priests and temples serve a king, and the gods grow bigger and more moral to match the size of the society.

In what Karl Jaspers called the Axial Age, roughly 800 to 200 BCE, something new appears. The Buddha, Confucius, the Hebrew prophets and the Greek philosophers all address the individual: the inner life, a universal ethic, a path anyone can walk. The audience is now a person inside a mass society.

With print, the audience becomes readers. Martin Luther was the first best-selling author of the printing age, and the Reformation was partly a change of medium: once each reader could hold the text, each reader could interpret it, and denominations multiplied.

With broadcast, the audience becomes a mass in the modern sense: one voice, millions of listeners, all receiving the same thing at once. That’s the era of the televangelist and the guru on television, and of simple, large answers.

And with the feed, the audience becomes a mass of one. Everyone receives something different, chosen for them.

Unbundling

The most important thing the feed has done is to unbundle religion.

A religion used to be a bundle. One institution sold, as a single package, community, ritual, meaning, identity, ethics, a calendar, rites of passage, care for the sick and the dying, and a forecast about what comes next. You didn’t choose the parts. You joined, and you got all of them.

The businessman Jim Barksdale once said there are only two ways to make money: bundling and unbundling. The internet has been unbundling everything it touches, and religion is no exception. Each part of the old bundle is now sold separately, by a seller who specializes in it.

Community is sold by gyms, running clubs and CrossFit boxes. Casper ter Kuile and Angie Thurston, in a 2015 report from Harvard Divinity School called How We Gather, found that groups like these were doing much of what congregations once did: weekly gathering, shared effort, someone who notices when you don’t show up. Ritual is sold by wellness: the morning routine, the cold plunge, the candle. Meaning is sold by podcasts and books. Identity is sold by politics and fandom. And the forecast is sold by astrology apps, which have millions of users who check them every morning.

Tara Isabella Burton calls the result Strange Rites. People aren’t leaving religion so much as assembling their own, a piece at a time. In the United States, about three in ten adults now say they have no religion at all. Many of them still meditate, read their horoscope and belong to something that meets every week.

What unbundling costs

Unbundling is usually sold as freedom, and it is freedom. You take what you want and leave the rest. But bundles did things their parts can’t.

A bundle carries parts you wouldn’t buy alone. Nobody shops for obligations to strangers, visits to the sick, or a weekly reminder that they’ll die. Inside a bundle you got them anyway, along with the parts you wanted. Unbundled, they’re the first to go, because nobody can sell them.

A bundle checks itself. Its ethics constrained its forecast, and its community constrained its leaders. Unbundled, each seller optimizes one piece, and no one looks at the whole. The fine print, from the last essay, gets harder to read, because every buyer sees a different label.

And the unbundled parts are tuned for a different goal. As I argued in Culture, Entertainment and the Need, the old forms had endings. A service finishes, a festival ends, and you go home. Products sold through a feed are tuned for engagement, so they open the loop and don’t close it. The need is met just enough to bring you back.

The nature of a mass

There’s one more thing about the buyers, and it explains the whole business.

A mass isn’t a large person. It has properties no individual has. In the 1830s Adolphe Quetelet noticed that while no one could predict whether a given person would commit a crime or marry that year, the totals for a whole country were remarkably stable from year to year. You can’t know what one person will do. You can know what a million will.

That creates an asymmetry. The economist Frank Knight distinguished risk, which can be measured and priced, from uncertainty, which can’t. A single buyer faces uncertainty: they don’t know what will happen to them. The seller, facing a mass of buyers, faces only risk. The seller’s certainty comes from the mass. The buyer’s certainty comes from the seller. Insurance runs on this gap, and so do casinos, and so do astrology apps. The house doesn’t know your future, but it knows the average.

Masses also behave differently from individuals in another way. Gustave Le Bon, in 1895, described crowds as irrational and contagious. Later research, especially by Stephen Reicher, found something more precise: crowds act on a shared identity. They aren’t mad. They’re grouped. That connects back to Michael Hogg’s finding that the more uncertain people feel, the more tightly they hold to a group. A frightened mass is a strongly identified one.

And the mass selects the product. This is the cultural evolution from Why Belief Systems Survive, sped up. A feed shows what spreads, and what spreads isn’t what’s true. Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral studied about 126,000 stories on Twitter and found that false news spread farther and faster than true news, largely because it was more novel. The market rewards the answer that travels, not the one that holds.

Rebundling

Barksdale’s line has a second half. What gets unbundled eventually gets rebundled.

It’s already happening. Some wellness brands now sell community, ritual, meaning and identity together, with a founder as the exemplar. Some political movements sell identity, meaning, community and an enemy in one package, and ask for the loyalty a church once did. And the largest rebundle of the modern age is older than any of these: the nation-state, which took over the schools, the calendar, the rites of passage and the promise of being remembered after death. That’s the next essay.

The short version

The need is constant. The market moves with four dials: how secure people are, how answers reach them, how large the group is, and how many sellers compete. Over history, the unit of the audience has shrunk from the tribe to the kingdom, the reader, the viewer and finally the individual feed. Religion, once a bundle, is now sold in pieces, and the pieces that nobody would buy alone are the ones being lost.

And this essay is part of the market too. It sells into the need like everything else. The Bundle applies here: read the fine print.

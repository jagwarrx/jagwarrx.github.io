Avoid, Detect, Remove Bullshit with ADHD

November 2026. 1,340 words.

In Removing Bullshit I defined bullshit as anything that costs something every day and changes nothing about what you do. That essay was about systems: the priority scale where everything is high, the tags nobody filters by, the weekly review that exists in the template and not in the week.

There’s a second kind, and the philosopher Harry Frankfurt described it. A liar cares about the truth enough to hide it. A bullshitter doesn’t care either way. He cares only about the effect. That’s bullshit in claims rather than in systems.

I’ve come to think both kinds have the same source. In The Need Is Built In I argued that uncertainty hurts, and that the brain reaches for an answer and a ritual to make it stop. The need for an answer produces bullshit claims. The need for relief produces bullshit systems: motion that lowers anxiety and feels like work. Frankfurt put the first half in one sentence: bullshit is unavoidable whenever circumstances require someone to talk without knowing what he is talking about. The need creates those circumstances all the time.

And in an ADHD brain, as I argued in The ADHD Version of the Need, it’s worse in a specific way. It seizes the first answer fast. It mistakes novelty for insight. And reorganizing a list gives exactly the relief that doing the work would, sooner.

The usual defense against bullshit is vigilance: checking claims, reviewing systems, staying careful. That’s the one thing an ADHD brain can’t keep up. So the defense has to be built differently. It has to be fast, written down, run at a calm time, and removal has to happen by default.

There are three stages. Avoid it, detect it, remove it.

Avoid

The cheapest bullshit to deal with is the kind that never gets in.

Don’t decide when the need is at its peak. The worst moment to adopt a new system, tool or belief is the day the old one broke, because that’s when the urge to seize is strongest and judgment is weakest. So I give it forty-eight hours. Whatever looked like the answer on the bad day has to still look like it two days later.

Give the need an honest answer. The need doesn’t actually require the truth. It requires the uncertainty to end. “I don’t know yet,” written down with what exactly I don’t know, ends it surprisingly well. It turns a fog into a list, and a list is something I can work on. A made-up answer feels better for an hour and costs more later.

Write the fault first. Before adding anything to a system, I have to write down what went wrong, and when. If I can’t name a fault, the addition is novelty dressed up as a fix.

Use fewer sources, and calibrated ones. Novelty feeds are where bullshit pours in fastest, because each new claim gives a small hit whether or not it’s true. Philip Tetlock found that the forecasters who sound most confident, and get the most airtime, are less accurate than the cautious ones who say “probably” and keep score. Prefer the ones who keep score.

And write only what I could defend out loud. Writing is where my own bullshit is born. A sentence that sounds right and that I couldn’t explain to a friend without notes doesn’t go in.

Detect

Some will get in anyway. The tests for catching it have to be one question each, because a checklist is another ritual I won’t keep.

For claims, four questions do most of the work. What would prove it wrong? If nothing could, it’s not saying anything. Would it fit anyone? A reading that fits everyone feels written for you; that’s the Barnum effect. Can it be read two ways? Daniel Dennett calls a sentence that’s trivially true on one reading and profound but false on the other a deepity, and they’re everywhere in productivity writing. And who needs me to believe this? Follow the need, and you often find a seller.

It’s worth knowing how easily this works. Gordon Pennycook and colleagues showed people randomly generated sentences of impressive words, like “Hidden meaning transforms unparalleled abstract beauty,” and many rated them profound. The people most taken in were the ones who tend to accept the first answer rather than reflect on it.

For systems, I already have three tests. What would I do differently if this were gone? If nothing, it goes. What exists now that didn’t this morning? If the answer is a reorganized list, the day was spent on the system, not the work. And the one number: minutes per day spent on the system. If it rises, the system is failing, however elegant it looks.

Then there are three signals that are specific to an ADHD brain, because they catch its particular failures.

Relief or progress? If the main thing something gave me was relief, I suspect it. Relief is what the need wants, and bullshit is good at supplying it.

New or better? The glow of something new feels a lot like insight. They’re different, and the only way to tell is to wait until it isn’t new.

Do I know this, or did I just read it? This one is for my own writing. Something read this morning can feel like knowledge by the afternoon. It isn’t yet.

And the tests run at a calm time, not in the middle of the urge. The weekend, when I look at the big list anyway, is when I check. Testing a claim while I’m still feeling the relief it gave me doesn’t work.

Remove

Removing is where ADHD most needs a different design, because the normal method, reviewing everything regularly and pruning what’s stale, is sustained upkeep. So removal has to happen by default.

Let things expire. The sticky note with today’s three things goes in the bin at the end of the day, and nothing on it survives unless I write it again. The same idea works elsewhere. A tag or a field that nothing has used in thirty days gets deleted. If it mattered, it will come back with a reason.

Schedule one cut. Ten minutes at the weekend, and at least one thing goes every week. One is small enough that I’ll actually do it, and over a year it’s fifty things.

Delete, don’t argue. Alberto Brandolini’s law says that refuting bullshit takes an order of magnitude more energy than producing it. That’s why it piles up. The answer isn’t to win the argument. It’s to drop the claim and move on.

Downgrade beliefs rather than fighting them. Where I can’t settle a claim, I mark it unverified. That’s enough to stop it steering anything.

Let an agent flag it. Finding what nothing reads is exactly the kind of tedious scan I won’t keep doing. An agent can list it, and I only have to say yes or no. That’s the same move as Not Tools, but Automation: the ritual runs without my keeping it going.

And cut until it looks too simple. When it’s done, every piece left should be something I’d miss.

The four rules

Everything above follows from four rules, each one a workaround for something ADHD is short of.

Fast: one question, never a checklist. External: on paper or automated, never “I’ll remember to check.” Calm time: detect and cut at the weekend, not in the middle of the urge. Default removal: things expire unless renewed, so nothing depends on vigilance.

None of this removes the need. It can’t, because the need is built in. What it does is give the need honest answers, so the bullshit has less room. That’s the same principle as Meeting the Need with ADHD: meet the need before it reaches for whatever answers fastest.

One last thing. This is the fifth essay I’ve drafted today, on subjects I was reading about this morning. That’s close to the condition Frankfurt describes: writing faster than knowing. So the tests apply here first.

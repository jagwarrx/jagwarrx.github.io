What Is Enlightenment?

November 2026. 1,310 words.

After a day of writing about Buddhism, I realized I’d been using the word enlightenment without being able to say what it meant. Enlightened about what? You get enlightened about a subject, usually. Somebody explains something, and you understand. So what is the subject here? What exactly are you supposed to be getting enlightened about?

I also had a suspicion, which I wrote down earlier: that enlightenment is just a label for satisfying the restlessness. The final reward, the one that finally works. This essay is an attempt to check that.

Two enlightenments

The English word has two famous meanings, and they turn out to be useful together.

The first is the European Enlightenment. In 1784 Immanuel Kant wrote a short essay with the same title as this one. His answer was that enlightenment is our emergence from a self-imposed immaturity: the inability to use our own understanding without someone else’s guidance. His motto was sapere aude, dare to know.

What’s striking is how he described the immaturity. It’s comfortable, he wrote. If I have a book that understands for me, a pastor who has a conscience for me, a doctor who decides my diet for me, I don’t need to think at all. Others will gladly take over the tiresome work. That is, almost word for word, what I called buying the answer in Answers That Compound. Kant’s enlightenment is about the need: stop buying your answers from guardians, and make your own.

The second meaning is the one the word was borrowed to translate, and it points somewhere else entirely.

The original words

The Buddhist word usually translated as enlightenment is bodhi, and it doesn’t mean light. It comes from a root meaning to wake up. The Buddha is the one who woke up. The metaphor isn’t learning something new. It’s coming out of a dream you didn’t know you were in.

The other words around it say the same. Nirvāṇa means blowing out, as a flame goes out. In Zen, kenshō means seeing your own nature. In Hindu traditions, mokṣa means release. Waking, going out, seeing, being let go. None of them means acquiring information.

That’s the first answer to my question. You don’t get enlightened about a subject. Nothing gets added. Something you were doing all along gets seen, and stops.

What gets seen

If the object isn’t new information, what is it? Buddhism has a short list, called the three marks of existence. Impermanence: everything changes. Unsatisfactoriness: nothing conditioned satisfies for long. And not-self: there’s no fixed, separate someone behind experience.

The obvious objection is that I already know all three. I’ve just written essays about them. If I can explain impermanence, haven’t I already been enlightened about it?

Philosophers have a distinction that answers this. Bertrand Russell separated knowledge by description from knowledge by acquaintance: knowing facts about something, versus knowing it directly. Frank Jackson made the point vivid with a thought experiment. Mary is a scientist who knows every physical fact about color, but has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room. One day she walks out and sees a red tomato. Does she learn something? Most people feel she does.

Enlightenment, in the traditional sense, is supposed to be like Mary seeing red. You already know that everything changes. The claim is that you can come to see it, in every moment, in a way that changes how you live. Note Gone, the practice from Bottom-Up Programming for Enlightenment, is a way of walking out of the room a little at a time.

In terms of the need

Put that into the language of these essays and the answer gets specific. What you’re getting enlightened about is the need itself.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. Ordinarily we don’t see the need. We see its objects: the forecast, the plan, the reassurance, the next thing. Waking up, in this sense, is seeing the need as a need. Seeing that the restlessness is the machine running, in the terms of Signal or Feature. Seeing that the someone who needs to know is less solid than it feels. And seeing that nothing will hold still long enough to be finally known.

So enlightenment isn’t an answer to what comes next. It’s seeing that the question was driving you.

Is it just a label for satisfying the restlessness?

Now the suspicion. As it’s often sold, yes. Enlightenment gets marketed as the ultimate reward: permanent bliss, the end of all problems, the thing that will finally be enough. Sold that way, it’s the treadmill’s last and biggest prize, and chasing it is just more chasing.

But the traditions claim the opposite, and the word itself says so. Satisfying the restlessness would be feeding the fire. Nirvāṇa is the fire going out. Not the gap filled, but the demand that it be filled, dropped. That’s why Buddhism warns so hard against craving enlightenment, and why the parable of the raft tells you to put the teaching down once you’re across. If your enlightenment feels like a prize you’ve won, the tradition would say you’ve found a new object for the old restlessness.

So my suspicion holds for the product, and not for the thing the product is named after.

Sudden or gradual

Traditions disagree on how it happens. In the famous Chinese story of the Platform Sutra, two monks compete to express their understanding in a poem. One writes that the mind is a mirror that must be wiped clean, constantly: gradual practice. The other, Huineng, replies that there was never a mirror and nowhere for dust to land: sudden seeing. Huineng wins the robe.

Theravāda Buddhism describes four stages, each one dropping more. Modern teachers like Shinzen Young describe it in operational terms, as a stable shift in how the self is perceived, built from skills you can train. Most accounts agree on one thing: it isn’t a single fireworks moment that settles everything.

What it isn’t

A few things enlightenment is not, by the traditions’ own accounts.

It isn’t the end of pain. Teachers widely described as awakened still get headaches and grieve. In Shinzen’s formula, the pain stays and the resistance drops.

It isn’t knowing everything. Waking up from a dream doesn’t teach you chemistry.

And it isn’t being good. This is the most important one, and the evidence is uncomfortable. Many Buddhist communities, in Asia and in the West, have had scandals involving teachers said to be awakened: abuse of power, money and students. Whatever those teachers saw about experience, it didn’t make them trustworthy. Seeing through the self isn’t the same as caring for others. That’s why the traditions pair insight with ethics and community, and why a technique sold alone, without the community, is risky.

The two together

Here’s where the two enlightenments meet. Kant’s says: stop buying your answers, and dare to think for yourself. The Buddha’s says: see the one who needs the answers, and stop needing them so badly.

They’re the two moves these essays keep coming back to. Make the core yourself, from Meeting the Need Yourself. And loosen the need, from the Buddhism essays. One is enlightenment about the world. The other is enlightenment about the one looking at it.

The short version

You don’t get enlightened about a subject. The original words mean waking up, going out, seeing and release, not learning. What you’re supposed to see is something you already know by description: that everything changes, nothing satisfies for long, and the self is less solid than it feels. In the terms of these essays, you’re getting enlightened about the need itself.

Sold as the final reward, enlightenment is just the restlessness with a new target. As the traditions describe it, it’s the restlessness seen for what it is, and let go.

Compounding in the Real World

November 2026. 696 words.

On paper, compounding is a smooth curve: growth in proportion to what you already have, at a fixed rate, for as long as you wait. Put $100 in at 7% and it doubles in about ten years, then doubles again, and again. In a life it doesn’t look like that. It’s messy, and most of the time you can’t see it.

Most of it comes late

The early part of an exponential curve looks flat. Warren Buffett is the usual example. He bought his first stock at 11, but Morgan Housel worked out that of Buffett’s $84.5 billion, $84.2 billion came after his 50th birthday. The skill was there early; the money showed up decades later. Time did most of the work. For most of the years you’re compounding there is almost nothing to show for it, and that’s when people quit.

How it actually works

  • It’s lumpy. Gains don’t arrive at a rate. They arrive as events: a job offer, a client, an introduction from someone you met years ago. The rate is an average you only see looking back.
  • It’s lagged. The input is today and the result comes years later. The feedback that would keep you going arrives after you’d have stopped.
  • The average lies. Gain 50%, then lose 50%, and you’re at 75, not 100. Losses cost more than equal gains make back, and a big one can take years to repair. In real life the rate swings, and the swings eat into the growth.
  • It runs through other people. Outside a bank account, compounding is mostly selection. A small early edge gets you picked for the next chance, which builds a bigger edge. Robert Merton called this the Matthew effect: well-known scientists get more credit for the same work. In Canadian youth hockey, players born early in the year, a few months older than the others in their age group, were more likely to make the better teams, got more coaching there, and were overrepresented in the top junior leagues and the NHL.
  • It runs into limits. Nothing doubles forever. A skill, a market or a relationship grows along an S-curve and flattens. Compounding goes on only by moving to the next curve, best of all one that builds on the last: a skill that makes your other skills worth more.
  • It runs in reverse too. Debt, neglect, small health costs and a reputation for cutting corners all compound, just as quietly.
  • There’s no statement. Money comes with a balance. Skills, trust, health and knowledge don’t. You can’t see the stock, only what it does now and then.

Why you can’t see it

Put those together. The early years look flat, the noise hides the trend over any short stretch, the results come late and in lumps, and the stock has no number. And when you look at other people you see the end of their curve, not the flat part, so your own flat part looks like failure.

Working with it

  • Track the inputs, not the output. The output lags and is noisy; the inputs are yours and happen every day. A log of what you put in makes the stock visible on paper before it shows anywhere else.
  • Judge it at the right scale. Years, not weeks. Over a week, compounding and doing nothing look the same (How to Let the Rest Play Out).
  • Don’t interrupt it. A big loss costs more than a big gain pays, so avoiding ruin matters more than finding the best rate. That’s the job of Secure Your Position First and of the barbell in Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know.
  • Prefer a boring rate for a long time. A modest rate that keeps running beats a high rate that stops.
  • Pick things that combine. Skills that multiply each other, people who introduce you to people (Build the Network Before You Need It), answers you make rather than buy (Answers That Compound).
  • Know which way it runs. Real goods compound up and substitutes compound down (Cheap Substitutes for Real Needs). The same patience that builds a skill lets a bad habit build too.

  3. Merton, “The Matthew Effect in Science,” Science 159, 1968.
  4. Barnsley, Thompson and Barnsley, “Hockey success and birthdate: The relative age effect,” 1985. Malcolm Gladwell made it well known in Outliers, 2008.

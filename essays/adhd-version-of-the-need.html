The ADHD Version of the Need

November 2026. 1,490 words.

In The Need Is Built In I argued that the need for meaning under uncertainty isn’t something priests or astrologers create. Uncertainty hurts, the brain fills the gap with a story, and anxiety turns into ritual. Evolution built it that way, and imagination made it chronic, because we can fear a lion that isn’t there.

But most of what I write here is about designing around an ADHD brain, and the essays kept pointing at something the last one left out. The need is the same. Its time horizon isn’t.

In ADHD, the distant future pulls less and the next few minutes pull more. So the need reaches less for big meaning and more for fast relief. That one shift explains a lot, including why I built my personal system eighteen times in a year.

Proximate: the future is faint

Start with time. Russell Barkley, who has studied ADHD for decades, describes it as a kind of time blindness. People with ADHD do worse at estimating and reproducing lengths of time, and the deficit shows up across many studies. The way it feels from the inside is that there are only two times: now, and not now.

This matters for the need, because the engine of chronic uncertainty in humans is imagination: our ability to run the future in our heads, which Thomas Suddendorf and Michael Corballis call mental time travel. If the future arrives faintly, the uncertainty about it arrives faintly too. Next year doesn’t keep me up at night the way it might someone else. That sounds like a gift, and partly it is. It also means next year gets no plan.

Proximate: waiting hurts

The near future is the opposite. Edmund Sonuga-Barke found that for many people with ADHD, waiting is aversive in itself. He calls it delay aversion, and puts it beside executive dysfunction as one of two separate routes into the condition. A related finding is steep delay discounting: offered a smaller reward now or a larger one later, people with ADHD lean harder toward now, and a meta-analysis finds the effect is real, though moderate.

There’s a plausible mechanism underneath. Normally, dopamine neurons learn to fire not just at a reward but at the cue that predicts it, so the anticipation itself carries some of the pull. Gail Tripp and Jeff Wickens proposed that in ADHD this transfer to the cue is weak. Brain imaging fits: a meta-analysis by Plichta and Scheres found lower activity in the brain’s reward center while people with ADHD were anticipating a reward. If the cue doesn’t carry the reward, then only the reward itself does, and it has to come now.

Put that next to the last essay. Not knowing what to do next is a kind of waiting. So the uncertainty that hurts most in ADHD isn’t about next year. It’s about the next ten minutes.

Proximate: decide on less, then drift

Two more differences change what the need does once it fires.

The first is that people with ADHD tend to decide on less information. In tasks that let you uncover evidence before choosing, they uncover less and choose sooner. The second is that their minds wander more, and specifically more spontaneously. Paul Seli and colleagues found that ADHD symptoms track the mind-wandering that happens to you, not the kind you choose. Sonuga-Barke and Francisco Castellanos traced it to the default mode network, the brain’s idle network, failing to switch off during tasks.

Arie Kruglanski described the general response to uncertainty as seizing and freezing: grab the first answer that ends the doubt, then hold on to it. My read is that ADHD does the first part faster and the second part worse. It seizes fast and freezes less. It jumps on an answer, and then novelty pulls it to the next one.

Ritual runs the other way

The last essay ended in ritual. Anxiety drives the hazard-precaution system, and behavior turns repetitive, rigid and exact. In obsessive-compulsive disorder, that system is stuck on. It can’t stop.

ADHD has the opposite problem. The trouble isn’t a ritual that won’t stop. It’s keeping one going at all. Routines need the very things ADHD is short of: remembering, sustaining, doing the same thing again tomorrow when it’s no longer new.

So ritual still works, because the anxiety-lowering effect is real. But it only works if it doesn’t depend on being kept up. As I put it in Free of Tools: for ADHD, the ritual is done because it’s simple. The ritual has to be external, small and cheap enough to start that starting isn’t a decision.

Ultimate: why the traits survive

Now the evolutionary question. ADHD is highly heritable, around 70 to 80 percent in twin studies, and it isn’t a separate kind of brain so much as the far end of traits everyone has. Traits that costly and that heritable don’t usually stick around unless they pay somewhere.

The most interesting idea is that they pay in exploration. Every forager faces the explore-exploit trade-off: stay with the patch you know, or leave to look for a better one. In a stable world, staying wins. In a world that keeps changing, leaving early wins. A restless, novelty-seeking, now-focused brain is a decent explorer.

There is a little evidence for this. Among the Ariaal of Kenya, Dan Eisenberg and colleagues found that a gene variant linked to ADHD went with better nutrition in men who were still nomads, and worse nutrition in men who had settled. And in an online foraging game, David Barack and colleagues found that people with more ADHD traits left patches sooner and earned more.

I’d hold this loosely. It’s a handful of studies, and the popular version, that people with ADHD are hunters stuck in a farmer’s world, runs well ahead of them. But it fits the pattern from the last essay. It’s a mismatch: a trait tuned for exploring, running in a world of classrooms and offices built for sitting still and waiting.

Consequences

If the need in ADHD is tuned to the next few minutes, a few things follow.

The far future gets no ritual. The uncertainties other people dread and build meaning around, like retirement, health and the long arc of a career, pull weakly, so they get fewer plans. This is a blind spot, not a virtue.

The near future gets every ritual. And here I think the essays on this site have been describing the same thing from different sides. For ADHD, the new productivity system plays the part of the horoscope. It ends the “what do I do now?” uncertainty at once, which gives closure. It’s new, which gives novelty. It feels like control. Then the novelty fades, the system stops being kept up, and the next one is bought. Eighteen systems in a year, with a median life of four days, is what seizing fast and freezing less looks like from the outside.

Which means the sellers have found the need. The market in apps, templates and courses for ADHD is the astrology market of the near term. It sells into a need that’s built in, and it sells best at the moment the buyer is least able to judge: when the old system has just collapsed. The three questions still apply. Did they create the need? Could I do this without them? What does leaving cost me?

One caution. Anxiety is common alongside ADHD, in something like a quarter to a half of adults. For them, uncertainty may pull harder, not weaker, and the far future may be all too vivid. Everything here is an average, and ADHD varies a lot.

Meeting it honestly

If the need is built in and tuned to the next ten minutes, the answer isn’t to fight it. It’s to answer “what now?” before the need has to.

A short list, picked the night before, leaves no uncertainty in the morning. That’s the core of The Minimum System for ADHD: one list with everything, and one with at most three things for now.

A timer makes time visible, which is what a faint sense of time needs. Constraints Drive Actions is about designing around exactly this.

Small, immediate rewards shorten the wait, so the reward doesn’t have to travel back from the end of the task.

Automation keeps the ritual running without my having to keep it going. That’s the argument of Not Tools, but Automation and The Automated Morning Brief: the thing that answers “what now?” should arrive without my asking.

And the far future, the part that pulls too weakly, has to be brought near. Not as a goal for next year, but as one step that fits on today’s list.

The need is built in. In ADHD it’s built for the next ten minutes. The best system isn’t the newest one. It’s the one that has already answered the question by the time I ask it.

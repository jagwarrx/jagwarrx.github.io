Choosing a Task

November 2026. 424 words.

Most of the time, the gap between my best option and my third-best is much smaller than the time I spend agonizing over it. Any reasonable task started now beats the perfect task started forty minutes from now.

So the aim isn’t to choose well. It’s to choose fast, and take the decision off my plate.

Good enough is the rule

Herbert Simon had a word for this: satisficing. Take the first option that clears the bar, instead of searching for the best one. The search has a cost, and for a choice between tasks that are all worth doing, the cost is usually bigger than the difference. Barry Schwartz later found that people who always try to find the best option, maximizers, tend to end up less satisfied than people who settle for good enough.

Ways to take the decision off my plate

Choose before I need to. At the end of each block, or the night before, write down what the next block will be. Choosing is easier when I’m not about to start, and I sit down to an answer instead of a question.

Pick one tiebreaker and stick to it. Oldest first, smallest first, or the one I’m dreading. Which rule matters less than not reopening it every time.

Shrink the menu. Pull three candidates from the list and ignore the rest. Choosing between three is easy. Choosing among thirty is paralyzing. A short menu, all worth doing, makes any pick a safe one.

Timebox the choice. Sixty seconds. If I’m still torn, roll a die or flip a coin. If I feel a pang of “no, not that one” when it lands, I’ve learned what I actually wanted. Piet Hein wrote a short poem about exactly this trick.

Compare two at a time. Ranking a whole list is draining. “Do I want to do this one more than that one?” is easy. That’s the idea behind Mark Forster’s Final Version Perfected: go down the list asking that question, pick out a short chain of tasks, then do the last one picked. No ranking, no priorities, just quick gut comparisons. It’s the same question as “this, or next?”, which is the only one I can answer with a full list in front of me.

After the choice

The choice is spent once I start. If I reopen it halfway through, I’m choosing again, and the new option always looks better only because it’s new. So: choose fast, start, and stay with it until a stopping point.

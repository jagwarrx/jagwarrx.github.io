Understand Power as Defense, Lean Towards Mastery

November 2026. 403 words.

Robert Greene’s 48 Laws of Power teaches the serpent: how power games are played. Ramana teaches the dove: acting from the Self, without a separate agenda. Jesus asks for both together (The Serpent and the Dove), which means understanding the game fully while choosing not to play it dirty. So how should you read Greene? Understand power mainly as defending against it, and lean towards mastery.

The wrong kind of game

The book assumes the wrong kind of game. Most of its examples come from royal courts, where interactions were zero-sum, trust was low and power came from the top. Modern careers and relationships are mostly repeated games where reputation compounds. That’s the setting where Robert Axelrod found that cooperative strategies win: in his tournaments the winner was the simplest kind program, which cooperated first and then copied the other side’s last move.

Dacher Keltner’s research points the same way: “The cultural notion that you grab power is largely wrong. You don’t trick people and become powerful.” In his work, groups give power to the people who help them.

The cost

Living by these laws means treating relationships as instruments. The Harvard Study of Adult Development, which has followed its subjects since 1938, found that relationships are what matter most for a happy life.

Read it as defense

So read it defensively, to recognize the moves, not as a playbook to follow. Someone taking the credit for your work, hiding what they want, keeping you dependent: you see it for what it is and don’t walk into the trap. That’s the serpent’s half, and it’s enough.

Lean towards mastery

If you like Greene’s writing, his later books Mastery and The Laws of Human Nature are more balanced. Mastery is the better lean. Power from position can be taken away; power from skill goes with you.

It lines up with the three needs in Happiness Can Be Designed, Partly:

  • Competence. Mastery builds it, and it comes first, because you can trade it for the other two. It’s the edge worth having, measured against your past self (Why an Edge Feels Like Survival).
  • Autonomy. Defense guards it. Seeing the moves keeps your day out of someone else’s game.
  • Relatedness. Not playing dirty keeps it. People stay people, not instruments.

Power is still a means (Power Is a Means). Mastery is the clean way to get enough of it.

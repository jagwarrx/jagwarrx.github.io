Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know

November 2026. 1,370 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. Not to know what comes next, full stop, but to know what comes next well enough to act. Most answers to that need try to supply the knowing: a forecast, a plan, a promise from a god or a government.

Nassim Nicholas Taleb offers a different answer, and it may be the most radical one in these essays. Stop trying to know. Arrange things so you don’t need to.

You can’t know

Taleb’s earlier book, The Black Swan, made the case that the events that shape the world most are the ones nobody predicted: wars, crashes, pandemics, inventions. They’re rare, they’re huge, and afterward everyone explains why they were obvious.

The evidence on forecasting backs him up. Philip Tetlock followed hundreds of experts making thousands of predictions about politics and economics over nearly twenty years. On average, they did little better than chance, and the most famous ones, the ones most sure of their big theories, often did worse.

Taleb’s favorite illustration is a turkey. A farmer feeds it every day for a thousand days. Each day, the turkey’s confidence that the farmer loves it grows. On day one thousand and one, which happens to be the day before Thanksgiving, its confidence is at its highest. The forecast feels most certain just before it breaks.

Three relationships to the forecast

If you can’t know what comes next, the important question is how much you depend on knowing it. In Antifragile, Taleb sorts things into three kinds.

The fragile needs the forecast to be right. A glass, a business with one client, a household spending every dollar it earns. When the unexpected arrives, it breaks.

The robust doesn’t need the forecast. A rock, savings, a skill that’s useful anywhere. When the unexpected arrives, it stays the same.

The antifragile gains when the forecast is wrong. Muscles, which grow from the strain of lifting. An immune system, which learns from each infection. A person holding many small bets, each of which loses a little or wins a lot. When the unexpected arrives, it gets better. Taleb’s image for it: wind extinguishes a candle and energizes fire.

Seen through the need, this is a ladder. The fragile depends on knowing. The robust doesn’t need to know. The antifragile is glad it didn’t.

How the need makes us fragile

Here’s the uncomfortable part. Our usual answer to the need often makes us more fragile, not less.

The need wants things smooth and predictable, so we suppress the small swings. For decades, forest services put out every small fire, and the undergrowth piled up until fires came that were too big to stop. Parents who prevent every scrape raise children who’ve never learned to fall. Taleb calls this harm from too much intervention iatrogenics, after the medical term for harm caused by the healer.

People do it to themselves too. In Projecting, Masking and ADHD I described masking: hiding each small slip so the forecast others expect stays intact. It works, until the hidden slips add up into one big break. Smoothness bought this way is the turkey’s kind of safety.

It’s the reverse of Answers That Compound. A short-term answer to the need that builds a long-term fragility.

The tools

Taleb’s practical advice comes down to a few moves, each of which reduces how much you need to know.

Options. An option is a right without an obligation. Aristotle tells the story of Thales, the philosopher, who paid olive press owners a small fee in winter for the right to rent their presses at harvest. He couldn’t know whether the harvest would be good. He didn’t need to. If it was bad, he lost the small fee. If it was good, he made a fortune. Options turn surprise into something that can only help. Derek Sivers makes a related point in his essay on choices: there are always more than two options, and people who see only two have stopped looking.

The barbell. Instead of putting everything in the middle, medium risk, medium safety, split it. Keep most of what you have very safe, and put a small part into bets that could pay off hugely. The safe side means the unexpected can’t ruin you. The wild side means it might make you. A writer with a steady job and a novel on the side is on a barbell.

Remove before adding. Taleb calls it via negativa. It’s usually easier to know what harms you than what helps, so subtract first: the debt, the bad habit, the person who drains you. That’s the spirit of Avoid, Detect, Remove Bullshit with ADHD and Free of Tools.

Trust what has lasted. The Lindy effect says that for ideas, books and practices, the longer something has survived, the longer it’s likely to keep surviving. Time is the forecaster you can trust. Sivers has a chapter in How to Live with the same advice: value only what has endured.

Small doses of stress. Muscles, bones and immune systems get stronger from stress in the right dose. Mark Seery and colleagues found something like this in people. Those who had faced some adversity in their lives were doing better than those who had faced a lot, and also better than those who had faced none. A little hardship seems to build the capacity to meet more.

Fail small and often. A system that breaks a little, often, rarely breaks a lot, once. In Manage Commitments, Not Tasks I argued for renegotiating early: telling people about a slip while it’s small. That’s the same move applied to promises.

In an ordinary life

None of this needs a hedge fund. In a life, arranging things so you don’t need to know looks like savings, so a bad month isn’t a crisis. Skills that travel, so losing one job isn’t losing your work. Friends of many kinds, so one falling out doesn’t leave you alone. Trying things cheaply before committing, renting before buying, which Sivers lists among the traits of useful perspectives. And many small experiments, each with a small downside and an open upside.

This also extends the answer from Know How You’ll Meet It. That essay said: you can’t know what comes next, but you can know how you’ll meet it. Taleb adds: you can also arrange things so that whatever comes, you’re fine, and some of what comes makes you better.

For ADHD

An ADHD brain is variable. Brilliant one day, absent the next. In a world that rewards smoothness, the same output every day, that variability is fragile, and masking tries to hide it.

But variability isn’t fragile everywhere. In work where bursts pay off and a single miss is cheap, creative work, projects, many small bets, it can be close to antifragile. The deeper fix may be less about smoothing the brain and more about choosing settings where its swings don’t break anything, and sometimes pay.

The limits

Dose matters. Stress in the right amount strengthens, and too much breaks. Trauma isn’t hormesis, and Seery’s curve turns down at the high end.

Antifragility is easier to claim after the fact than to show in advance. “It made me stronger” is a story we tell looking back, and survivors tell it more than those who didn’t survive.

And Taleb overstates. Much of Antifragile is argument and attitude rather than measurement, and some things he calls antifragile are simply robust. The core idea survives the exaggeration.

The short version

You can’t know what comes next. The experts can’t either, and the forecast feels most certain right before it breaks. So the question isn’t how to know. It’s how much you depend on knowing. The fragile needs the forecast, the robust doesn’t, and the antifragile gains when it’s wrong.

Our usual answer to the need, smoothing out every swing, often makes us more fragile. The alternative is to hold options, keep a barbell, remove before adding, trust what has lasted, take stress in small doses and fail small and often. Know what comes next well enough to act, and where you can’t, arrange things so you don’t need to know.

The Wall of Awful

November 2026. 457 words.

The Wall of Awful is ADHD coach Brendan Mahan’s name for the emotional pile-up behind a task. Each past attempt that went badly, each bit of guilt, each “why haven’t you done this yet” adds a brick.

After a while the hard part isn’t the task anymore. It’s facing everything I feel about the task. That’s why “just do it” doesn’t work. It’s a wall of feelings, not a time-management problem.

How the wall grows

The wall is built out of time. A task I put off today is a little heavier tomorrow, because now it carries a day of not doing it. The task itself doesn’t change. The bricks do. It’s also why a task that once felt fun can go sour: the longer I think about it without doing it, the less novel it is, and the more it holds.

Four ways to meet it

Staring at it. I freeze, scroll and feel worse, which adds another brick.

Avoiding it. I do something else, sometimes something productive, so the avoidance feels okay.

Smashing through. I wait until panic, anger or a deadline gives me enough adrenaline to push through. It works, but it wears me out, and it teaches my brain that the task only happens through misery. That’s a brick for next time.

Climbing it. The sustainable way. Over the wall in small, kind steps.

What climbing looks like

Name the bricks. Say or write what the dread actually is. “I’m embarrassed it’s been three weeks.” “I’m scared it’ll be bad.” Naming a feeling takes some of its weight off.

Make the first step embarrassingly small. Not “write the report” but “open the doc and type the title.” The goal is to start, not to finish.

Remove one brick before starting. Send the “sorry for the delay, working on it now.” Put on the playlist. Get the snack. Clear whatever makes it feel heavy.

Borrow momentum. Work next to someone, in person or online. Set a ten-minute timer. Start right after something I’ve already finished.

Talk to myself like a friend would. Self-criticism adds bricks. “This is hard and I’m doing it anyway” takes them away. A missed deadline is a fault in the design, not a flaw in me.

Notice the win. Each time I get over, the next wall is a bit shorter. So I let myself register it.

Keep the wall low

Climbing is for walls that already exist. The cheaper move is to stop them growing. Start soon, even badly, so a day of not doing it doesn’t become a brick. Plan the first step the night before, so there’s no gap where dread can settle. A wall can only be built where a task sits untouched.

The Need Is Built In

November 2026. 2,160 words.

In Why Belief Systems Survive I ended up putting one question ahead of the others: who needs it, and why? Follow the need before you follow the money or the power, because the money and the power ride on a demand that’s already there.

But that only moves the question back a step. Why is the need so strong? Why do people facing an uncertain outcome reach so reliably for a ritual, an omen, a horoscope, a story? Is it something in our psychology, or something evolution put there?

Both, and the difference between those two answers turns out to be the key to the whole thing.

Two kinds of why

Biologists learned long ago that “why” is two questions. Ernst Mayr called them proximate and ultimate, and Niko Tinbergen built them into his four questions about behavior.

The proximate question is how it works: what happens inside the animal, right now, that produces the behavior. The ultimate question is why it exists: what about this behavior made ancestors who had it leave more descendants than ancestors who didn’t.

Take sugar. Why do you want the cake? The proximate answer is that sweetness lights up your reward system. The ultimate answer is that for most of our history, calories were scarce, and the ones who loved sweet fruit ate more of them. Both answers are true. Neither replaces the other. They answer different questions.

The need for meaning in uncertainty works the same way. Proximately, uncertainty feels bad and the brain acts to make it stop. Ultimately, the brains that acted that way left more descendants. Let’s take them in order.

Proximate: uncertainty hurts

The brain is best understood as a prediction machine. In the predictive processing view, associated with Karl Friston, its basic job is to keep surprise low: to predict what comes next and update when it’s wrong. Uncertainty is the state in which the predictions are failing. The machine is telling you it doesn’t know.

That state is felt, and what it feels like is anxiety. Jacob Hirsh, Raymond Mar and Jordan Peterson call it psychological entropy: the more possible outcomes are open, the more disorder in the system, and the more the mind strains to reduce it.

The strangest evidence for this is that not knowing can be worse than bad news. In a 2016 experiment by Archy de Berker and colleagues, people played a game in which they sometimes got electric shocks. Their stress, measured by sweat and pupil size, didn’t track how likely a shock was. It tracked how uncertain it was. When a shock was nearly certain, people were calmer than when it might or might not come.

So the first part of the need is simple. Uncertainty is aversive in itself, apart from whatever it’s uncertain about. You want it to end, and an answer ends it.

Proximate: the brain fills the gap

The second part is what the brain does about it. It doesn’t wait for facts. It makes them.

The clearest demonstration comes from Michael Gazzaniga’s work with split-brain patients, whose two hemispheres had been surgically disconnected. In one test, the right hemisphere was shown a snow scene and the left a chicken claw. The patient’s left hand, run by the right hemisphere, picked a picture of a shovel. Asked why, the left hemisphere, which had seen only the claw and knew nothing of the snow, answered at once: you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. It wasn’t lying. It just couldn’t bear a gap. Gazzaniga called this system the interpreter. It produces a confident story whether or not it has the facts.

And the stories it prefers have someone in them. People see agents and intentions everywhere: faces in clouds, purpose in accidents, a message in a run of bad luck. Justin Barrett called this hyperactive agency detection, and Stewart Guthrie argued that it underlies religion itself. A world run by someone can be pleaded with. A world run by chance can’t.

Proximate: anxiety turns into ritual

The third part explains why the answer so often takes the form of an act rather than just a belief.

Pascal Boyer and Pierre Liénard describe a hazard-precaution system: a set of mental routines that ancient dangers, like contamination, predators and harm from other people, set off. When it fires, behavior becomes repetitive, rigid and exact. Wash again. Check again. Do it in the right order. In obsessive-compulsive disorder the system runs with no off switch. In ordinary life it shows up as ritual.

You can watch it happen. In a 2015 study by Martin Lang and colleagues, people who were made anxious, by being told to prepare a public speech, began moving their hands in more repetitive and rigid ways while handling an object. Nobody taught them a ritual. The anxiety produced one.

And ritual works, proximately. Alison Wood Brooks and her colleagues found that performing even a meaningless ritual before a stressful task lowered anxiety and improved performance. Ellen Langer showed the general tendency behind it: when an outcome is uncertain, people act as if their own actions can sway it, the illusion of control. The act gives the mind something to do with the uncertainty, and the anxiety drops.

Put the three parts together and you have the proximate need. Uncertainty hurts. The brain fills the gap with a story, usually one with an agent in it. And the anxiety drives toward exact, repeated action. That’s a mantra, a horoscope and a puja in three sentences.

Ultimate: the cheaper mistake

Now the other why. Why would evolution build a brain that invents causes it can’t see?

Because the two possible mistakes don’t cost the same. Martie Haselton and David Buss call this error management. Hear a rustle in the grass. If you take it for a predator and it’s the wind, you’ve wasted a jump. If you take it for the wind and it’s a predator, you’re dead. Selection doesn’t favor the accurate animal. It favors the one biased toward the cheaper error. So we’re built to see too many agents, too many causes, too many patterns, because the ancestors who saw too few were eaten.

Randolph Nesse calls this the smoke detector principle. A smoke detector that goes off when you burn toast is annoying. One that stays quiet during a fire is a catastrophe. So smoke detectors are set to go off too often, and so is anxiety.

The same logic shows up in learning itself. In 1948 B. F. Skinner fed pigeons at fixed intervals, no matter what they did. The pigeons developed rituals anyway: one turned in circles, another bobbed its head, each repeating whatever it happened to be doing when food last came. Superstition doesn’t need a priest. It falls out of any system that learns from coincidences. Kevin Foster and Hanna Kokko later showed formally that natural selection favors this. When missing a real cause is costly, an animal that also believes in some false ones comes out ahead.

Ultimate: imagination

That explains why animals have the machinery. It doesn’t explain why humans have it so much worse. For that, you need imagination.

Other animals fear what’s in front of them. Humans can fear what isn’t there yet. Thomas Suddendorf and Michael Corballis call our ability to imagine possible futures mental time travel, and it may be the most powerful thing our species has. It lets us plan, store food for a winter that hasn’t come, and see the trap before we’re in it.

But the same faculty that sees the trap can see a thousand traps that aren’t there. A gazelle’s uncertainty ends when the lion leaves. Ours doesn’t end, because we can keep imagining the lion. We can be uncertain about next year, about our children’s marriages, about our own death. Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert found that people’s minds wander from what they’re doing almost half the time, and that they’re less happy when they do.

Imagination turned the smoke detector from a device for emergencies into a background condition. That’s why the need for meaning in humans isn’t occasional. It’s chronic.

Ultimate: the group

There’s one more layer. Humans don’t face uncertainty alone, and a meaning shared by a group does something a private one can’t: it lets people coordinate. If everyone agrees the omens are bad, the whole village waits. If everyone performs the same rite, everyone knows who’s committed.

This is where the scholars split. One camp, including Boyer and Scott Atran, says religion is a byproduct: it piggybacks on machinery built for other jobs, like agency detection, mind reading and hazard precaution. Another, including David Sloan Wilson in a book called Darwin’s Cathedral, says religion is an adaptation in its own right, selected because groups that had it cooperated better than groups that didn’t. The most likely answer is both. The parts are byproducts. The packages that won were selected at the level of the group.

Mismatch

All of this was tuned for a particular world: small bands, physical dangers, threats that came and went. We no longer live in it.

The uncertainties we face now are abstract and permanent: jobs, markets, health, whether a marriage will last. They don’t leave like the lion. Biologists call this evolutionary mismatch, a trait shaped for one environment running in another. Sugar was a good thing to crave when it was scarce. A taste for meaning was a good thing to have when the dangers were real and brief. Now the alarm has nowhere to stop.

Consequences

Once you see the need this way, several things follow.

The first is that the need is never satisfied for long. If uncertainty is chronic, so is the demand to reduce it. That’s why the most durable products in this market are subscriptions: the daily almanac, the yearly horoscope, the weekly rite.

The second is that the fastest answer beats the truest one. The job of meaning, proximately, isn’t to be right. It’s to end the uncertainty. Arie Kruglanski calls this the need for closure, and describes what it does: people seize on the first answer that ends the doubt, then freeze on it. An astrologer who answers today will always beat a statistician who says the question can’t be answered.

The third is that demand peaks when control is lowest. Malinowski saw it in the Trobriand Islands, where fishermen used no magic in the safe lagoon and a great deal on the open sea. The same is true now. People pay most for meaning before an exam, a surgery, a wedding, a funeral. This is the moment a seller has the most power, and the moment the buyer is least able to judge.

The fourth is that the alarm can be pulled on purpose. If anxiety is set to go off too often, someone who can set it off can sell the cure. A planetary “defect” in a marriage chart is a manufactured rustle in the grass. So is a lot of advertising, and a lot of news.

The fifth is that the same machinery produces conspiracy theories. Agency detection plus a loss of control is a recipe for seeing a plot. Some studies found that people made to feel less in control see more patterns in noise. Not all of those findings have held up, but the mechanism is the same one that sees a god behind a storm.

The sixth is the hopeful one. Science runs on the same need. The urge to explain what happened, and to predict what happens next, is what science is made of. The difference isn’t the need. It’s the discipline: science lets an answer be wrong, and keeps the question open until it isn’t. That’s harder, because it means living with more uncertainty for longer, which is exactly what the machinery hates.

What to do with it

If the need is built in, you can’t argue it away, and you shouldn’t try to. What you can do is meet it on purpose.

Know that the alarm is a smoke detector. Most of the time it’s toast. The feeling that something must be done is not evidence that something can be.

Reduce the uncertainty you can actually reduce, and name the rest. Writing down what you don’t know is a strange relief, because it turns a fog into a list.

Use ritual knowingly. A ritual you know is a ritual still lowers anxiety; the studies used rituals people knew were meaningless. A plan for the day does the same job. It ends the uncertainty about what to do next, and that’s worth having even though the plan doesn’t change the future.

And when someone offers to end your uncertainty for a fee, ask the three questions from the last essay. Did they create the need? Could I do this without them? What does leaving cost me?

This is why need comes first. The need is built in. Priests, astrologers and apps don’t create it. They sell into it.

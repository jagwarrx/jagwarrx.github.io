Control, and Letting the World Play Out

November 2026. 473 words.

The last few notes were about control. An edge mostly buys it (Why an Edge Feels Like Survival), and a sense of it predicts health and happiness. But control has a limit, and past that limit it turns into the problem. What you want is a balance of control and letting the world play out.

Two ways to get it wrong

Too little control is learned helplessness: you stop steering because steering never seemed to work, even after things change.

Too much is the opposite failure. Taleb’s point in Arrange Things So You Don’t Need to Know is that suppressing every small shock makes a system fragile: put out every small forest fire and you get the big one. In a person, over-control looks like needing to know what comes next, and fighting what’s already happening. That fight is the resistance in Suffering = Pain × Resistance.

The old split

Epictetus opens his handbook with it: some things are within our power, and others are not. Reinhold Niebuhr’s Serenity Prayer asks for the serenity to accept what can’t be changed, the courage to change what can, and the wisdom to know the difference. The Tao Te Ching goes furthest with wu wei, acting without forcing: do less and less, and nothing is left undone.

The hard part is the last line of the prayer, knowing the difference. That’s a job for observation. As in Observation Is a Tool, you train attention until it can see, in the moment, which side of the line a thing is on: this I can move, this I can only meet.

Slack

The practical way to let the world play out without losing the thread is slack. Tom DeMarco, in Slack, uses the sliding-tile puzzle: it only works because one square is empty. Fill that square to be efficient and nothing can move. The same holds for a queue. In the simplest model, a server busy 90% of the time has waits nine times as long as one busy half the time (Mechanics of a System). Zvi Mowshowitz defines slack as the absence of binding constraints on behavior.

For a life, slack is unplanned time in the week, a money buffer, energy left at the end of the day, a plan with room in it. It’s what lets you respond when the world moves, instead of being knocked over by it. A fully controlled schedule has no slack, so it’s the one most easily broken.

The short version

Steer what’s yours, let the rest play out, and leave room between them. Observation tells you which is which. Slack gives you room to act when things move. That’s the same balance as in Balance Is the Ground, Not the Goal: not a fixed point, but somewhere you keep coming back to.

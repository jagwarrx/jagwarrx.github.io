Buddhism Through the Lens of the Need

November 2026. 1,830 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. In What Each Religion Sells I sorted the major religions by how they answer that need. Some forecast the outcome. Some fix the response. And two, Daoism and Buddhism, try to loosen the need itself. Buddhism goes furthest. It’s the only one that names the need as the problem.

Reading Robert Wright’s Why Buddhism Is True, I came across a sentence that stopped me. Pleasure is designed not to last. Feelings are tools natural selection built to keep us striving, and a pleasure that lasted would stop us striving. I’d known about the hedonic treadmill for years, but I’d never seen it explained as a design choice.

That sentence turns out to be the key to reading Buddhism through the need.

Why pleasure doesn’t last

Wright’s argument comes from evolutionary psychology. Natural selection doesn’t care whether we’re happy. It cares whether we eat, mate, win status and protect our children. Feelings are how it gets us to do those things. Pleasure rewards a step in the right direction, and then it has to fade, or we’d stop taking the next one. The psychologists Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell called the result the hedonic treadmill: however much things improve, we drift back to where we started and want more.

Neuroscience adds the mechanism, and it’s the same one behind the need. Wolfram Schultz found that dopamine neurons fire for a reward that comes as a surprise. But once a reward is fully predicted, the burst moves to whatever predicts it, and the reward itself barely registers. The brain responds to the gap between what it expected and what happened. Close the gap, and the signal goes quiet.

So pleasure fades for the same reason uncertainty hurts. The brain is a prediction machine. It cares about error. A pleasure it can predict perfectly is no longer news. That’s why the second cup of coffee is less exciting than the first, and why the new phone stops feeling new in a week.

Two sides of one machine

Put that next to the earlier essays and something clicks. The need to know what comes next is the threat side of the machine: uncertainty about a danger hurts, and the brain reaches for any answer to close it. The treadmill is the reward side: a pleasure, once predicted, stops satisfying, so the brain goes looking for the next one.

Buddhism saw both sides, and a third. It names three roots of suffering, often called the three poisons. Greed, or craving: the reward side. Aversion: the threat side. And delusion: the model the machine builds, which it mistakes for the world. That’s a fair description of a predictive brain, written about two and a half thousand years before anyone had the term.

The Four Noble Truths as a diagnosis

The Buddha’s core teaching has the shape of a doctor’s diagnosis, and it reads naturally as a diagnosis of the need.

The first truth is dukkha, usually translated as suffering, though unsatisfactoriness is closer. It isn’t only pain. It’s the fact that nothing quite satisfies, and that even the good things slip away. That’s the treadmill.

The second is its cause, taṇhā, literally thirst. The tradition names three kinds: thirst for pleasure, thirst to become something, and thirst to be rid of something. Wanting more, wanting to be more, and wanting the bad thing gone. Each one is the brain trying to close a gap.

The third is that it can end. The fourth is the path that ends it.

Impermanence: no forecast holds

Every other answer to the need tries, in some way, to make the future knowable. Buddhism refuses. Its teaching of impermanence, anicca, says that everything conditioned changes. Not some things, and not occasionally. Everything, always.

That’s a direct reply to the need. You want to know what comes next. Buddhism says that whatever comes next will also pass, so no answer you seize can hold. The problem isn’t that you lack the right forecast. It’s that you’re asking a changing world to stay still.

Buddhism’s word for the grasping is upādāna, clinging. It’s close to what Arie Kruglanski measured as the need for closure in The Why Behind the Need: seizing the first answer and freezing on it. Clinging is the need for closure, seen from the inside.

Not-self: who needs to know?

The strangest Buddhist teaching is anattā, not-self: there’s no fixed, separate self behind experience. Wright argues that modern psychology points the same way. The mind looks less like a single CEO and more like a set of modules that take turns in control. Split-brain studies by Michael Gazzaniga showed one half of the brain confidently explaining actions the other half had caused. The self that feels in charge is often narrating after the fact.

Seen through the need, this matters because so much of the need is about protecting someone. The fear of what comes next is the fear of what will happen to me. Ernest Becker built a whole theory on the terror of that self ending. If the self is less solid than it feels, there’s less to defend, and the need has less to grip.

The technique: watch the need fire

Buddhism’s method for all of this is meditation, and the version Wright practices is simple to describe. Sit, and watch feelings arise. Notice an itch, an urge, a worry, as a sensation, without acting on it. See it peak and fade.

In terms of the need, that’s practice at letting the gap stay open. The urge to check, to fix, to know, comes up, and you don’t answer it. You learn, by repetition, that the open gap is survivable. Clinicians use a version of this called urge surfing, in which people with addictions learn to ride out a craving instead of fighting it or obeying it, and it’s part of a relapse-prevention program with trial support.

There’s also a newer account in the language of predictive processing. Ruben Laukkonen and Heleen Slagter have argued that meditation gradually loosens the brain’s grip on its own predictions, so they shape experience less forcefully. If that’s right, meditation works on the machine directly: not by giving the need an answer, but by turning down how hard it insists on one.

The aim is equanimity, upekkhā. Not knowing what comes next, and being all right with it.

The problem underneath the problems

Seen side by side, the religions in the last essay share a pattern. Each names a problem: sin, chaos, exile, disconnection. And each of those is, in a sense, an instance. Solve one, and the need produces another, because the machine that generates problems is still running. The relief is real, and it’s temporary.

Buddhism names the generator. Its problem isn’t a particular lack. It’s the craving and clinging that turn every situation into a lack. Of all the traditions, it’s the only one whose problem is the need itself.

It isn’t the only one that noticed the restlessness. Augustine wrote, in the fourth century, that our heart is restless until it rests in God. That’s the treadmill, named sixteen hundred years before Brickman. The Bhagavad Gītā traces a chain from dwelling on things, to attachment, to desire, to anger, to delusion. Seeing the restlessness is common. What differs is what it’s taken to mean. For Augustine it’s a signal: we’re missing something real, and only the right thing will fill it. For Buddhism it’s a feature of how the mind works, and nothing will fill it, because filling is the problem. That fork is big enough for its own essay, Signal or Feature.

Buddhism also does something no other tradition here does. It warns you against its own product. If craving is the problem, then craving enlightenment is just more craving. In the parable of the raft, the Buddha compares his teaching to a raft for crossing a river: once you’re across, you don’t carry it on your back. The Zen teacher Linji put it more violently: if you meet the Buddha, kill him. Don’t cling even to the answer.

So the line, sharpened: every religion names a problem. Only Buddhism names the problem that makes problems, and then tells you not to turn its own answer into the next one.

The fine print

Every answer has costs, and Buddhism’s are worth naming.

Detachment can become disengagement. The psychologist John Welwood called it spiritual bypassing: using practice to avoid feelings and problems rather than face them. Feelings can be filed as defilements rather than read as signals. Grief and anger sometimes carry information, and watching them pass isn’t always the right response.

And the claim that Buddhism is science deserves care. The evidence for meditation is real but modest. Daniel Goleman and Richard Davidson, sympathetic reviewers, found that much of the research is weak, and that the strongest effects appear in long-term practitioners. The philosopher Evan Thompson, in Why I Am Not a Buddhist, argues that the scientific Buddhism of Western books is a modern construction, and that Buddhism is a religion, not a theory of mind.

The part the books leave out

Wright’s Buddhism is an unbundled one, in the sense from The Market for the Need. It keeps the diagnosis and the technique, and sets aside rebirth, devotion, monastic life and the community. That’s a reasonable choice for a book. But the tradition itself would object to the last omission.

Buddhists take refuge in three things, the Three Jewels: the Buddha, the teaching, and the saṅgha, the community of practitioners. Community is a third of the refuge. That fits the limit from Meeting the Need Yourself: some of the need can’t be met alone. A meditation app sells the technique without the sangha, which is the version of Buddhism the tradition itself would recognize as incomplete.

The tradition also holds the other half. In the account of his last days, the Buddha tells his followers to be islands unto themselves, to rely on themselves and on the teaching, not on a leader. Self-sufficiency and community, together. A core you make yourself, inside a group you chose.

The short version

The brain is a prediction machine. On the threat side, uncertainty hurts, and it reaches for any answer. On the reward side, a pleasure it can predict stops satisfying, and it reaches for the next one. Buddhism named both sides, craving and aversion, along with the model that mistakes itself for the world.

Its answer isn’t a forecast or a fixed response. It’s to see that nothing holds still, loosen the clinging, and practice letting the gap stay open. That’s the most direct answer to the need any tradition has offered. Its fine print is that detachment can become avoidance, and that the technique sold alone leaves out the community the tradition counts as a third of the refuge.

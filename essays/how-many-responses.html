How Many Responses You Need

November 2026. 1,260 words.

In Know How You’ll Meet It I argued for a different answer to the need. You can’t know what comes next, but you can know how you’ll meet it. Fix the response, not the forecast.

There’s an obvious objection. The world has endless situations. If I need a response ready for each one, I’ve swapped an impossible forecast for an impossible list. So how many responses do you actually need?

The answer is about five, plus one default. The reason it’s so few is the interesting part.

Ashby’s law

In the 1950s the psychiatrist and cyberneticist W. Ross Ashby worked out a law about anything that tries to keep itself steady: a thermostat, a body, a company, a person. He called it the law of requisite variety, and put it in four words: only variety can destroy variety. To hold steady, a regulator needs at least as many distinct responses as there are distinct disturbances it has to deal with.

Taken at face value, that’s bad news. A day can go wrong in a thousand ways. By Ashby’s law, a thousand ways of going wrong need a thousand responses.

But the law has three escape routes built into it, and between them they take care of almost everything.

Protect only what matters

Ashby didn’t ask a regulator to respond to every disturbance. He asked it to protect its essential variables: the few things that must stay within limits for it to survive. A body doesn’t respond to the color of the sky. It responds to its temperature, its blood sugar, its oxygen.

A life has essential variables too. Sleep. The one task that matters this week. Money. The few people closest to you. Most of what happens in a day doesn’t touch any of them.

And anything that doesn’t touch them needs no response at all. Ignoring is a response, and it covers more situations than every other response put together. The first question about any disturbance isn’t “what do I do?” It’s “does this touch anything that matters?” Usually it doesn’t.

Cut what comes in, and add slack

The management theorist Stafford Beer, who built on Ashby, saw that there are two ways to balance the equation. You can add responses, or you can reduce the variety coming in. He called the second attenuation.

Filters attenuate. Fewer inputs, fewer apps, no feed in the morning, one place where things are written down. Every source you cut is a whole family of disturbances you never have to answer.

Buffers do something similar from the other side. They absorb disturbances without needing any response. Slack in the day, so one block slipping doesn’t break the rest. Money in the bank, so a surprise bill is just a bill. A coat handles a thousand kinds of cold without deciding anything. A buffer is a response you made once, in advance, that keeps working.

A purpose covers more than a script

Beer’s other move was amplification: making each response cover more situations.

The way to do that is to write responses as purposes, not scripts. A script handles one situation. “When the system collapses, open the backup app and re-enter the tasks” works only for that collapse, in that shape. A purpose handles many. “Get back to one next step” works when the system collapses, when the day goes off the rails, when I’ve lost an hour, and when I don’t know why I’m stuck.

Armies found this out under the most variety anyone faces. That’s why they give orders as commander’s intent: the purpose rather than the steps. One purpose, held by everyone, produces the right move in situations no plan foresaw.

Experts work the same way. Gary Klein studied how fire commanders make decisions under pressure and found they almost never compare options. They recognize the situation as a kind they’ve seen, and the kind tells them what to do. Their repertoire is large, but it’s built from kinds, not cases.

What’s left

After all three, what’s left is small enough to write on a card.

One default, for everything that isn’t named. Mine is: stop, breathe out long, and find the smallest next step. It works for almost anything, badly for some things, but well enough to get to the next decision.

Then three to five named responses, for the situations that keep coming back. When the system collapses, I go back to a sticky note with three things on it. When anxiety spikes, I breathe, name it, and write the loop down. When energy is low, I switch to a minimum day.

And around them, a few filters and buffers, which mean most disturbances never arrive.

Why so few named ones? Because the responses have to be available when you need them, and that’s under stress. Nelson Cowan’s review of working memory puts what we can hold at once at about four items. Atul Gawande, studying checklists in surgery and aviation, found the good ones have between five and nine items and leave out everything a professional would do anyway. Pilots carry thick books of procedures, but only a handful of steps are memorized, and the first rule above all of them is to fly the plane. The rest can be looked up once the plane is flying.

How a response earns its place

The list stays small only if adding to it is hard.

My rule is that a situation gets a named response when it has hit three times and the default didn’t handle it. Once is noise. Twice might be a coincidence. Three times is a pattern worth naming. It’s the same discipline as writing the fault first, from Avoid, Detect, Remove Bullshit with ADHD: no addition without a written failure behind it.

And responses can retire. If a named one hasn’t been used in a couple of months, it goes back to the default. If the situation returns, it will earn its place again.

Too many responses

There’s a failure on the other side, and it’s familiar. A list of thirty responses is a system, and it fails the way systems fail: nobody remembers it at the moment it’s needed, and keeping it up becomes its own work. It’s the forecast again, in a new form: trying to cover every case in advance.

Ashby’s law says you need enough variety. It doesn’t say more is better. Past the point where you can recall it under stress, each new response makes the whole set weaker.

The ADHD version

For an ADHD brain the card has to be physical and in view, for the same reason the day’s three things are on a sticky note. A response I have to remember to look up isn’t one I’ll use. The card goes where I’ll see it at the moment things go wrong: on the desk, on the back of the phone, as the lock screen.

And the default matters more than the named ones. The named responses cover the situations I’ve seen. The default covers the ones I haven’t, and an ADHD life produces a lot of those.

The short version

Ashby’s law says you need as much variety as the world throws at you. But you only have to protect what matters, filters and buffers soak up most of the rest, and a purpose covers more than a script. What’s left is one default and three to five named responses, on a card you can see.

You can’t know what comes next. You don’t need a thousand ways to meet it. You need about five, and one for everything else.

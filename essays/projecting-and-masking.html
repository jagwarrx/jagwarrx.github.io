Projecting, Masking and ADHD

November 2026. 1,280 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. That includes what comes next from other people. Every person you deal with is something you have to predict: will they be kind, will they do what they said, will they turn up.

So we help each other out. We project a version of ourselves that’s easy to read. What we show the world is, more than anything, a forecast of ourselves. And for people with ADHD, that forecast is unusually hard to keep, which is where masking comes in.

What we project, and why

Susan Fiske’s research found that we judge other people on two things first: warmth and competence. Will you harm me, and can you do what you say? Robert Cialdini added a third: consistency. People work hard to seem the same tomorrow as today, because a person who changes is a person you can’t plan around.

Seen through the need, projecting works in both directions. It lets others predict you, which is what trust is: a confident forecast of someone. And it lets you predict them. Mark Leary argued that self-esteem works like a gauge of how accepted we are, and rejection is the uncertainty most people fear most. Managing how you come across is a way of making your future treatment more predictable.

William Swann found something stranger. People want others to see them as they see themselves, even when that view is negative. Given the choice, many prefer a partner who confirms their low opinion of themselves to one who flatters them. Coherence beats praise. That’s the need, plainly: a predictable world matters more than a pleasant one.

The ADHD gap

Russell Barkley, one of the leading ADHD researchers, puts it in one line: ADHD is a disorder of performing what you know, not of knowing what to do. People with ADHD usually know they should start early, reply to the email, leave on time. The trouble is doing it, at the moment it needs doing.

The most distinctive feature is inconsistency. Brilliant on Tuesday, absent on Wednesday. Hours of focus on something interesting, nothing on something urgent. That’s exactly the thing projection is supposed to hide. Other people want a stable forecast, and the ADHD brain can’t reliably produce one.

Worse, when the forecast breaks, others reach for the wrong explanation. Lee Ross named the fundamental attribution error: we tend to explain other people’s behavior by their character, not their circumstances. A missed deadline from someone with ADHD gets read as laziness or not caring. The cause is invisible. The broken promise isn’t.

Masking

So many people with ADHD learn to mask. The term comes mostly from autism research, where Laura Hull and colleagues studied what they called camouflaging. Research on ADHD masking is younger, but people with ADHD describe something very similar.

It looks like this. Rehearsing what to say. Writing everything down in secret so no one sees how much you’d otherwise forget. Staying up late to catch up on what others did in the afternoon. Laughing off lateness as a quirk. Sitting on your hands. Watching how other people pace a meeting and copying it. Saying yes, then scrambling.

In terms of the need, masking is keeping up the forecast others expect with a private scramble behind it. To the outside, a reliable person. Inside, a lot of work no one sees.

Why it costs so much

Erving Goffman described social life as a stage, with a front stage where we perform and a back stage where we rest. Masking makes the gap between the two very wide, and keeping it costs energy every day.

James Gross found that suppressing an emotion doesn’t make it smaller. It leaves the feeling just as strong inside, adds physical stress, and makes the person harder to connect with. Masking is suppression all day long.

There’s a quieter cost too, and Swann’s work explains it. If people praise the mask, the praise doesn’t land, because it doesn’t match the person you know you are. You get the worst of both: the effort of the performance, and none of the reassurance it was meant to buy. That’s a common recipe for feeling like an impostor.

And the forecast is fragile. A mask held up by effort fails when effort runs out: when you’re tired, sick, or under too much at once. Then the drop is sudden, and it confirms the moral explanation others were already reaching for. In Dealing with Anxiety When You Have ADHD I described anxiety as the engine many ADHD brains run on. Masking is one of the main things that engine is powering.

What helps

The aim isn’t to stop projecting. Everyone projects, and other people do need to predict you. The aim is to make the forecast cheaper to keep. Here are the moves I’ve found most convincing.

Promise less, then keep it. The simplest way to be predictable is to make fewer, smaller predictions. A narrow forecast you can meet beats a broad one you can’t. “I’ll send a draft Friday” is better than “I’ll have it all done this week.”

Move the reliability outside. Barkley’s main advice is to put help at the point of performance: the reminder, the timer, the visible list, the person waiting, right where and when the action has to happen. Then the reliability is real, not performed. Structure carries the load the mask used to carry.

Show the system, not the scramble. Masking hides the tools. But people trust visible systems. “Let me put that in my tracker” or “send me a reminder the day before” projects reliability honestly, and it’s how many very organized people work anyway.

Name the mechanism to a few people. Telling someone how your brain works turns a moral explanation into a known variable. “If I go quiet, nudge me, it isn’t that I don’t care.” It doesn’t have to mean a diagnosis, and it doesn’t have to be everyone. Disclosure at work carries real risks, so choose who and how.

Keep a back stage. Some people and places where no mask is needed. Rest isn’t a luxury here. It’s what lets the front stage last.

Choose places that fit. Some jobs and teams reward bursts of deep focus and don’t punish variability. Where your pattern is expected, there’s less to hide.

Be kinder about the gap. Kristin Neff’s research on self-compassion found that treating your own failures with kindness, rather than harshness, is linked to more resilience and more willingness to try again. Swann shows we cling to our self-view. Self-compassion gives you a better one to cling to.

The fine print

Each of these has limits. Disclosure can backfire, and some workplaces will use it against you. Tools can become a new mask if you hide them. And dropping the mask too fast, everywhere at once, can cost trust you’ll need. The point isn’t to be unmasked. It’s to stop paying for a forecast in effort when you could pay for it in structure.

The short version

What you project is a forecast of yourself. Others need to predict you, and you need to predict how they’ll treat you. ADHD makes that forecast hard to keep, because the core problem is inconsistent performance, and others read the broken forecast as a flaw of character.

Masking keeps the forecast up with a private scramble, and it costs energy, connection and even the reassurance it was meant to buy. What helps is making the forecast cheaper: promise less, put the reliability in structure, show the system, tell a few people how you work, keep a back stage, choose places that fit, and be kind about the gap.

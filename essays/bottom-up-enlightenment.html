Bottom-Up Programming for Enlightenment

November 2026. 950 words.

Paul Graham has a line about Lisp. In Lisp, he wrote, you don’t just write your program down toward the language, you also build the language up toward your program. You grow a small vocabulary that fits the problem, and then the program gets short, because the words do most of the work.

Reading Shinzen Young’s The Science of Enlightenment, I kept thinking of that line. Shinzen doesn’t start from the big words people usually bring to the mind: ego, soul, enlightenment. He builds a small language for experience, from the bottom up. And once the language exists, the practices written in it are so short they fit in a sentence.

The words

Shinzen’s language has three words: see, hear and feel. Each comes in two kinds, outer and inner.

See outer is what’s in front of your eyes. See inner is a mental image, like the face of someone you’re annoyed with. Hear outer is sound: traffic, a fan, a voice. Hear inner is mental talk, the voice that says you’ll never finish. Feel outer is physical sensation: the chair, an itch, a sore shoulder. Feel inner is emotional sensation in the body: the squeeze in the chest that comes with dread, the warmth that comes with relief.

That’s the whole vocabulary. Everything you experience fits in one of those six places, or several at once. And each place has a resting state: a blank visual field, a quiet mind, a relaxed body. Even nothing gets a word.

It feels almost too small to be useful. Lisp gets the same reaction.

Why small words help

The trouble with the big words is that you can’t do anything with them. Anxiety arrives as one huge thing, about your whole life, all at once. There’s no handle on it.

In Shinzen’s words, it comes apart. There’s an image: you, failing, in front of someone. There’s some talk: “this is going to go badly.” And there’s a feeling: a tight band across the chest. Three things, each of them small. An image is just an image. A sentence is just a sentence. A squeeze is just a squeeze.

This is the oldest trick in programming. A problem you can’t solve gets split into problems you can. The monster was never one thing. It was three ordinary things happening at the same time.

Three skills

With the words in place, Shinzen trains three skills. Concentration: staying with what you chose to attend to. Sensory clarity: seeing exactly which of the six places something is in, and how it’s changing. And equanimity: letting it happen without pushing it away or holding on.

The third is where the suffering goes. In Suffering = Pain × Resistance I wrote about his formula. The words make resistance easier to drop, because it’s much easier to stop fighting a squeeze in the chest than to stop fighting your future.

Programs that fit in a sentence

Here is where the bottom-up payoff shows. Once you have the vocabulary, the practices are tiny.

Noting: every few seconds, notice what’s most prominent and name where it is. See. Hear. Feel. That’s the whole instruction. After a few minutes, experience stops being a blur and becomes a stream of small, named events.

Note gone: whenever something vanishes, a sound stopping, a thought ending, a twinge fading, notice it and say “gone.” Do it for a while and you collect hundreds of small proofs that nothing stays. That’s impermanence, the teaching I wrote about in Buddhism Through the Lens of the Need, turned from an idea into something you can check.

Do nothing: let attention go wherever it wants, and don’t steer. Whenever you notice you’ve started steering, stop. That’s it. It’s the shortest instruction of the three and by far the hardest, which is another thing it has in common with Lisp.

None of these needs a belief, a ritual or a teacher in the room to get started. They need the words, and a few minutes.

Why it suits some brains

The classic instruction, watch the breath, asks you to keep looking at one thing and wait for it to be interesting. For an ADHD brain, that’s close to impossible.

Noting asks for something different. Whatever shows up, you name it, and then the next thing. There’s a small task every few seconds and nothing to wait for. My guess is that this suits restless minds unusually well. That’s a guess, not a trial result, but it’s the first meditation instruction I’ve read that seems written for a mind that won’t sit still.

What the language leaves out

A small language is powerful because of what it leaves out, and it’s worth knowing what that is. Shinzen’s vocabulary has no words for meaning, purpose or other people. It describes experience, not what to do with a life. That’s fine for a practice, but it isn’t the whole answer to the need, which, as I argued in Meeting the Need Yourself, includes parts that can’t be made alone.

The evidence for meditation is real but modest, and for Shinzen’s system specifically it’s thin. And intensive practice can sometimes stir up hard things, so it’s worth starting small.

The short version

Graham’s advice was to build the language up toward the program. Shinzen did that for the mind. Six small words, see, hear and feel, inner and outer, plus a word for rest. Big feelings come apart into small ones. And the practices written in that language are short enough to remember: name it, notice it’s gone, stop steering.

The right words make the program short. In this case they may also make it a lot less painful.

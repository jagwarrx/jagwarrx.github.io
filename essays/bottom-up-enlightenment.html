Bottom-Up Programming for Enlightenment

November 2026. 1,370 words.

Paul Graham has a line about Lisp that I’ve loved for years. In Lisp, he wrote, you don’t just write your program down toward the language, you also build the language up toward your program. You grow a small vocabulary that fits the problem, and then the program becomes short, because the words do most of the work.

Reading Shinzen Young’s The Science of Enlightenment, I had the odd feeling of reading a Lisp book. Shinzen doesn’t describe the mind in grand words like soul, ego or enlightenment. He builds a tiny language for experience, from the bottom, and then writes a handful of very short programs in it.

So this is an attempt to read meditation as a programmer would. It’s a metaphor, and I’ll push it further than it wants to go. But it’s a surprisingly good one.

The primitives

Every good language starts with a small set of primitives. Lisp has atoms and lists. Shinzen has three, and each comes in two flavors.

See, hear and feel. Each can be outer or inner. See outer is the room in front of you. See inner is a mental image: the face of the person you’re annoyed with. Hear outer is the traffic. Hear inner is mental talk: the voice saying you’ll never finish this essay. Feel outer is a body sensation: the chair, the itch. Feel inner is an emotional body sensation: the squeeze in the chest that comes with dread.

That’s it. Six bins. Everything you will ever experience goes in one of them, or several at once. It’s the “everything is a list” of the mind.

There’s also a value for nothing. Each bin has a rest state: a blank visual field, a quiet mind, a relaxed body. Any language needs a null, and in this one null is pleasant.

Why primitives matter

The point of bottom-up design is that big, scary problems turn out to be made of small, boring parts.

Take anxiety. Anxiety is a top-down word. It arrives as one huge thing, with your name on it, about your whole future. Now decompose it. There’s an image: you, failing, in front of someone. There’s some talk: “this is going to go badly.” And there’s a feeling: a tight band around the chest. Three streams. Each of them, on its own, is manageable. An image is just an image. A sentence is just a sentence. A squeeze is just a squeeze.

Programmers call this divide and conquer. A problem you can’t solve becomes several you can. The monster was never one thing. It was three small processes running at once, and it only looked like a monster because nobody had looked at the process list.

The compiler flags

Shinzen’s three core skills read naturally as settings you turn up.

Concentration is keeping the process on the CPU: staying with what you chose to look at instead of being preempted by every interrupt.

Sensory clarity is the logging level. At low verbosity, all you get is “bad feeling.” At high verbosity, you get exactly which bin, how big, where, and changing how. Most of the benefit of debugging is just turning the logging up.

Equanimity is the interesting one. It’s not interfering with the process while it runs. No killing it, no wrapping it in try-catch to hide the error, no blocking on it. Just letting it execute.

The bug

Every program has a famous bug, and this one is resistance.

In Suffering = Pain × Resistance I wrote about Shinzen’s formula. In programming terms, resistance is a spinlock. The process wants a lock, can’t get it, and instead of sleeping it sits in a tight loop asking, again and again: is it free yet? Is it free yet? It burns the whole CPU doing nothing useful.

That’s exactly what the need to know what comes next feels like from the inside. The mind spins on the open question. Is it going to be okay? Is it okay now? Pain is the wait. Resistance is the spinning. And suffering, as any profiler would tell you, is mostly the spinning.

Program one: the noting loop

The first program is noting, and it’s a read-eval-print loop. Attend to whatever’s most prominent, classify it, say the label, repeat, every few seconds.

(loop
  (let ((x (attend)))
    (label (bin x))))   ; see, hear, feel

That’s the whole thing. It looks too simple to do anything, which is also what people say about Lisp. What it does is turn the logging up and keep it up. After a few minutes, experience stops being a blur and starts being a stream of tokens.

Program two: note gone

The second program watches for deallocation. Whenever something you were experiencing vanishes, a sound stops, a thought ends, a twinge fades, you note it: gone.

(loop
  (when (vanished-p (attend))
    (label 'gone)))

This is a garbage collector you can watch. And watching it changes something. Do it for an hour and you have hundreds of pieces of evidence that nothing stays allocated. Buddhism calls this impermanence, and in Buddhism Through the Lens of the Need I argued it’s the most direct reply to the need there is. Note gone is the unit test for it.

Program three: do nothing

The third program is my favorite, because it’s the shortest program that does anything at all.

(loop)

Shinzen calls it Do Nothing. Let attention go wherever it goes. Don’t steer. And whenever you notice you’ve started steering, you’ve written a line inside the loop, so delete it. That’s the only instruction.

It’s much harder than it looks, which is the other thing people say about Lisp.

What compiles

So what happens when you run these long enough? Here the metaphor gets a little cosmic, so I’ll keep it short and honest.

Every program I’ve ever written has had a global variable named something like self or me, holding the state, read by everything. The claim of the tradition, put in these terms, is that after enough debugging you discover the global was never declared. Every function ran fine without it. The processes were reading from each other the whole time, and the sense of someone in charge was a log message written afterward. Michael Gazzaniga’s split-brain patients, confidently explaining actions the other half of their brain had caused, are a small, strange piece of evidence for that.

I haven’t run the program to completion, and I don’t have anyone’s logs. Shinzen describes the result as a stable shift in how the self is perceived. That’s a report, not a measurement. Treat it as the release notes, not the test suite.

A note for ADHD brains

The usual meditation instruction, watch the breath, is a polling loop. You check the same register, over and over, and wait for it to be interesting. For an ADHD brain that’s close to torture.

Noting is different. It’s interrupt-driven. Whatever fires, you handle. A new token every few seconds, a tiny task each time, no waiting. I suspect that’s why it suits brains that are interrupt-driven already. That’s my guess, not a trial result. But it’s the first meditation instruction I’ve read that was written for the hardware I actually have.

Known issues

Every release needs a known-issues section.

It’s a metaphor. The mind isn’t a computer, and stretching the analogy too far gets you nonsense. The evidence for meditation is real but modest, and for Shinzen’s system specifically it’s thin. And as I noted in the last essay, intensive practice can sometimes bring up things that are hard to handle, so run it in small batches, and with a teacher if the pain is large.

The short version

Build the language up toward the program. Shinzen’s language has six primitives, see, hear and feel, inner and outer, plus a null for rest. Big feelings decompose into small streams. Three settings, concentration, clarity and equanimity, decide how well the programs run. The famous bug is resistance, a spinlock on the open question. And the three main programs are short: label what fires, note what’s gone, and the empty loop.

Graham was right that the right language makes the program short. It may also make it a lot less painful to run.

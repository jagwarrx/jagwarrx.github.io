Happiness Can Be Designed, Partly

November 2026. 706 words.

In Power, Edge and Enough, the thing an edge really buys turned out to be a sense of control over your life. That raised three questions. What is that called? What else predicts happiness? And if we know, can happiness be designed?

A sense of control

The common term is locus of control, from Julian Rotter in the 1960s. People with an internal locus believe their own actions shape what happens to them. People with an external one believe luck, fate or other people decide. It’s what the Stoics meant by focusing on what’s in your power.

A few neighbors cover slightly different parts of it:

  • Self-efficacy, Albert Bandura’s term: confidence that you can do a specific thing.
  • Agency: the felt sense of being the one who acts, not someone things happen to.
  • Autonomy: feeling that your choices are your own.
  • Decision latitude: the term in the Whitehall research for how much say people have over their work.

The opposite is learned helplessness, Martin Seligman’s term. Repeated experiences of having no control teach you to stop trying, even after things change.

Three needs

Cal Newport, in So Good They Can’t Ignore You, borrows three needs from Self-Determination Theory, the work of the psychologists Edward Deci and Richard Ryan:

  • Autonomy: control over your day, and the sense that what you do matters.
  • Competence: being good at what you do.
  • Relatedness: connection to other people.

His point is about order. The direct route to all three is to get good at something rare and valuable. Competence comes first, and you can trade it for the other two. Each one connects back. Autonomy is the control from Whitehall. Competence is the mastery motive behind wanting an edge, measured against your past self instead of other people. Relatedness is what the Harvard study found matters most.

Three things in a day

There’s no official list for daily life, but research that tracks how people feel hour by hour keeps pointing to the same three things.

Connection. When Daniel Kahneman’s team had people reconstruct the previous day, time with friends and family rated as the most enjoyable, and commuting as the least.

Presence. Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert pinged thousands of people at random moments. They were thinking about something other than what they were doing 46.9 percent of the time, and they were less happy when they were. Whether the mind was wandering predicted happiness better than what the person was doing. A wandering mind is an unhappy mind. Training attention, as in Observation Is a Tool, is training for this one.

Progress. Teresa Amabile studied about twelve thousand daily work diaries. The biggest lift to people’s inner work life was making progress on meaningful work, and small wins counted.

Two of these are Newport’s: connection is relatedness, progress is competence. Presence is the extra one, whether you’re actually in your day while you live it. And the Kahneman study found that life circumstances, like job security or being married, had a fairly small effect on how people felt across the day. Securing your life matters. Day to day, how you spend each hour matters more.

Can happiness be designed?

Partly. You can design the conditions. You can’t design the feeling.

A famous pie chart said 50 percent of happiness is genes, 10 percent circumstances and 40 percent what you do. Later critics showed the 40 percent was likely a large overestimate. There is a share you can design, probably smaller than the chart says.

And aiming straight at the feeling backfires. Iris Mauss found that the more people value happiness, the more likely they are to feel disappointed. Better to build the conditions and let the feeling follow.

This is the stake and the rope from Principles Are Guiding Policy for a Life. Connection, presence and progress are the principles. The design work is turning each into a concrete policy:

  • Connection: a standing weekly meal with friends, not a vague plan to see people more.
  • Presence: the phone stays in another room during meals and conversations.
  • Progress: at the end of each day, name one small win.

Like balance, happiness works best as something you keep up and adjust, not something you capture once.

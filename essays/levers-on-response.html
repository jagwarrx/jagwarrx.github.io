The Levers on How You Respond

November 2026. 1,340 words.

The last few essays have been circling one idea. You can’t know what comes next, but you can know how you’ll meet it. In Know How You’ll Meet It that was an answer to the need. In How Many Responses You Need it became a card: one default and a few named responses.

But a card is only part of it. When something happens, what actually decides how you respond? Not just what you planned. Whether you slept, what was in front of you, how you read the moment, who was with you.

Those are levers, and there are about eight of them. The useful thing isn’t the list. It’s the order.

The map

The psychologist James Gross built the most widely used map of this, his process model of emotion regulation. Its key move is to arrange the ways we shape a response by when they act. Some act before the moment arrives. Some act during it. And Gross found that the early ones tend to work better and cost less than the late ones.

His sharpest finding was about the latest lever of all, suppression: feeling the reaction and holding it in. In his experiments, people told to hide their feelings while watching upsetting films showed no less distress inside, and their bodies showed more stress, not less. Holding it in is expensive. Changing how you read the situation, earlier, was both cheaper and more effective.

So the levers below run in time order: before, during, after.

Before the moment

The first lever is the situation itself. The cheapest way to handle a hard moment is not to walk into it, or to change it before it starts. Leaving the phone in another room. Having the difficult conversation in the morning rather than at the end of a long day. Gross calls these situation selection and situation modification, and they come first because they act before any reaction has begun.

The second is cues and friction. Much of what we do isn’t decided at all. Wendy Wood’s research found that about 43 percent of everyday actions are habits, done almost every day in the same context, set off by what’s around us. That means the environment is choosing a lot of your responses for you. You can use that. Put the cue for the response you want where you’ll see it, and add friction to the one you don’t. A few seconds of extra effort is often enough to break a habit loop.

The third is state. The same event gets a different response from a rested body than from a tired one. That isn’t a metaphor. In The Why Behind the Need I described how the body budget sets the baseline, and how Arie Kruglanski found that the need for quick closure rises with fatigue, time pressure and noise. Sleep, food and movement don’t just make you feel better. They change which response comes out.

The fourth is the repertoire: responses decided in advance. This is the card. Peter Gollwitzer’s work on implementation intentions, plans in the form “when this happens, I’ll do that,” finds that deciding the response ahead of time makes it much more likely to happen. Gary Klein found the same in experts. Fire commanders don’t weigh options under pressure. They recognize the situation, and the recognition brings the move with it.

In the moment

When the moment has arrived, there are three levers left, and they’re harder to pull.

The fifth is the gap: any delay between the event and the response. The fastest version is in the body. One long breath out, or the physiological sigh from Dealing with Anxiety When You Have ADHD, slows the heart and buys a few seconds. The slowest version is a rule, like waiting forty-eight hours before adopting a new system. Either way, the gap is where the other levers get a chance to work.

The sixth is attention: which part of the situation you look at, and from how far away. Ethan Kross found that talking to yourself by your own name, or as “you,” cools the reaction. It puts a little distance between you and the moment, the distance you’d have if it were happening to a friend.

The seventh is meaning: how you read what’s happening. Two findings stand out. Matthew Lieberman found that simply naming a feeling, “this is anxiety,” dampens the brain’s alarm. And Alison Wood Brooks found that before a stressful performance, people who said “I am excited” did better than people who tried to calm down. The arousal was the same. The reading changed what it did.

After

The eighth lever acts after the moment is over, and it’s the one most often skipped: review. What happened? What did I do? What would I do next time?

The U.S. Army made this a formal practice in the 1970s, the after-action review, run after every exercise, short and without blame. It matters because it’s the lever that updates the others. A review is where a repeated situation earns a named response, where a bad cue gets moved, where you notice the hard days were the short-sleep days.

Two that run through all of them

Other people. James Coan and colleagues put people in a scanner under threat of a mild shock. Holding a spouse’s hand reduced the brain’s threat response, and the better the relationship, the bigger the effect. His interpretation is that the brain treats trusted people as a resource it can count on, so it spends less on its own. That’s the secure base again, and it’s why working next to someone, or telling someone your plan, changes what you do.

Purpose. When no prepared response fits, a purpose picks one. “Get back to one next step” works where no script does. That’s the commander’s intent from the last two essays, and it works at every stage: it shapes which situations you choose, which cues you set up and how you read the moment.

Why the order matters

Put the levers in a line and a pattern shows up. The early ones are set once, while calm, and then work without effort. The late ones have to be pulled in the moment, under load, every time.

That’s why the common advice gets it backwards. Most of what people are told about handling hard moments is in-the-moment advice: stay calm, think it through, don’t react. Those are the levers that cost the most and fail first. The cheaper move is to do most of the work before the moment arrives, so that less depends on what you can manage when it does.

The ADHD version

For an ADHD brain the order matters even more, because the in-the-moment levers are exactly the ones ADHD is short of. Pausing, shifting attention and reframing all need working memory and inhibition, under load. That’s the hardest condition for an ADHD brain to work in.

So the weight goes on the first four. Choose the situation. Set the cues and the friction. Protect sleep. Write the card. Russell Barkley’s rule applies here as everywhere: put it outside you, at the point where it’s needed. A cue on the desk does more than a resolution in your head.

In the moment, start with the body. A long breath out works even when thinking doesn’t, and it buys the gap the other levers need.

And give review a fixed time, because it’s the lever an ADHD brain is most likely to skip. Ten minutes at the weekend is enough to ask what went wrong, and what to change before it happens again.

The short version

Eight levers, in the order they act. Before: the situation, cues and friction, your state, your prepared responses. During: the gap, attention, meaning. After: review. And two that run through all of them: other people and purpose.

The earlier the lever, the cheaper it is and the better it works. Most of how you’ll respond is decided before the moment arrives. That’s where to do the work.

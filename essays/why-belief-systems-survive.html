Why Belief Systems Survive

November 2026. 4,160 words.

I started with a simple question. What is the point of Hindu chants, the kalasam, the hand gestures in a puja? Is there any proof they do anything? Is it all psychological? And what do the old texts say?

Ask that and you usually get one of three answers. Believers say it’s science: vibrations, energy, something NASA once recorded. Skeptics say it’s superstition. Polite people say it’s psychological, which ends the conversation without anyone having to commit to anything.

None of these is very interesting. What I wanted to know first was whether there’s an internal logic. Whether it’s true is another matter. But a practice that has lasted three thousand years should at least hang together from the inside, and if it does, I wanted to see how.

It does. And following that logic led somewhere I didn’t expect: not to an answer about whether mantras work, but to a better way of asking what any belief system is.

The logic from the inside

The texts give a surprisingly consistent account of how ritual works. A few ideas carry most of the weight.

The first is correspondence. The Brahmanas, the ritual manuals attached to the Vedas, treat the sacrifice as a scale model of the universe. Act on the model and you act on the thing. A later saying puts it briefly: as in the body, so in the cosmos.

The second is that sound is close to reality itself. Words aren’t labels people stuck on things. The Mīmāṁsā school, which made a science of ritual, held that the link between a word and its meaning is inborn. No one made it. That’s why the Veda is called authorless: the seers heard it; they didn’t write it. If the bond between a word and a thing is built in, then the right sound reaches the thing directly. Tantra goes further. A mantra doesn’t stand for the deity. It is the deity’s body.

The third is precision. If sound does the work, the exact sound matters, the way the exact characters matter in code. The texts tell a story to make the point. A father performs a rite to get a son who will kill Indra. He chants the word indraśatru with the accent on the wrong syllable, and the compound flips: instead of “killer of Indra” it means “whose killer is Indra.” The son is born, and Indra kills him.1 A phonetics manual draws the moral: a mantra with a wrong accent or letter doesn’t carry its meaning. It becomes a thunderbolt of speech and strikes the person chanting it.

The fourth is the delayed result. Mīmāṁsā said a correct ritual creates an unseen potency, apūrva, that bears fruit later, the way a seed does. The radical part is that the gods are almost unnecessary. The ritual works by itself. The god is just the one named in the mantra.

The rest are applications. The kalasam is a container: a pot of water, mango leaves and a coconut into which the deity is invited. The verse recited over it places Viṣṇu at its mouth, Rudra at its neck, Brahmā at its base, and all the oceans and rivers and the four Vedas inside. Then the charged water is sprinkled, or poured over an idol. Invoke, hold, transfer. Mudras, in temple worship, are steps in a protocol: one gesture invites the deity in, another turns water into nectar. And there’s a rule for the worshipper too. To worship a god, become the god. The priest places mantras on parts of his own body until it is the deity’s.

Put together, this reads less like superstition and more like a specification. There’s a theory of what the world is made of (sound), how to act on it (exact sound), how the action travels (correspondence, containers) and when the result arrives (later). You can reject every premise. But it hangs together.

Four levels of sound

The most interesting part of the theory is about speech itself. A verse in the Rig Veda says speech has four quarters: three are hidden, and humans speak the fourth. Later thinkers turned this into levels.

At the bottom is spoken speech, breath shaped by the mouth. Above that is inner speech, the words in your head, still in order but silent. Above that is what the grammarian Bhartṛhari called paśyantī, “the seeing”: the meaning grasped all at once, before words, while word and meaning are still one. The Kashmir Śaivas added a fourth level above it: pure awareness, the sense of “I” before there is anything to say.

The line that matters is sequence. Above inner speech, everything is at once. Below it, one word follows another.

Modern psychology describes speaking in a similar way. In Willem Levelt’s model, speech goes from a wordless “preverbal message” to grammatical form to the motor plan of the mouth. And anyone who has had a word on the tip of their tongue knows the “seeing” level exists. The meaning is fully there. The word isn’t.

The theory also explains a practice. Speaking moves outward, from awareness to sound. Japa, repeating a mantra, reverses the path. That’s why the manuals have you chant aloud, then whisper, then repeat silently, and why Manu rates silent repetition a thousand times above a sacrifice.

What can be measured

So what does science say?

Some effects are real. Chanting slows breathing. A study in the BMJ found that reciting mantras, and the rosary too, brought breathing down to about six breaths a minute, which improves the body’s regulation of heart rate and blood pressure.2 Humming raises the nitric oxide in the nose about fifteen times. Repeating a word steadily seems to quiet the parts of the brain that wander. Rituals lower anxiety, and chanting or moving in time with other people makes them trust each other more.

What no one has found is the mechanism the texts describe. No one has measured charged water, or energy sealed by a hand gesture. And the specific sound doesn’t seem to matter much. When Herbert Benson studied meditation in the 1970s, he found that repeating the word “one” worked about as well as a Sanskrit mantra.

So the answer to “is it all psychological?” is that the parts you can measure are psychological, physiological and social. But psychological doesn’t mean fake. Slow breathing, steady attention and a group moving together are real things with real effects. They just aren’t the effects claimed.

There are two other powers of sound that are fully proven, though neither is the one the texts mean.

One is storage. The Vedas were preserved by chanting them in scrambled orders, word pairs forward and back, so that any slip would break a pattern and be caught. It worked. The Rig Veda has come down by mouth for about three thousand years with almost no variation. As a way of storing data without writing, it may be the best ever built.

The other is social. The philosopher J. L. Austin pointed out that some sentences don’t describe anything. They do something. “I now pronounce you married.” Once the words are said by the right person in the right setting, the couple is married, and everyone treats them that way. A wedding mantra works exactly like this. It really does make a marriage. It works through people agreeing, not through vibration.

John Searle later gave the general form: X counts as Y in context C. These words, said by this person, at this fire, count as a marriage. He calls the result an institutional fact. Money, property and borders are institutional facts too. Much of what a priest produces is this kind of fact, and it’s as real as money.

The tradition already argued about this

What surprised me most is that “it’s all psychological” isn’t a modern putdown. It’s an old Hindu position.

The schools disagreed about how ritual works. Mīmāṁsā said it works automatically, through the unseen potency. Advaita Vedānta said ritual purifies the mind but can’t free you, which is a psychological account. The Yoga Sūtra says to repeat Om while dwelling on its meaning, and the mind will turn inward: psychological again. Tantra said it works through the subtle body. Two of the four explained ritual mostly through the mind.

And there were harsher critics. A scholar named Kautsa argued that mantras are meaningless. The Buddha compared Brahmins reciting the Vedas to a line of blind men, each holding on to the one in front. The Cārvāka materialists said the Vedas were written by buffoons, knaves and demons, and that the fire ritual was a livelihood for people with no brains and no spine.3

So the tradition carried its own skeptics. That turns out to matter.

The logic came later

Here is what changes the picture. The practices are old. Vedic ritual goes back three thousand years or more. The theories that explain it are much younger. Mīmāṁsā was systematized in the last centuries BCE. Bhartṛhari wrote in the fifth century CE. Abhinavagupta, the great theorist of Tantra, wrote around the year 1000.

So most of the internal logic is a rationalization. People did these things for a long time, and then thinkers built more and more refined accounts of why they worked.

That raises a harder question. Is the whole system an artifact of thousands of years of worship: a structure of ideas that bound societies, kept loyalties and propagated itself?

Largely, yes. Look at its features the way you’d look at an organism’s, and you find ones that help it survive. The result arrives later and unseen, so it can never be shown not to arrive. If a ritual fails, the performer got it wrong; the system never does. The Veda has no author, so there’s no author to question. Mantras have to be received from a guru, so outsiders can’t test them. Rites mark every stage of life from birth to death, so there’s no moment when you’re outside. And rivals get absorbed: the Buddha, who rejected the Vedas, was made an avatar of Viṣṇu.

Philosophers of science have names for these moves. Karl Popper called a theory unfalsifiable when no possible observation could count against it, and a result that arrives later and unseen is a clean example. Imre Lakatos noticed that even good scientific programs protect a hard core with a belt of auxiliary hypotheses, and blame each failure on the belt. “The accent was wrong” is an auxiliary hypothesis. And the Duhem–Quine point is that every test checks a theory together with all its assumptions, so a failure can always be pinned on an assumption instead. A rite that needs the right priest, the right hour, the right materials and the right state of mind has a great many assumptions to blame.

It was also gated, and the gatekeepers lived off it. One law book says that a Śūdra who deliberately listens to the Veda should have molten tin poured in his ears.4 Priests were paid fees set by texts the priests kept. Kings got legitimacy and priests got land. Max Weber called this social closure: a group raises its rewards by limiting who may enter.

But I think the usual image for this is wrong. People call systems like this cathedrals, and a cathedral has an architect. Hinduism never had one. No pope, no council, no fixed canon, and schools that fought each other for centuries. It’s more like a coral reef: built up by accretion, with no plan, shaped by what survived. The joins get grown over, so that later it looks designed.

There’s a field that studies how reefs like this grow: cultural evolution, built by Robert Boyd, Peter Richerson and Joseph Henrich. Its basic claim is that ideas are copied with biases, and the biases decide what spreads. Content bias: some ideas are simply easier to remember and pass on. Pascal Boyer found that the stickiest religious ideas are minimally counterintuitive, ordinary in every way but one, like a pot of water that holds a god. Prestige bias: people copy those with status, and the Brahmin’s status was what the gate protected. Conformity bias: people copy what most people around them do, which is why a rite everyone performs is so hard to drop. And credibility-enhancing displays, Henrich’s term for costly acts that make a belief believable. A priest who keeps exhausting rules of purity is evidence, to the people watching, that he believes what he says. Richard Sosis found the same pattern in nineteenth-century American communes: the religious ones outlasted the secular ones, and among the religious ones, those that demanded the most lasted longest.

And a reef is alive. Selection works on two levels here: on groups, which hold together better with shared rites, and on individuals. Some of what survived did so because it helped the person doing it, not just the group: slow breathing, steady attention, a shape for grief. Some of it is real intellectual work. Linguists still admire Pāṇini’s grammar. Bhartṛhari had ideas about meaning that the philosophy of language reached more than a thousand years later.

A case study: astrology

Astrology shows the reef growing.

The horoscope, with its twelve signs and its houses, isn’t originally Indian. It was put together in Hellenistic Alexandria from Babylonian, Egyptian and Greek parts, and it reached India with the sea trade around the second or third century CE. The key text is the Yavanajātaka, “the horoscopy of the Greeks.” The evidence is still in the vocabulary. Horā is the Greek hōra. Kendra, the angular houses, is kentron. Drekkāṇa is dekanos, the decan. The seventh house, jāmitra, is diametron. The sixth-century astronomer Varāhamihira gave the sign names in Greek forms and was candid about the source: the Greeks are foreigners, he wrote, but this science is well established among them, so even they are honored like sages.5

The import brought a problem with it. Greek astrology rested on fate: the planets drive events. India already had karma: your life is shaped by your own past actions. If the stars decide, what does karma do?

The solution was elegant. Varāhamihira’s book on horoscopes opens by saying that this science reveals the ripening of the good and bad karma built up in another birth, as a lamp reveals objects in the dark.6 The planets don’t cause anything. They show. You’re born at the moment the sky matches the karma you bring, so the chart is a kind of receipt. And it covers only the karma that has begun to ripen in this life. What you do now is still yours.

The West made the same move a few centuries earlier. Plotinus argued that the stars don’t cause events but signify them, like letters written in the sky. A theory of signs survives criticisms that would sink a theory of causes, because nobody has to explain how a planet pushes anything.

It even settled the old argument about fate and effort. A law book says success depends on both, and then explains: fate is just the effort of a previous body, made visible.7

But notice what this did. It made the chart impossible to refute. Astrologers later added a split between fixed karma and karma that can be changed. If a remedy works, the karma wasn’t fixed. If it fails, it was. Every outcome confirms the theory. The imported system took on the local theology and became true by definition. It’s no accident that astrology was Popper’s own example of a pseudoscience.

This may be part of why astrology did so much better in India than in Christian Europe, where it clashed with free will and grace, and Augustine attacked it. In India it fit. Today it guards the gate of arranged marriage. Meanwhile the tests that have been done, including one in Pune where astrologers tried to tell gifted children from mentally disabled ones by their charts, come out at chance.8

Why does it feel accurate anyway? In 1949 Bertram Forer gave his students what he said were personal readings, all identical and copied largely from an astrology book. They rated their accuracy 4.26 out of 5. A reading that fits everyone feels written for you. Psychologists call it the Barnum effect, and together with confirmation bias, which makes the hits memorable and the misses forgettable, it does most of an astrologer’s work.

Were the priests running a racket?

It’s tempting to say the Brahmins had the bullshit jobs of their age. I don’t think that’s right, at least not in David Graeber’s sense. A bullshit job is one so pointless that even the person doing it can’t justify it. Most priests believed in their work.

And much of what Brahmins did was real. Before writing, they were the libraries. They kept the calendar, did astronomy, wrote grammar and law, and served as ministers. Most Brahmins weren’t priests at all. Manu lists temple priests who live off the idol alongside physicians and meat sellers, as men not to invite to a rite for the ancestors.9 The prestige was in learning, not in ritual for pay.

What they did run was a guild with a monopoly, and part of their income came from controlling access rather than producing anything. Economists, following Gordon Tullock and Anne Krueger, call that rent-seeking. Lawyers, academics and consultants are partly in the same business.

What they often sold was meaning. That sounds cynical, but it’s a real service. You can’t marry yourself. Some meanings need a witness other people recognize. Death rites give grief a shape and an end date. An auspicious time takes away one decision. Malinowski noticed that Trobriand Islanders used no magic when fishing in their safe lagoon and a great deal of it on the open sea. People pay for meaning where they can’t control the outcome. Ellen Langer later showed the general tendency in the lab and called it the illusion of control: when an outcome is uncertain, people act as if their own actions can sway it.

The question is whether the seller creates the need. A funeral that gives closure is a service. A planetary “defect” in your marriage chart that calls for an expensive remedy is a racket. There’s a simple test for any seller of meaning, old or new. Did they create the need? Could I do this without them? What does leaving cost me?

The real harm, I think, wasn’t that the work was useless. It was that the role was hereditary and the knowledge was closed. A bullshit job wastes the person doing it. A closed guild wastes everyone it keeps out.

Follow the money?

So does “follow the money” explain it? Or “follow the power”?

Each explains a part. Money explains the remedies, the gemstones, the temple economy, the apps. Power explains caste, the gate around the Veda, the legitimacy of kings, and karma’s convenient account of why some are born high and some low. But neither explains where the ideas came from, or why people practice them for free: the grandmother doing japa, the household puja nobody is paid for.

And both lenses share a trap. They assume that whoever benefits from an idea created it. Often not. In one Upaniṣad, the doctrine of rebirth is taught by a king to a Brahmin, and the king says that this knowledge has never before gone to Brahmins.10 Brahmins later profited from karma. In the tradition’s own story, they didn’t invent it. Daniel Dennett adds a stranger possibility: sometimes the main beneficiary of an idea is the idea itself, the way a cold benefits from your sneeze.

What works better is to ask one question at a time and use the right lens for each. Where did it come from? Follow the transmission: texts, loanwords, trade routes. Why do people want it? Follow the need. Who sells it? Follow the money. Whom does it protect, and whom does it shut out? Follow the power. Why did it outlast its rivals? Follow the selection. Does it work as claimed? Follow the evidence. And who argued against it? Follow the dissent, because critics from the inside keep the story honest.

The order matters. Need comes first. Money and power ride on a demand that’s already there; they rarely create it from nothing. Start with them and you miss half the picture, and you end up treating believers as dupes, which is both unkind and usually wrong.

Who needs it, and why

If I had to keep only one of those questions, it would be the need. Who needs this, and why? It’s the question that explains the most per word, and it’s worth running through every practice in turn.

Who needs japa? The anxious, the grieving, the person who can’t sleep. They need something to do with a restless mind, and repeating a mantra gives them slow breathing and one fixed point to attend to. Nobody has to sell it to them. That’s why it survives with no money attached.

Who needs the kalasam? A group marking a beginning: a new temple, a new house, a wedding. They need the invisible to become something they can look at, carry and pour. A pot of water is something a crowd can watch together. Durkheim called the charge a crowd feels at moments like this collective effervescence. Harvey Whitehouse adds a distinction: doctrinal rites are frequent and routine, like daily japa, and carry a body of teaching; imagistic rites are rare and intense, and bind the people who were there. A temple’s consecration, with the kalasam poured over the tower about once every twelve years, is the second kind.

Who needs mudras? The priest, first. A long rite done from memory needs steps, and gestures make the steps exact and easy to recall. They also show the people watching that something is happening.

Who needs the exact accent and the scrambled recitation? A culture that wanted to keep its texts without writing them down.

Who needs astrology? A family about to make the biggest and least certain decision it makes: whom a child will marry. They need cover for the choice, and sometimes a polite way to say no. And someone in a bad stretch of life needs an explanation and something to do about it.

Who needs karma? Here the answer splits, and that’s where power comes in. The suffering need a reason for their suffering. The fortunate need one for their fortune, and karma tells them it was earned. Max Weber called karma the most consistent theodicy history has produced: an answer to why the innocent suffer that leaves nothing over. The same idea meets two opposite needs, and only one of them is innocent.

Who needs the priest? The bereaved, who need grief given a shape. The couple, who need a witness. The king, who needs legitimacy.

The frame does more than explain. It sorts service from rent. Where the need is real and you can meet it yourself, like japa, the practice stays free. Where the need is real but needs a witness, like a wedding or a funeral, a seller appears, and that’s fair. Where the seller has to create the need first, like the planetary defect that calls for a costly remedy, it’s a racket.

And it predicts. When the need goes, the practice goes with it. The great Vedic sacrifices, which made kings, are now rare, because kings stopped needing them. Japa, which meets a need everyone has, is everywhere. If more marriages come to be chosen by the couple rather than arranged by the family, horoscope matching should weaken with them, and the astrologers will have to find another gate.

Three questions, kept apart

Where I’ve ended up is that most of the confusion here comes from mixing three questions.

Why did it spread? Mostly for social reasons: identity, loyalty, power, gatekeeping and beauty.

Does it do anything? Yes, to the body and the mind. Not, as far as anyone has shown, to the world outside.

Is the metaphysics true? That’s open, and knowing how a belief spread doesn’t settle it. Judging a belief by its origin is the genetic fallacy. Science also spreads through prestige, gatekeeping and careers, and it’s still mostly right.

Keep the questions apart and a lot of the argument goes away. The believer and the skeptic are often answering different questions. The believer is right that it does something. The skeptic is right that it doesn’t do what it says. Both miss the most interesting part: a system of ideas that tuned itself for millennia to survive, built its logic afterwards, and still carries real insights about language and the mind.

I write here a lot about systems I build for myself, and the same warning applies to them. A system can grow until it feels true just because it has been around and can explain anything. The defense is the same as with any reef: know what you need from it, check whether it delivers, and listen to whoever says it doesn’t.

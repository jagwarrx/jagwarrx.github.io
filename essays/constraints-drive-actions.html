Constraints Drive Actions

November 2026. 801 words.

When I don’t do something I meant to do, the first explanation that comes to mind is about me. I’m lazy. I’m distracted. I don’t want it enough. It feels honest, and it’s almost never useful, because it points at the one thing I can’t change by Monday.

The more useful explanation is about constraints. What I do is mostly decided by the limits I’m working inside, long before I decide anything.

Properties and conditions

There’s a simple idea from physics I keep coming back to. A steel ball has the same properties on a table and in free fall. Its behaviour is completely different. Properties tell you how something responds. The conditions decide what it actually does.

People work the same way. “Lazy” is a property. It describes a result and explains nothing. Put the same person in different conditions and the result changes. A useful test: would someone else, in exactly my situation, do the same thing? If yes, the cause is the situation, not me.

How constraints become actions

A constraint is a limit: a budget, a deadline, the hours in a day, what’s on the screen. Every constraint creates a question. A budget makes you ask how to stay inside it. A deadline makes you ask what can be done by then. The questions you’re forced to ask decide what you decide, and what you decide becomes what you do.

Then there are defaults: what happens when nobody decides. Most of the day runs on defaults. A calendar’s default is that empty time is free time, so meetings fill it, and the deep work that has no slot never happens. Nobody chose that. The structure did.

So the chain runs: constraints and defaults, then questions, then decisions, then actions. By the time you’re acting, most of the choice is already made.

The constraints I have to design around

In Own the list, rent the app I named some of mine. This is the fuller list.

Out of sight, out of mind. Anything not in front of me stops existing. If a task is buried, I won’t do it.

No sense of time passing. Ten minutes and two hours feel the same from the inside. I don’t notice a task running long, or a deadline getting close, unless something outside me shows it. There’s no feeling for the future either: everything is in the present.

Novelty-driven attention. My attention goes to what’s new, not to what’s important. If a task is boring and something else is new, I’ll do the new thing.

Dopamine needs. I need some payoff along the way. A task that only pays at the end loses to anything that pays now.

And a few others. Every decision at the moment of capture makes capture less likely: if filing it takes three decisions, I won’t write it down. Starting is the hardest part of any task. And I can hold only a few started things at once.

For years I treated these as flaws to fix with effort. They behave much more like gravity. They’re there every day, they don’t respond to effort, and they predict what I’ll do far better than my intentions do.

Once I wrote them down as constraints instead of failings, they became design inputs. The important things go where I can see them. Time goes where I can see it too: a block of work has a length, and a timer runs while I’m in it. Novelty gets spent on the surface, where it’s cheap. The fun of a task is saved for when I start it, and progress gets counted where I can see it. Capture takes no decisions at all. The first step is small enough to begin. None of that needs more willpower. It needs the constraints to point the right way.

The one that binds

Not every constraint matters at once. At any moment there’s usually one that limits the result, and everything else is noise. If I have three hours and no idea what to start, the constraint isn’t time, it’s clarity. If I know exactly what to do and I’m exhausted, it’s energy. Fixing anything other than the binding constraint feels productive and changes nothing.

So the question I try to ask, when the same bad result keeps repeating, is not “what’s wrong with me?” It’s “what limit is making this the obvious thing to do?”

What this doesn’t mean

It doesn’t mean people don’t matter. Two people in the same situation still act differently, because skill, character and effort differ. But those differences are better treated as constraints too: fixed facts you design around, not verdicts you argue with.

And it doesn’t mean you’re stuck. Constraints drive actions, but many constraints can be changed. That’s the subject of the next essay.

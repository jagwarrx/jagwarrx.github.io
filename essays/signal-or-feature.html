Signal or Feature

November 2026. 1,160 words.

Almost every tradition that has thought hard about human life has noticed the same thing. Nothing quite satisfies. The good things fade, the new thing stops being new, and we go looking for the next one. In Buddhism Through the Lens of the Need I called it the treadmill, and traced it to a brain that stops responding to rewards it can predict.

The traditions agree on the observation. Where they split is on what it means. And that split, I think, is the deepest fork in how people answer the need.

Is the restlessness a signal, pointing to something real that we lack? Or is it a feature, the way the machine is built, pointing to nothing at all?

The signal view

The signal view says the restlessness is information. We keep wanting because there’s something we’re made for and haven’t found, and nothing else will fill the space it’s meant to fill.

Augustine opens his Confessions with the classic statement of it: you have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you. Pascal, thirteen centuries later, described an infinite abyss in us that can only be filled by something infinite and unchanging. Ecclesiastes says God has set eternity in the human heart. Islam speaks of fiṭra, an innate disposition toward God that every person is born with. The Persian poet Rumi opens his great poem with a reed flute, cut from its reed bed, whose music is the sound of its longing to return. And in Hindu devotion, viraha, the ache of separation from the beloved god, is treated not as a problem but as the heart of love.

C. S. Lewis turned it into an argument. Creatures aren’t born with desires unless satisfaction for those desires exists. A baby feels hunger, and there is food. If I find in myself a desire which no experience in this world can satisfy, he wrote, the most probable explanation is that I was made for another world.

The feature view

The feature view says the restlessness is how the mind is built, and points to nothing beyond it.

Buddhism is the oldest version. Thirst, taṇhā, is the cause of suffering, and the way out isn’t to find the right object but to stop needing one. Evolutionary psychology gives a modern version. Robert Wright argues that natural selection made pleasure fade so we’d keep striving, because an animal that stayed satisfied would stop eating, mating and climbing. Neuroscience gives the mechanism: Wolfram Schultz’s dopamine neurons respond to the unexpected and go quiet for what’s predicted. On this view, the restlessness would be there whatever we found. It isn’t a sign that something is missing. It’s a sign that the machine is running.

Can we tell which?

This is the uncomfortable part. Both views predict exactly the same experience. If the restlessness is a signal, nothing in this world satisfies for long. If it’s a feature, nothing in this world satisfies for long. The evidence we can collect from inside a life is the same either way.

In Why Belief Systems Survive I described the Duhem–Quine problem: an observation never tests a single claim, only a claim together with its background. This is a clean case of it. The feeling of restlessness fits both packages equally well.

The signal view does make one claim the feature view doesn’t: that some satisfaction exists that would finally last. But Lewis places it in another world, and the mystics who report tasting it in this one usually describe it as passing. William James, studying mystical experience, listed transience as one of its four marks. So the decisive evidence, if there is any, isn’t available on this side.

The feature view has the advantage of economy. It explains the restlessness with machinery we already know exists, and needs nothing more. The signal view would point out that economy is a preference, not a proof, and that an explanation of how a desire works doesn’t settle what it’s for. I don’t think the question can be closed from here, and I’d be suspicious of anyone who says it can.

Where they agree

What’s striking is how much the two views agree on in practice.

Both say: stop expecting the things of this world to fill it. Augustine and the Buddha would give the same warning about the new house, the promotion and the perfect system. They won’t do it. On the signal view, because they’re the wrong object. On the feature view, because no object can. Either way, the person who keeps buying the next thing to finally satisfy the restlessness is making the same mistake.

That’s worth noticing, because it’s exactly the mistake the market for the need is built on. Almost every product, from a phone to a productivity method, is sold as the thing that will finally be enough. Both views say it won’t be.

Where they split

They split on what to do with the restlessness that’s left.

The signal view says direct it. Turn the longing toward its true object, through prayer, devotion and love. The restlessness becomes the engine of a spiritual life, and the ache of separation is the beginning of the return.

The feature view says release it. See the craving arise, don’t obey it, and let it pass. The restlessness becomes something to understand and loosen, and the aim is equanimity, not union.

There may be a third option, compatible with the feature view, which is to use it. If the restlessness was built to keep us moving, it can be pointed at things that compound, rather than at things that only relieve. The engine stays on. What changes is what it’s connected to.

The fine print on each

By the test from What Each Religion Sells, each view has its costs.

The signal view’s risk is that someone will always claim to sell the true object. If you believe the longing points to something real, you’re open to anyone who says they have it: the guru, the movement, the final system. The longing is real, and so is the market for it.

The feature view’s risk is flatness. If the restlessness means nothing, so might everything it once pointed toward: beauty, love, awe. Taken badly, it becomes a reason to stop wanting anything, which is the spiritual bypassing from the Buddhism essay, or plain resignation.

The short version

Every tradition sees that nothing satisfies for long. The deep question is why. The signal view says we were made for something we haven’t found. The feature view says the mind is built to keep wanting, whatever it finds. Both fit the evidence we can collect, and the question can’t be closed from inside a life.

But both agree on the practical point. Nothing you can buy will finally be enough. The difference is only in what you do with the wanting that’s left: direct it, release it, or put it to work.

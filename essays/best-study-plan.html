Is There a Best Possible Study Plan for a Subject?

November 2026. 777 words.

Every time I start a new subject, I spend the first days looking for the perfect plan. The right course, the right book, the right order. It feels like the most important decision, because a bad plan could waste months. So is there a best possible study plan for a subject?

No single one. But there’s a best plan for a given goal and a given person, and the good ones share the same structure. Knowing that structure helped me more than any course I found.

Why there’s no single best plan

“Best” needs a goal. The best plan to pass an exam is not the best plan to do the job, and neither is the best plan to understand the field deeply. Same subject, three different plans.

It also needs a person. What you already know decides where you start. Your constraints decide what you’ll actually do: how much time you have, how your attention works, what bores you. A plan that ignores those is the best plan for somebody else.

So the question changes. Not “what’s the best plan?” but “what’s the best plan for this goal, for me, now?” That one has an answer.

What good plans share

They start from the end. What should you be able to do when you’re done? Not “know about databases” but “choose a database for a real system and defend the choice.” Write that as a test you could fail. Everything in the plan is there to pass it, and anything that doesn’t help comes out.

They map the subject before walking it. A subject is a set of concepts that depend on each other. Some unlock everything after them; some are details you can look up when you need them. Draw the map, learn the foundations first, and the rest gets cheaper, the same way bottom-up programs get shorter as the language grows. Most of the leverage is in a small number of concepts.

They pair concepts with cases. A concept alone doesn’t stick. A concept with two or three real cases, compared and contrasted, does, because the contrast shows you where the idea stops working. This is how I learn business, from sources like Commoncog that put a concept next to the companies it explains.

They test more than they teach. Rereading feels like learning and mostly isn’t. Answering a question without the book open is. So a good plan is full of retrieval: questions, flashcards, explaining it out loud, being questioned by someone who keeps asking why. Writing an essay about a topic is the hardest test I know, which is half the reason I write them.

They make room for what can’t be read. Some of every subject is tacit: judgement, taste, how an expert actually goes about it. You get that by watching people do the work and by doing it yourself on real problems. Someone on LessWrong collected tacit knowledge videos for every subject for exactly this reason.

They check and adjust. Run the end test early and often. When you fail a part, that part of the map gets more time. The plan is a guess that gets corrected, not a contract.

The plan for my brain

For me there’s one more piece, and it’s the same split as the spine and the surface. The map of concepts and the end test are the spine. They don’t change. The formats are the surface: a video one day, flashcards the next, an explainer, a Socratic session, a small project. When a format gets boring, I switch it and keep going through the same map. Most of the study plans I abandoned didn’t fail because the content was wrong. They failed because they were the same format for six weeks.

That’s also why the search for the perfect course is a trap. A course is one map plus one format. If the format stops working for you, it feels like the whole subject stopped working.

The answer

With AI, building a course for one person now takes an afternoon. I built one for solution architecture this week, shaped around the job I want to do with it. So the plan itself is no longer the scarce part.

What’s left is what no plan can do for you: choosing the end test, doing the retrieval, and coming back the next day. The best possible study plan is a clear test, a good map, concepts with cases, a lot of questions, and a surface that keeps changing so you keep showing up.

Whether you can find that plan objectively, by modelling memory and formats and testing the result, is the next essay.

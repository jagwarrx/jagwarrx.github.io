Three Layers of Dysregulation

November 2026. 1188 words.

Jenna Free is a counselor who has ADHD herself. Her claim: a lot of what I blame on my ADHD brain gets much worse when my nervous system is stuck in fight-or-flight. She calls that dysregulation. My body can’t tell a long to-do list from a bear, so it reacts to the list the way it would to the bear.

It reacts in four ways. Fight: snapping. Flight: avoiding, scrolling, every chore except the real one. Freeze: forty-five minutes thinking about a shower, or needing the exact right task first and so doing none. Fawn: saying yes to everything.

It runs as a cycle. I live in the red, frantic and rushing, then crash into the blue, with no energy for anything. The crash seems to prove I have to go hard while I can, so the cycle keeps going. The goal is the middle. Fear, guilt and urgency aren’t the only things that can move me. They’re just the only ones I can feel while I’m dysregulated.

She works on it in three layers, in order: body, thoughts, behavior. The order matters. Behavior is the last thing to change, so I don’t start there.

1. The body

Know my signs. Pick one to three: rushing, tense shoulders, a racing heart, a knotted stomach, racing thoughts, rumination, negative self-talk. When one shows up, label it: “oh, I’m dysregulated.” Be curious, not judging. Compulsive scrolling, bingeing shows or shopping count too. That’s soothing, not just a dopamine hunt.

Interrupt it physically. Slow down. Relax my shoulders. Take one deep breath. Look at what’s in front of me. I can’t talk my nervous system out of it. I can only act like there’s no bear.

Keep doing the task. This is the part I’d get wrong. Leaving to do yoga or grab a coffee teaches my body that work is the unsafe place. Breathe, and keep typing.

Count reps, not wins. I slow down at the start of the hallway and I’m rushing again by the end. That still counts as a rep.

Three mantras. One thing at a time. Rushing is never helpful. If I have to rush, there isn’t time, so leave the extra task and go.

Two pictures. I see the day like Tetris, every task stacked at once. It’s actually a line, one thing after another. I see a big task as a brick wall, every step due now. Lay it flat into a path and step on the first brick. The first brick doesn’t have to be the best one. It’s just a brick.

Regulate the calendar too. Spread appointments across the week instead of stacking them. Leave gaps so I’m not rushing. Take the lunch break. Keep a night or two open for whatever comes up.

2. The thoughts

She splits life into potential (shoulds, coulds, judging myself, over-planning) and reality (what’s actually in front of me). Potential costs as much energy as acting, and I get nothing for it.

The tool: “The reality is ___. Now what?” The reality is I haven’t started the presentation. Now what? Open it and make the title slide. “Now what” points at only the next step.

Watch for the three traps. Thinking about doing instead of doing: lists aren’t the goal, action is. Fighting reality: “I should have done five, not three.” Obsessing over efficiency: an hour planning a task that takes forty-five minutes. The giveaway words are could, should, have to, regret and what if.

Think neutral. “The floor has crumbs” instead of “why am I so lazy.” I sweep or I don’t. Most of the stress is the judgment on top of the task, not the task.

Swap beliefs. Don’t ask whether a belief is true. Ask whether it’s regulating. “I’m behind” becomes “I am where I am.” Others: “There is the time there is.” “The list will never end, so there’s no rush.” Put one on one sticky note, not thirteen, and move it now and then so it doesn’t go invisible.

Zoom out. When I’m lost in small stuff, ask what actually matters. Her clients’ two usual answers: connection with people, and being present for my own life.

3. The behavior

Here each ADHD symptom gets read as a sign of dysregulation, not a flaw.

All or nothing. Pick one practice for the week. Chip away: unload just the utensils. Cut corners on purpose: write down the task’s purpose, then ask what I can cut. Act before planning: hands on the keyboard, rough draft. Aim for continuation, not consistency. Missed a day? “Let me get back to that.”

Overcompensating. Quadruple-checking every email is the same pain in the other direction. Replace it with an accommodation, one light support. One list, not ten. Three alarms, not twenty-three. One calendar, not three. The test: is the energy worth the result, and is the tool solving the problem or making a new one? Say “I’ll get back to you” instead of a quick yes. My problem is usually overcommitting, not follow-through.

Limbo. Body on the couch, mind in the shower. Pick a lane: take my body to the task, or bring my mind back and rest fully. No bargaining (“fine, but tomorrow I’ll do everything”).

The avoidance cycle. Dread, discomfort, avoid, relief, and the pattern sets. Break it: name what I’m avoiding and why, breathe, take one step (just log in), then notice the step instead of saying it barely made a dent.

The urgency cycle. A thought feels like it must be done now, so I jump. Stay put instead, even if I’m unfocused for a bit. If it’s fear of forgetting, sort it. Top tier, real consequences: act now or set an alarm. Middle tier: jot it in the calendar. Bottom tier: let it go, close the tabs.

Let it be easier. Boundaries only I have to keep: “I don’t answer work calls after 5,” not “don’t call me after 5.” A quitting time. Ramp-up time at the start of the day, wrap-up time at the end.

Rebelling against my own rules. Drop the shoulds. There’s nothing I have to do. Ask what my calm self wants, and own the consequences either way.

The check-in

Her six questions, for when dysregulation is winning:

  1. Am I regulated?
  2. Am I in reality?
  3. Am I zoomed out?
  4. Am I thinking flexibly?
  5. Am I in the avoidance or urgency cycle?
  6. Am I letting it be easier?

Where it fits

Freezing over which task to pick is a body problem before it’s a choice problem. “There’s a right or wrong choice” is on her list of dysregulating beliefs, and her line is that success comes from making decisions, not from which decisions I make. So breathe first, then pick any reasonable brick.

Blocks give the work a container. This is what gets me to start one, and what keeps me in it when the tension rises: breathe and keep going, don’t leave. The avoidance cycle is also how the Wall of Awful grows.

And one tool at a time. She calls the book a toolbox and says to practice one chapter before the next. Everything above zero adds up.

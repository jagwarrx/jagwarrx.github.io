The Block Method

November 2026. 674 words.

The Block method comes from David Cain, who writes Raptitude. He was diagnosed with ADHD at forty, and it finally explained why the big productivity systems never stuck for him. They’re built by high achievers, so they assume the doing is easy once you’re organized. For people like us, the doing is the hard part. What he wanted instead was “a system with almost no moving parts.”

What a Block is

A Block is 25 minutes, of uninterrupted work, aimed at finishing a task. All three, or it doesn’t count.

Choose a task. Don’t worry about the right one. Any reasonable one will do.

Picture the finish line. Five or ten seconds. The document saved as final, the letters stacked and ready to mail. Without it, it’s easy to slip into “working on” something without working toward anything.

Make the Block. Start the timer and work toward the end, with mild urgency. No quick checks. If I drift into email or a snack, the Block doesn’t count. That sounds harsh, but it keeps every Block worth something.

Tally it. A mark on a sticky note. Each one is a piece of real work, done.

Consider a break. Optional, but at least stand up and say what the next Block is for.

Repeat.

It’s the 25 minutes of the Pomodoro Technique without the rest of its structure, and without the mandatory five-minute break. For people like us, Cain says, that break has two dangers: it runs long, and it kills momentum just when work is finally moving.

Goals are a number of Blocks

The name is literal. Cain pictures each Block as a small wooden box, all the same size, so they stack. A vague, frightening project becomes a count. Work out the steps, and it costs some number of Blocks: seventeen, or seventy-seven. In his words, “if it can be done, it can be done in some number of Blocks.”

That’s a number on the task that isn’t time. And it’s the unit of raw firepower: when the path is clear, the plan is just how many Blocks, and how many a day.

How it climbs the Wall of Awful

The Wall of Awful stands in front of the whole task. A Block never asks for the whole task. It asks for 25 minutes. Most of the climbing steps are built in.

The first step is small. “A Block is always a significant amount of work, and it is almost always achievable.” On a bad day, agree to one Block before calling it a day. One is easy to do, and it usually makes tomorrow easier.

The start is a paper wall. His image: “the beginning of any task is a paper wall that just needs to be punctured, and then the going is easier.” Once the timer runs, most of the resistance is behind me. “Each Block you do makes it easier to start.” That’s the wall getting shorter.

It stops the smash and quit. When I hit a snag in a Block, I’m only minutes from the bell, so storming off makes little sense. Quitting, he notes, means tomorrow I have to start the task again at its worst part. That’s a brick. Staying until the bell takes one away.

It counts the wins. The tally is the “notice the win” step, made physical.

It shrinks the dread. A deadline stops being about how many days are left. It’s about how many Blocks I can make, and I know I can make Blocks.

Two more tools from the book lower the wall before I reach it. A Right Now List is a sticky note with the next two or three physical actions, for when I don’t know how to start: open the file, reread the email, list my reservations. Then I’m already in the middle of it. And when I put a task off until tomorrow, roll out the red carpet: use the time I just saved to make tomorrow’s start as easy as it will ever be. The start gets decided while I’m calm.

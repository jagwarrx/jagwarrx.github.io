The Spine and the Surface

November 2026. 806 words.

I’ve switched to-do apps more times than I can count. Each switch felt like a fresh start, and each one quietly lost things. A promise to call someone, a bill, an idea I’d meant to come back to. None of it was dramatic. It just stopped existing.

For a long time I thought the problem was the apps. Now I think it was me, and specifically the way my attention works.

If you have ADHD, anything not in front of you stops existing. So you have to write everything down. But writing things down is only half of it. Once you have a list, it needs upkeep: filing, re-sorting, building folders. That upkeep is the exact kind of effort you have least of. And your attention follows novelty, not importance, so after a few weeks the list gets boring and you want a new one. Moving to the new one is where things get lost.

So a system like this has two jobs, and they pull against each other. It has to stay stable enough to hold everything, and fresh enough that you keep using it. Most systems try to do both at once and fail at both. They change too often to be trusted, or they stay the same until you stop opening them.

The way out is to notice that the two jobs don’t have to live in the same place. You can split them. Keep a small, permanent spine that never moves, and make everything around it disposable.

The spine is one store. Everything goes in it. It’s sorted only into a few broad areas, like Work, Health, Home and Money, with a flat list under each. No folders inside folders, no projects inside projects. That sounds too simple, and that’s the point. Deciding where something goes in a deep tree is organizing work, and when filing is hard, you stop capturing. A store you stop capturing into is worse than none, because you still trust it.

Each item carries three things: whether it’s started, whether it matters, and when. No tags, no priority scales, no effort estimates. Every extra field is one more thing to keep current, and once fields go stale you stop believing the list.

Then there’s the rule I find hardest: no more than three things started at once. Started means in progress right now, not everything on the list. If three things are open, you finish one or consciously drop one before opening a fourth. Working memory holds only a few live things. A pile of half-started work is how the important ones fall out of view without you noticing.

And the store doesn’t move. Ever. This is the rule everything else depends on, because every other rule assumes the store survives. When the urge for something new hits, and it will, it isn’t telling you the system is broken. It’s telling you that you need novelty. That’s a real need, so meet it somewhere else.

That’s the surface. Change how you work from the list as often as you like. A different view. A paper list for the day. A new café, a new ritual. A walk or a workout before the first task. Novelty is fuel, and there’s no reason to ration it, as long as you don’t spend it on moving the store.

Day to day, I work from two lists. The store holds everything. A daily note holds what gets done today, which is the two or three most important items that are due or already started, in order. I trust that shortlist instead of my memory. That trust is the whole point of the store.

When I start a task, I open a scratch page for it, think on it, and throw it away afterwards. I write down the smallest possible first step, because starting is the hard part for me, and the small win from starting is often enough to carry me into the next step. Big tasks get broken into pieces I can actually begin. And only active work gets notes. Things for someday get a line in the store and nothing more, so the system doesn’t fill up with plans I’ll never touch.

None of this is clever. What surprised me is where the cleverness had to go. I’d spent years looking for the right app, when the problem was that I wanted one thing to be both permanent and new. Once I split those apart, each got easy. The spine can be boring because it doesn’t have to entertain me. The surface can change every day because it doesn’t have to hold anything.

I suspect this works well beyond to-do lists. Whenever something has to be both reliable and fresh, it’s worth asking whether those are really one job, or two jobs that ended up in the same place.

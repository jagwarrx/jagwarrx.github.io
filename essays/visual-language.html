The Unusual Effectiveness of Visual Language for ADHD

November 2026. 526 words.

Bullet journals. Bullets. Checkboxes. Mind maps. Kanban boards and canvases. For an ADHD brain, marks and shapes do organizing work that words do badly. Even short words work better than sentences.

So it’s worth developing a language for organization: a few signs, each with one meaning, used the same way everywhere.

Why marks beat words

A mark is seen. A sentence has to be read. An empty box against a ticked one is clear at a glance, before reading starts. Anne Treisman showed that a single feature, a shape or a colour, pops out of a field at once, while anything that needs combining takes a search. A sentence always needs a search. And every search is a moment to drift.

The page holds the structure, so my head doesn’t have to. Working memory is short with ADHD. Indentation says “this belongs to that” without my having to remember it. Russell Barkley’s rule is to put it outside you, at the point where it’s needed. A visual language is that rule applied to structure.

Status without reading. The bullet journal does this with a handful of signs: a dot for a task, a cross when it’s done, an arrow when it moves to another day, a circle for an event, a dash for a note. A page of them shows what’s open and what’s closed from across the room.

Position carries meaning. In a list, the top is what matters most. In a mind map, a branch is a connection (trees for thinking). On a kanban board, the column is the status. On a canvas, things that sit close belong together. None of it has to be written down.

Short words still count

Words aren’t the enemy. Long ones are. A label of one to three words is a handle: it points at the thing without explaining it. “Essay draft” beats a sentence about the essay, because I look at a list many times and read it fully only once. Every extra word is paid again at every look.

Developing a language

Marks only work if they mean the same thing every time. A box that sometimes means a task and sometimes means a reminder has to be read, not seen. So the language has rules.

Few signs. Five or six, not twenty. A sign I have to look up isn’t saving anything.

One meaning each. And the meaning doesn’t change on a whim.

Written down once. A key, like the bullet journal’s, so the language is a fact and not a mood.

The same everywhere. On a sticky note, in a notebook, in a plain text file. My own is small: [ ] open, [/] started, [x] done, ! for what matters, a date after > for when. It’s the list I own, and it reads the same in any app.

The limit

Decoration isn’t language. A sign with no meaning is noise, and colour on everything means nothing. Making the page beautiful is still organizing, and organizing wins against the real work every time. The test is whether a glance tells me something. If it doesn’t, the mark is decoration.

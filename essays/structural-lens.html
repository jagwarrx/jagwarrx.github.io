The Structural Lens

November 2026. 877 words.

Use the lens when the same bad result keeps coming back. It looks for the cause in the structure, not in the person. It has six levels, and each one sets the next: structures, questions, decisions, execution, distributions, outcomes. Then the outcomes go back and change the structure.

In Rumelt’s kernel (Good Strategy, Bad Strategy) it’s a tool for the diagnosis stage. It answers “what is going on here?” for problems that repeat, then hands off to the policy: it says which part of the structure has to change, not what to change it to.

A structure has three parts

  • Category. A class the structure names. Each category makes a question: the category “task” makes “which task is next?”
  • Constraint. A limit: a budget, a date, an order of steps, a physical law. Each one makes a question too: a budget makes “how do we stay inside it?”
  • Default. What happens when nobody decides. Changing it takes a reason. When a savings plan enrolls everyone by default, most people stay in it.

The rule: you can’t ask about something that has no category, constraint or default. That’s where failures happen, and nothing shows them until they do.

The six levels

  1. Structures. Write down the categories, constraints and defaults, and who owns each. Mark the constraint that limits the result right now.
  2. Questions. Which questions does the structure make possible? Find one it has no word for.
  3. Decisions. Find the decisions you make again every day and turn them into defaults. For each, write the condition it assumes and the sign that the condition has changed.
  4. Execution. Find where information gets lost: a handoff, an incentive, the step from a category to a real case. A rule that nothing enforces isn’t a constraint.
  5. Distributions. What range of results can the action give? Work out the best the constraints allow; if you need more, change the structure or the want. If one result could ruin you, limit your exposure to it.
  6. Outcomes. How fast and how accurate is the feedback, and does it reach whoever owns the structure? Ask: where is the stress we didn’t ask about? The answer changes level one.

When a strategy fails, look from the top down. Don’t start at execution.

Example: a calendar

The categories are event, person and time. The constraint is 24 hours. The default is that empty time is free time. What it can’t express is “which work matters most?”, so meetings fill the day and the deep block gets no place. The fix is a new category and a default: put the deep block on the calendar as a repeating event. The test: deep blocks done per week should go up. Check the log.

Why it works

An object’s substance, internal structure and shape cause its properties. The properties, together with the forces acting on it, cause its behavior. Diamond and graphite are both carbon, arranged differently, so one is hard and one is soft. A steel ball has the same properties on a table and in free fall, and does different things. Systems people make work the same way: the structure is the form, the questions and decisions are the properties, and what you see is the behavior. Four rules follow:

  • Don’t use a property as a cause. “The team is slow” is a property; find what makes it so.
  • Look at the arrangement, not only the parts. The same people arranged differently get different results.
  • Look at the conditions. A structure that works in one can fail in another.
  • Don’t trust the name. A team, a role or a habit can keep its name while its structure changes.

The comparison stops in three places. People can change their structure, which is why the outcomes audit it. A system can have an opponent who adapts. And an object has no purpose: the lens says what causes a result, not whether it’s bad, so always ask bad for whom.

Limits

It’s easy after the fact, since you can always find a category to blame, so predict before you look: “if the structure is the cause, I must see X by this date.” It puts little weight on the person, so ask whether someone else would get the same result in the same structure. It finds the wrong structure, not a better one. And it’s the wrong tool for one-off events, problems of skill, physical facts, questions of value and emergencies, though it’s good afterwards, on the defaults an emergency exposed.

Where it sits in a strategy

  • Diagnosis. Rumelt says a good diagnosis simplifies, explains and points. The lens does all three: it sets the person aside and looks at the structure, it says why the result repeats, and it points at the category, constraint or default to change.
  • The crux. The lens has no rule for which problem to fix first. Rumelt’s crux fills the gap: the most important problem you can actually change.
  • The test. Rumelt asks what would prove a diagnosis wrong. The lens asks for a prediction before you look. It’s the same check.
  • The hand-off. The lens ends at “can I change this structure?” Change it, work in it, or leave it: that choice is the guiding policy, and the lens doesn’t make it.

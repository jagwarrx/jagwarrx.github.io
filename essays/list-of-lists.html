A List of Lists, One Item at a Time

November 2026. 692 words.

The list I keep everything in is a list of lists: a few broad areas, with a flat list under each. That’s how I organize. It isn’t how I look. When it’s time to work, I look at items one at a time.

Those are two shapes for the same data, and holding both is a tradeoff. It’s the one an ADHD mind pushed me into, through two of the constraints I have to design around: out of sight is out of mind, and attention that goes to whatever is new.

Organizing: a list of lists

Organizing needs somewhere to put things. One flat list is a pile: the water bill sits next to the essay draft, and every look at it is a re-sort. A deep tree goes the other way. Areas hold projects, projects hold headings, headings hold tasks.

Two things go wrong in the tree. Filing takes decisions, and every decision at the moment of capture makes it more likely I stop capturing. And a task two clicks deep is the same as a task I never wrote down.

A list of lists is what’s left after the cutting. Two levels. Every item has one place, and nothing is more than one step from the top.

Looking: one at a time

Looking is a different job. For storing, the rule is that anything not in front of me stops existing, so everything gets written down where I can find it. For doing, a full list in front of me is every item asking at once. Each line is a small decision: now, later, or why haven’t I done this yet? Everything on the screen pulls about equally, so the newest thing wins, or the easiest, or nothing does.

What’s on the screen is a constraint, and every constraint creates a question. A full list asks “which of these thirty?” One item asks “this, or next?” I can answer the second one.

It’s also the fastest lever I have. Whatever is in view becomes the obvious next thing, so I put one thing in view.

This isn’t the same as being handed a single task. I still pick from options. I just meet them one at a time, and saying “next” is a choice too.

What the data has to say

One at a time is an interface, and an interface can only show what the data can say. To show me one item, something has to know which one comes first. So the lists are ordered, and the order means something: order is priority.

That’s the only thing the narrow view asks of the store. It’s a line’s position, not a new field.

What I give up

It’s a tradeoff, so it has a cost. Three, really.

The overview while I work. With one item on the screen I can’t see what else is waiting, how much is left, or whether something further down matters more. I have to trust the order. So the order gets set when I organize, not while I do.

Depth. Two levels can’t hold a project inside a project. Anything bigger gets flattened, or becomes a list of its own.

One sitting for everything. Organizing and doing become separate modes. If I start re-sorting in the middle of doing, I’m looking at the whole list again, and the benefit is gone.

It also looks like it breaks the rule that out of sight is out of existence. It would, if hidden items stayed hidden. They don’t. The lists hold everything, I see them whole every time I organize, and each item gets its turn in front of me.

Two jobs, split

This is the spine and the surface again. There, one system was trying to be both permanent and new. Here, one screen was trying to serve both organizing and doing. Organizing wants to see everything. Doing wants to see almost nothing. A screen that tries to do both does neither well, at least for me.

So I split them. Structure where I store, one item where I act. Same data, two shapes: wide for organizing, narrow for doing.

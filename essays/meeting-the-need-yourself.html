Meeting the Need Yourself

November 2026. 1,360 words.

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. The last few essays have been about who sells into that need. Religions, in What Each Religion Sells. Nations, in The Nation as Religion. And in The Market for the Need, the feed, which now sells the old bundle in pieces.

That leaves the obvious question. How much of the need can you meet yourself, without buying it from anyone?

More than you’d think, but not all of it. The goal is a core you make yourself, and dependencies you’ve chosen with your eyes open.

Why bother

There’s nothing wrong with buying. Bread is a product. But every seller has fine print, and the fine print is easier to read when you aren’t desperate. A buyer who has some of the need met already can compare, wait and walk away. A buyer with nothing has to take the first answer that arrives, which is exactly the seizing Arie Kruglanski described in The Why Behind the Need.

So self-sufficiency isn’t about independence for its own sake. It’s about bargaining power with the sellers.

The core you can make

Six ideas, from six very different books, make up most of a core you can build yourself.

The first is the boundary. Epictetus opens his handbook by dividing things into what’s up to us and what isn’t. It sounds simple, and it does a lot of work. Most of what sellers offer is a forecast about the second category: the future, other people, the outcome. Once you’ve drawn the line, you can stop shopping for certainty about things no one can give you certainty about, and spend your attention on the side of the line where it does something.

The second is meaning in the act. The Bhagavad Gītā says you have a right to your action, never to its fruits. That’s usually read as a counsel of detachment, but it’s also a statement about where meaning can come from. If the meaning is in the outcome, you need someone to promise you the outcome. If the meaning is in the doing, you can supply it yourself, every day, whatever happens.

The third is your own experience library. Gary Klein’s research on fire commanders, nurses and pilots found that experts under pressure rarely compare options. They recognize the situation as a kind they’ve seen before, and the recognition brings the right move with it. That library is built from your own past, which means you can build it yourself, but only if you look back. Klein also invented a tool for looking forward that needs no seller: the premortem. Before starting something, imagine it has already failed, and ask why. It’s a forecast you run on yourself, and it’s honest because it assumes nothing goes well.

The fourth is surprise as material. Keith Johnstone’s Impro teaches actors to accept whatever a partner offers and build on it. There are no mistakes, only offers. That’s a way to meet the unknown without knowing it in advance, the answer from Know How You’ll Meet It.

The fifth is adapting over planning. Stanley McChrystal, in Team of Teams, describes how the US command he led in Iraq kept losing to a network that was smaller, poorer and faster, until it stopped trying to plan everything from the top. Its fix had two parts: everyone could see the whole picture, and people closest to the action were allowed to decide. For one person, that translates into seeing your own state clearly, and deciding at the moment of action by purpose, not by a plan drawn up a week earlier.

The sixth is putting it outside. Russell Barkley’s rule for ADHD is that what the brain can’t hold, the environment must: the card, the timer, the list in view. Self-sufficiency doesn’t mean doing it all in your head. It means you built the scaffolding and you own it.

Part by part

The old bundle, from the market essay, had parts. Here’s how far each one can be made at home.

The forecast: mostly. You can’t know what comes next, but a repertoire of responses, a boundary and a premortem cover most of what forecasts were for.

Ritual: yes. Small rituals you set up once, that run without your having to remember, are the subject of Not Tools, but Automation. A ritual doesn’t need a priest. It needs a time and a cue.

Meaning: yes, from the act itself, and from work that lasts longer than the day it was done.

Identity: partly. You can build an identity as a practice rather than a membership: the person who meets what comes, rather than the person who belongs to a group that has the answers. But identity is also something others reflect back to you, and that part can’t be self-made.

Community: no. It can’t be made alone, by definition.

The answer to death: only partly. The Stoics practiced remembering they would die, and found it clarifying rather than morbid. That helps. But the full answer, in every culture, has been shared: funerals, memory, people who carry you on. It’s the part of the bundle most clearly meant to be held by more than one person.

The limit

That last pair, community and death, is the limit, and it isn’t a failure of effort. It’s built into the need.

James Coan’s experiments, from The Levers on How You Respond, found that holding a trusted person’s hand reduces the brain’s response to threat. His interpretation is that the brain treats trusted others as part of its own resources. It spends less when it isn’t alone. If that’s right, then belonging isn’t a separate need that sits beside the need to know what comes next. It’s one of the ways the brain meets it. A person who tries to meet the whole need alone is running on a smaller budget than they have to.

So complete self-sufficiency would be a mistake, even if it were possible. The question isn’t whether to depend on anything. It’s which dependencies you choose, and whether you chose them.

Chosen dependencies

The difference between joining and being sold to is whether you’ve read the fine print.

A chosen dependency is one you picked when you weren’t desperate, whose costs you can name, and that you could leave. A group you joined because it does something you can’t do alone, and whose blind spots you know. A friend you call when things go wrong. A teacher whose limits you’ve noticed.

An unchosen dependency is one you seized when the need was at its peak, whose costs you’ve never looked at, and that would be hard to leave. The test from The Product applies to every one of them: what does it make hard to say?

One or two chosen dependencies are enough. A small core, made at home, and a few connections chosen with your eyes open, can meet more of the need than any bundle.

Two tests

For anything you build to meet the need yourself, two tests tell you whether it’s really yours.

Does it still work if nobody sells me anything? If it depends on a subscription, a guru or a feed, it’s a purchase, not a core.

And does it work on a day I forget it exists? That’s the test from the automation essay, and for an ADHD brain it’s the one that decides everything. A core that depends on remembering will fail on exactly the day it’s needed.

The short version

You can make most of the core yourself. Draw the line between what’s up to you and what isn’t. Put the meaning in the act. Build your own library of situations by looking back, and run your own premortem looking forward. Treat surprise as material. Adapt rather than plan. And put it all outside your head.

What you can’t make is the part that was always meant to be shared. For that, choose your dependencies when you’re calm, read their fine print, and keep them few. Make the core at home, and join the rest with your eyes open.

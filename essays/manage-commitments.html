Manage Commitments, Not Tasks

November 2026. 1,210 words.

Most advice for ADHD starts with the same question: how do I manage my tasks and be more productive? It leads to lists, apps, systems, and, usually, a few weeks later, a new list, a new app and a new system.

I think it’s the wrong question. A better one is: what have I promised, to whom, and can I keep it? That’s not a question about tasks. It’s a question about commitments. And it points at the root causes, not the symptoms.

A commitment is a forecast

All humans, first and foremost, need to know what comes next. Other people are part of what comes next, so they need to predict you. In Projecting, Masking and ADHD I argued that what we project to the world is a forecast of ourselves.

A commitment is the most concrete version of that forecast. “I’ll send it Friday” tells someone exactly what comes next. They plan around it. That’s why a broken commitment hurts more than a missed task. A task is between you and your list. A commitment is between you and a person who was counting on your forecast.

Commitments to yourself count too. “I’ll go to the gym tomorrow” is a forecast you made to the person you’ll be tomorrow.

Not the same as organizing

In the 1980s Terry Winograd and Fernando Flores argued that work isn’t made of tasks at all. It’s made of conversations: someone asks, someone promises, someone delivers, and someone accepts. A promise has two parts that matter: what counts as done, and by when. Everything else, the lists and the files, is support for those conversations.

Seen this way, organizing and keeping commitments are different skills. You can have a perfectly organized list and break promises every week. You can have no list at all and keep every promise you make. In The goal is not a well-organized life I made a similar point about work. Organizing is arranging things. Keeping commitments is a relationship with people over time.

The two questions even fail differently. A task fails as a messy list, and organizing fixes it. A commitment fails as a broken forecast, and only promising differently fixes that.

Why commitments break

If the problem were organization, a better tool would fix it. It usually doesn’t, because the causes are elsewhere. Here are the ones I think matter most.

Yes is cheap now and costly later. Russell Barkley describes ADHD as a kind of nearsightedness to the future. When you make a promise, its cost is far away and blurry. Its benefit is right here. And saying yes brings instant relief: it settles the other person’s uncertainty, and your own fear of disappointing them.

Everyone underestimates. Roger Buehler and colleagues found that people reliably underestimate how long their own projects will take, even when they know their past projects ran late. This is the planning fallacy, and it isn’t specific to ADHD. ADHD just makes the gap wider.

Too many open promises. Each one is a forecast you’re holding up. The more you hold, the more slips, and every slip costs trust.

Vague promises. “I’ll look into it” has no done and no date. It can’t be kept, because there’s nothing to keep. It just hangs there.

No why. In Meaning Makes Pain Bearable I wrote that meaning turns pain into a cost paid for something. A boring commitment with no why is just pain, and pain gets resisted.

Resistance at the start. In Suffering = Pain × Resistance, the discomfort of starting is the pain, and the fight with it is what multiplies it. Many commitments break in the first two minutes.

Masking. When a promise slips, the instinct is to hide it and catch up in secret. The slip grows quietly until it breaks in public.

What to do instead

None of these needs a tool. They’re ways of promising, not ways of organizing. In Free of Tools I argued for keeping the core of a system in your own habits, and these are meant to work that way.

Never say yes on the spot. “Let me check and tell you by tomorrow.” The pause is the whole fix for nearsightedness. It gives the future cost a chance to come into focus before you commit to it.

Make it small and dated. One deliverable, one date, one person. “A draft by Friday,” not “I’ll handle it.” Peter Gollwitzer’s research on implementation intentions found that people who decide exactly when and where they’ll act are much more likely to follow through. A specific promise is easier to keep, and easier to tell whether you’ve kept.

Hold only what fits in your head. If you can’t name every open promise from memory, you’ve probably made too many. The limit isn’t a number. It’s whether you can see them all at once.

Give each one a line of why. If you can’t say what a commitment is for, don’t make it, or let it go. If you can, say it to yourself before you start. It’s the cheapest way to make the starting pain bearable.

Renegotiate early. This is the most important one. A forecast changed before the deadline is a revision. The same change after the deadline is a betrayal. “I’ll be two days late” said on Wednesday costs almost nothing. Silence on Friday costs trust. Early renegotiation is also the opposite of masking: it shows the slip while it’s small.

Make promises to people, not lists. A person waiting is structure that needs no app. Barkley’s advice is to put help at the point of performance, and another person is the oldest version of that: a fixed time, someone who will notice.

Close every promise. Each commitment should end one of three ways: done, renegotiated, or released out loud. “I’m not going to get to this, sorry” is a kept commitment of a kind. What breaks trust is the promise that just quietly dies.

Start by making contact. Two minutes with the thing, letting the discomfort be there without fighting it. You don’t have to want to start. You only have to stop resisting the not wanting.

The fine print

This doesn’t make ADHD go away. Some weeks you’ll still break promises, and medication, sleep and the environment matter as much as anything here. Saying no more often has its own costs: some people and jobs reward the person who says yes. And renegotiating early takes courage, especially if you’ve learned that every slip is a moral failure. The point isn’t a perfect record. It’s fewer promises, kept more often, and honesty when they aren’t.

The short version

Change the question from how to be productive to what you’ve promised, to whom, and whether you can keep it. A commitment is a forecast someone is counting on. That’s a different problem from organizing, and it has different causes: a cheap yes, underestimation, too many promises, vague ones, no why, resistance at the start, and hiding the slip.

So pause before yes. Make each promise small, dated and for something. Hold only what fits in your head. Make them to people. Close each one, and when one slips, say so early. Reliability built on honesty is cheaper than reliability built on a mask.
